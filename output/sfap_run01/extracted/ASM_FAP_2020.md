## Background

The island of American Samoa is the only United States territory in the southern hemisphere and is located in the Pacific Ocean, approximately 2,500 miles southwest of the island of Hawai'i and approximately 1,600 miles northeast of New Zealand. American Samoa consists of seven islands: five inhabited high volcanic islands (Tutuila, Aunu'u, Ofu, Olosega, and Ta'u), one atoll (Swains), and one uninhabited atoll (Rose). Rose Atoll is a wildlife refuge managed by the US Fish and Wildlife Service, so it is not included in this assessment and resource strategy. The Swains Island is privately owned and well protected by its current landowners, so it is also not considered in this assessment and strategy due to its remote location and difficult accessibility. The total land area of American Samoa is 76 square miles, with a population of approximately 60,300. The majority of the population is on the south side of the largest island, Tutuila. The climate is hot, wet, and humid, with an annual rainfall of about 125 inches.
The topography is rugged and steep with about 42% of the land having slopes of 45% and higher. A total of 39,156 acres are forested in American Samoa

## American Samoa's Forests

Due to American Samoa's warm climate and year-round rainfall, the islands natural vegetation is comprised of tropical rainforests. Unlike the temperate and coniferous forests, which are typically dominated by a few species, tropical rainforests are dominated by a combination of numerous species. The dominant forest type is Rhus secondary forest, which is dominated by Tavai (Rhus taitensis), Maota (Dysoxylum maota), and Moso'oi (Canaga odorata). The second dominant forest type is the secondary scrub, which is typically dominated by Laupata (Macaranga harveyana), Fau (Hibiscus tiliaceus), and Soga (Pipturus argenteus). In American Samoa, native forests extend from the seashore up to the highest mountain peaks. Topographical variation, climate change, along with human and natural disturbances, has influenced the growth and distribution of various rainforest types across American Samoa's landscapes.
Throughout American Samoa's undisturbed coastal areas occurs a narrow strip of vegetation referred to as littoral forest. The littoral forest is comprised of various coastal species whose presence and distribution are influenced by the surrounding sea. Littoral forests provide habitat for a number of wildlife species including flying foxes, seabirds, and nesting sea turtles.
On the south side of American Samoa's largest island, Tutuila, lies the Tafuna Plain.
Within the Tafuna Plain occurs one of American Samoa's most threatened forest types, referred to as lowland lavaflow rainforest. The lavaflow rainforest sits directly above important aquifers from which the community receives the majority of its drinking water.
Tree species in this forest type are adapted to rocky lavaflow areas with little soil and low water-holding capacity. The lavaflow rainforest is also one of American Samoa's tallest forests highlighted by its enormous giant banyan (Ficus spp.) and Tava (Pometia pinnata) trees that tower over the plains. This forest is a major habitat for the Samoan Fruit bat (Pteropus samoensis) and the Fruit bat (Pteropus tonganus). In the past, the ASCC-ACNR Forestry Program received funding and support from the US Forest Service -Forest Legacy Program to acquire the lavaflow rainforest as a fully protected property.
However, complications with the official owner and title to the land have prevented the programs from acquiring the rainforest. The ASCC-ACNR Forestry Program, along with the DMWR, are interested in purchasing the property and will collaborate more in doing so (see Forest Legacy Program Assessment of Need, page 29).
Another threatened forest type in American Samoa is the mangrove forest. Out of the seven islands that comprise American Samoa, mangroves occur on only two, Tutuila and Aunu'u. Mangrove forests occur on these islands' coastal areas, particularly in sheltered lagoons and protected areas where freshwater enters the ocean at the mouths of streams. The largest mangrove forests are located on the south side of Tutuila Island in the lagoons of Leone and Nu'uuli villages. Mangroves serve as prime habitat for numerous fish, crustacean, and mollusk species. For thousands of years Samoans have depended on the resources forests provide for sustaining life and livelihood. Resources such as medicine, food, clothing, canoes, fishing gear, and traditional building materials exemplify the integral role forests play in traditional Samoan daily life. The Ifilele tree (Instia bijuga) is mainly used for handicrafts such as woodcarving, tool making, Samoan war weapons, and more. Most native plants are used for traditional medicines to treat various illnesses such as stomach aches and fevers. Many local healers (Taulasea Samoa) practice Samoan ethnobotany through water infusions made of fresh leaves or bark. Fresh leaves are often used externally in combination with healing oils for massage treatments as well. Trees and forests play an important cultural role, appearing frequently in Samoan legends, traditional ceremonies, and legends. For generations, Samoans have spoken of the forests in their proverbial sayings and chiefly language to symbolize important events and gatherings. In the lowland lava flow rainforest lies the Tia Seu Lupe star mound, which was an ancient pigeon-hunting snare that was used as a cultural activity.
Although no timber industry exists in American Samoa, forests provide other resources of economic value. The tropical forest is a place of rich species diversity inhabited by a number of unique and rare native plants and animals. Such features generate income by attracting eco-tourists who come to appreciate the forests' natural beauty. Forests are also economically valued by fishermen. The mangroves are natural fish nurseries that replenish fish stocks that fishermen depend on for income. ## Population Variability

American Samoa is comprised of five volcanic islands and two remote atolls-one uninhabited and the other home to only a few individuals. Of the five high islands, 96%
of the population resides on the main island of Tutuila. ## American Samoa Wildlife Conservation Strategy

The Comprehensive Strategy for Wildlife Conservation in American Samoa 2 nd Edition (2016) was produced by the American Samoa Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources (DMWR) as a guide to resources in need of conservation due to decline in abundance, highly localized or restricted distributions, and vulnerability to threats. Its description of wildlife resources and plans for their conservation reveal many areas of shared interest with the forestry programs and opportunities for continued collaboration.
The various native fauna species, both terrestrial and aquatic, are listed in this plan along with their background information, the threats affecting them, and the conservation priorities set out to conserve and protect them. 

## Invasive Species Strategy and Action Plan

The American Samoa Invasive Species Strategy and Action Plan was developed by DMWR in 2017. This document identifies priority invasive species and provides a framework for the different strategies and management actions recommended to address such species. Some species highlighted include current invasive species in American Samoa such as the Coral bean tree (Adenanthera pavonina) and potential invasive species that may enter the island such as the Brown tree snake (Boiga irregularis). Regulated and strengthened quarantine measures are critical in preventing the spread of invasive species to and from American Samoa and the whole Pacific. Pathways that are of major concern includes a single passenger/cargo boat (Lady Naomi) that travels between the State of Samoa and American Samoa, cargo vessels carrying containers for consumer goods from Hawai'i and California, and more. The strategic action plan lists 3 goals to address this issue in American Samoa: implement urgent and effective actions, enhance capacities for informed decisions, and advance prevention and rapid-response protocols and measures. This strategic action plan serves as a guide for agency collaboration, invasive species projects, and prevention/control efforts.

## CRITICAL ISSUES AND PRIORITY LANDSCAPE AREAS

## Sustainability of Urban Forests

American Samoa's urban forests are comprised of native and non-native species planted and managed around residential, church, and commercial property and public areas including sports fields, parks, and government lands. American Samoa contains more than 3,106 acres of urban forests with the majority concentrated in and around villages on the southwest lowland plains of the main island Tutuila. Park visitors, schools, and businesses in these urban areas rely on trees for shade and beautification. Residents rely on urban forests for food, protection from strong winds, and medicine. Urban forests face many challenges as the population continues to slowly increase and landscapes drastically change. The focus must be to conserve and sustain the urban forest while at the same time enhancing public benefits derived from this precious resource.
Pu'a (Hernandia nymphaeifolia) tree used for shade and recreational purposes in an urban area. Photo: Simon Stowers.

## Threats to Urban Forests

Because of the aesthetic value of the urban forest and the importance of individual trees in the urban landscape, damage or tree death from pests and diseases is perceived to be more important here than in other local forest settings. Exotic plants imported for the urban landscape can become invasive threats themselves as well as serving as pathways for the introduction of exotic plant-feeding arthropods and plant pathogens.
Education and awareness on these pests and diseases and tree damages from human and/or natural disturbances is critically needed. 

## Priority Landscapes for Urban Forest Sustainability

The priority landscapes for sustainability of urban forests include villages, commercial and privately-owned residential zones, government office grounds, schools, parks, sports fields, and churches. Because American Samoa's urban forests cover more than 3,106 acres, and all are critically important, all urban forested landscapes were designated as high priority. The largest of these areas are in the Tafuna and Leone Plains and the Pago Pago-Fagatogo area. Smaller areas occur in and around all the villages-mostly located along the coastlines.

## Protection of Coral Reefs

American Samoa contains over 900 fish species and over 200 coral species that sustain unique and diverse near-shore marine communities. These communities include a diversity of marine life including fish, turtles, seabirds, and countless invertebrates. Coral reefs may also become stressed and susceptible to diseases when they are exposed to high levels of sediment and nutrients. In American Samoa, increased amounts of sediment and nutrients may be deposited onto coral reefs as a result of forests being cleared for development and agriculture, especially on steep slopes and along stream corridors. As forests are cleared, the exposed soil becomes vulnerable to erosion and run-off, eventually ending up in streams which flow onto coral reefs. An increase in impervious surfaces in developed built-up areas also increases runoff. In addition, improper agricultural practices such as misuse of fertilizers and poor farming techniques have contributed to coral reefs being exposed to high levels of nutrients. These effects are intensified by American Samoa's topography and climate, characterized by steep slopes and year-round, often heavy, rainfall.

## Priority Landscapes for Coral Reef Protection

High priority landscapes identified include areas of rapid development and population growth in the Tafuna Plain, Pago Pago Harbor area, Nu'uuli, Vailoatai, Taputimu, Malaeloa, Faga'alu, the Manu'a Islands, and more. ## Conservation of Wetlands

American Samoa's wetlands-comprised of mangroves, marshes and swamps-provide residents with numerous cultural, ecological, and economic benefits. Wetlands are important for growing traditional food crops, mitigating flood waters, improving water quality, providing income, recharging ground water, and providing food and habitat for important native plants and animals. Culturally, the connection between wetlands and the animals that inhabit them have been symbolized in traditional Samoan proverbs and songs that have been passed down through the generations. Wetlands also serve as important agricultural areas for the cultivation of traditional taro crops-an agricultural system known to be one of the most productive in the world. In addition, wetlands provide food and habitat for numerous fish, mollusks, crustaceans, birds, and plants, including rare native species. Conservation and enhancement of wetlands is crucial to preserving Samoan culture, as well as improving the health of American Samoa's residents and environment. With only approximately less than 400 acres of wetlands left, immediate protective management plans must be implemented if future generations are to continue to enjoy the many benefits this valuable resource provides.

## Threats to Wetlands

Like coral reefs, American Samoa's wetlands are threatened by excessive sedimentation and nutrient enrichment. Wetlands become degraded when sediment and nutrient levels exceed the wetlands' natural ability to absorb pollutants. Activities such as improper farming practices and clearing of forests on slopes and along stream corridors have exposed wetlands to higher amounts of sediment and nutrients than they normally would receive. American Samoa's wetlands have been destroyed and degraded by activities such as clearing vegetation and filling. On the main Island of Tutuila, the rapid rise in population has resulted in the majority of suitable flat lands being converted for agriculture and development. With limited flat land remaining, residents clear and fill in wetlands to extend the land for building homes, roads, and commercial buildings. It has been estimated that more than 48% of American Samoa's original wetlands have already been degraded or eliminated. As a result, villages on Tutuila are beginning to experience regular flooding after heavy rains.

## Priority Landscapes for Wetland Conservation

Because wetlands are affected most directly by activities on their associated catchment areas, only wetland areas and their associated catchments were included in the landscape priority assessment. Watersheds containing wetlands include Leone, Nu'uuli, Vatia, Masefau, Fagaitua, Aoa, Tula, Alao, Aunu'u, Ofu, Olosega, Tufu, and Ta'u. Wetlands themselves were designated as high priority landscapes. Landscapes identified as high priority in the overlay analysis totaled 20,200 acres and include the wetlands that totaled up to 444 acres. Low priority area totaled up to 27,024 acres.

## Maintaining Fresh Water Quality

American Samoa's fresh water supply comes from both surface water and ground water.
The surface water consists of perennial and intermittent streams fed from surface runoff and ground water discharge, while the ground water is comprised of aquifers recharged by percolation from areas at the surface. Streams are important for recreation, bathing, fishing, cooking, recharging aquifers, and sometimes drinking (when boiled). During public water shutdowns, especially during and after strong storms, streams are heavily relied upon in providing freshwater for daily use. Streams and riparian zones also provide food and habitat for many of American Samoa's native fish and invertebrates.
Ground water provides the main source of drinking water in the territory. It is extracted from underground aquifers by the American Samoa Power Authority's submersible and vertical shaft water pumps and treated with liquid chlorine to kill microorganisms.
Approximately 95% of American Samoa's drinking water comes from aquifers below the Malaeimi Valley and Tafuna Plain which are recharged from the Nu'uuli and Tafuna Plain watersheds. American Samoa's fresh water quality faces many challenges as the population continues to increase. It is imperative that management solutions are implemented to maintain, enhance, and protect American Samoa's fresh water to the level of quality that current and future generations expect and enjoy.

## Threats to Fresh Water Quality

Threats to American Samoa's stream waters include sedimentation and nutrient enrichment. Forests along stream corridors and on steep slopes help moderate the flow of water into streams. As forests in these areas are cleared for agriculture and development, the water flow into streams increases, washing large amounts of exposed unstable soil-including agricultural fertilizers and pollutants-into streams. High levels of sediment and nutrients can be harmful to users of stream water, including fish and other wildlife that inhabit streams. These effects are intensified by American Samoa's rugged topography and high frequency and intensity of rainfall year-round. According to the AS-EPA FY18 Watershed Management and Protection Annual Report, water quality in streams resulted in poor quality due to anthropogenic inputs.
One of the most common issues relating to water quality is pollution and littering.
Different types of debris such as heavy metals, plastics, aluminum cans, and many more are found within these streams. The relationship between the amount of dissolved inorganic nitrogen found in streams and watershed areas with high human population are clearly evident from water quality reports, surveys, and visual assessments.
Heavy metals and other debris found in a stream. Photo: Daniel Helsham.
The majority of American Samoa's drinking water supply is retrieved from aquifers below the Tafuna Plain. The Tafuna Plain is an area of high population, urban development, and agriculture. The aquifers are made up of highly permeable volcanic soil and bedrock, which provide poor filtration of impurities. These factors increase the likelihood of drinking water becoming contaminated with pollutants released from piggeries, agricultural fertilizers and pesticides, and septic tanks. Studies have shown that the water levels, bacteria concentrations, and turbidity of groundwater in the Tafuna Plain increase during heavy rain events.

## Priority Landscapes for Maintaining Fresh Water Quality

High priority watersheds identified from the analysis include Leone, Aasu, Tafuna Plain, Nu'uuli, Pago Pago Harbor, Lauli'i-Aumi, Fagaitua, Amouli, Alao, Tula, and portions of Ofu, Olosega, Ta'u, Letula-Lepula, and many more. Within these watersheds, landscapes that pose the greatest threat of sediment and nutrient deposition into freshwater systems were delineated as high priority areas. These areas include landscapes with high population growth, rapid development, steep slopes, stream corridors, and agriculture.
Native forests were deemed important because of their role in absorbing excess nutrients and sediment, recharging aquifers, stabilizing soil, and moderating water flow.

## Conservation of Native Flora and Fauna

Among American Samoa's true measina, or treasures, are its forests and the unique plants and animals that inhabit them. The native flora and fauna that comprise these forests have always been an integral part of Samoan traditions and culture. The Talafalu understory plant (Micromelum minutum) is used for treating headaches and stomach gas, and also serves as the primary habitat for the Papilio godeffroyi. The variety of resources they provide-including medicine, food, traditional building materials, natural beauty, and recreation-have benefited Samoans for generations. Fortunately, American Samoa still possesses some of the most pristine and intact forests in the South Pacific. Scientists are attracted to American Samoa to study its unique flora and fauna, some of which are found nowhere else in the world. The endemic Samoan swallowtail butterfly (Papilio godeffroyi), a species once found on all the larger Samoan islands, now appears restricted to American Samoa. Numerous land snail species are endemic to the archipelago or to one or more islands of American Samoa. Several native species have been added to the Endangered Species Act (ESA) list such as the Pe'ape'a Vai or the Pacific sheath-tailed bat (Emballonura semicaudata rotensis) and Mao (Gymnomyza samoensis). As scientists continue to study American Samoa's flora and fauna, more new discoveries are being made. American Samoa's rugged and steep landscapes may have protected its measina for generations. However, some species have not been so fortunate. Many challenges face American Samoa's native flora and fauna as the population increases and landscapes are transformed.

## Threats to the Native Flora and Fauna

Much of the north side of Tutuila Island is still covered in native forest; however, invasive species such as Albizia (Falcataria mollucana), Red-bead tree (Adenanthera pavonina),
African tulip (Spathodea campanulata), Strawberry guava (Psidium cattleianum),
Cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum), and the Mexican rubber tree (Castilla elastica) are beginning to invade and pose a serious threat to native vegetation in these areas. Albizia is one of American Samoa's worst invasives due to its rapid dispersal, quick growth, and ability to outgrow and shade out native species. Evidence of its invasiveness can be seen on ridges and slopes in the central part of Tutuila, and already pockets are beginning to spring up on the western side of the island.
Unlike the native forests on the north side of Tutuila, most of the native forests in the south are greatly diminished due to clearing for agriculture and development. At one time, the lowland lavaflow rainforest covered most of the Tafuna Plain; today less than 28 acres of this forest remain in all of American Samoa. This drastic decrease is mainly due to its presence on flat land which is of high value for development. Many native animals depend on tree species within this forest type for food and shelter. The giant banyan trees in this forest produce fruit eaten by Pacific pigeon, Samoan starling, purple-capped fruit doves, and many-colored fruit doves. The many-colored fruit dove, in particular, is one of American Samoa's rarest birds and depends almost entirely on the banyan trees for food. Banyan trees also provide primary roosts for flying foxes. A new invasive species that was first detected in 2018 in Tutuila is the Little Fire Ant (Wasmannia auropunctata).
This fire ant specie is a major threat to other infested countries and forests. It is capable of reaching very high densities, harms plants indirectly through mutualistic relationships with phloem-feeding insects and can severely harm nearby residents causing severe rashes. Since 2018, invasive species research and control efforts have been mainly
focusing on the Little Fire Ant.
The Manu'a Islands also contain a significant amount of intact forest. With population and development decreasing in Manu'a, however, these forests are currently less threatened than those on Tutuila. Nevertheless, hurricanes and invasive species still pose a threat on these islands as they do on Tutuila. Rising sea level and storms have caused major coastal erosions for several areas in Olosega. With frequent cyclones and tropical storms, there is a high possibility that more invasive species will inhabit the Manu'a Islands. If current and future residents are to continue to enjoy the beauty and benefits of American Samoa's native flora and fauna, it is crucial that steps be taken to preserve them.

## CONCLUSIONS AND DATA GAPS FROM THE CRITICAL ISSUES ANALYSIS

While there seems to be broad consensus among stakeholders about the critical issues for American Samoa's forests, a paucity of spatial and non-spatial data makes it difficult to draw firm conclusions about the status of the resources and threats and to map priority landscapes for each issue. The resulting priority landscape maps will be useful Future iterations will undoubtedly benefit from the availability of better data, including time series, and better methods for putting that data together to provide an accurate assessment of forestry needs.
Despite these difficulties, the analysis did bring out some important points for program managers and evaluators. The first points apply to issues 2, 3, and 4: Protection of Coral
Reefs, Conservation of Wetlands, and Maintaining Fresh Water Quality. All three of these issues may be best addressed on a watershed basis. The resources and threats are linked to each other primarily within watersheds and the effects of programs implemented in a watershed to protect these resources and mitigate threats will be manifested within those watersheds. There are two other reasons why planning programs around watersheds makes sense. The first is that the boundaries between traditional Samoan communities, the villages, often coincide with watershed boundaries. The second is that other local agencies are increasingly focusing their program planning and evaluation efforts at the watershed level. By addressing these three issues at the level of the watershed, the forestry programs will improve their ability to implement programs at the community level, increase opportunities for synergy with partner agencies, and improve both the likelihood of achieving significant real impacts on these issues and the likelihood of being able to measure those impacts.

The forests of American Samoa can be divided into several different types, which are included in the list of vegetation types. The most basic difference between these forests is elevation. The lowest elevation forests are littoral forest and mangrove swamp, which are found on the shore, and the highest elevation forest is montane forest. Between these communities is lowland forest, which prior to the arrival of the first Samoans was the prevalent plant community in American Samoa. Two other types of vegetation should also be mentioned here, montane scrub and summit scrub. These two consist of scattered scrubby trees mostly less than 13 ft in height and often surrounded by a dense matrix of herbaceous plants, particularly ferns and vines. The last forest type described below is secondary forest.

## Littoral Forest

This forest type is found directly on the seashore and is dominated by trees directly or indirectly dispersed by the sea. It occurs on nearly all undisturbed shores of the main islands, but its total area is small, because it rarely occupies a zone more than 100-300 ft along coasts and in bays where coral rubble is being deposited. Most of the littoral forest has been removed from the south coast of Tutuila, while the best remaining examples are found on the north coast-protected in the central part by the National Park of American Samoa, but unprotected on the northwest and northeast.

## Mangrove Swamp

Mangrove swamp is a unique forest community dominated by trees able to grow in the saline conditions on mudflats, along estuaries, and in sheltered lagoons where sedimentation is occurring. Three mangrove species are found in American Sāmoa, all at their easternmost limit in the Pacific. Two types of mangrove forest are recognized in the territory-mangrove scrub dominated by the somewhat scrubby Rhizophora mangle (red mangrove), and mangrove forest dominated by the large tree Bruguiera gymnorrhiza (Oriental mangrove). Many species of marine animals are dependent upon mangrove forest, including edible fish and invertebrates, as well as food items for these edible species, making this community extremely important to inshore fisheries. Mangroves are also important for protecting the shore from wave and high wind damage, and as a site for the deposition of erosional material that would otherwise damage the surrounding reefs.
The extent of mangrove forests today is much less than it was in the past, due primarily to the activities of man. Although their waterlogged, saline soils are not suitable for agriculture, mangrove trees make excellent firewood and have long been cut for that purpose, but with an increasing population, regrowth may not be able to keep pace with cutting. In American Samoa, however, the major cause of mangrove loss is probably filling of the habitat for house sites. elevation.
The two environmental factors most affecting the montane forest, and which distinguish it from lowland forest, are the prevailing cooler temperatures and higher rainfall. There is no real dry season since no month has less than 16 in (40 cm) of rainfall, and humidity is always high. Clouds form daily and the ground and vegetation are usually wet. These factors contribute to the profusion of terrestrial and epiphytic herbaceous species, particularly mosses, ferns, and orchids.
Montane forest is shorter in stature than lowland forest, probably averaging between 50 to 60 ft (15 to 18 m) in height.

## Montane Scrub

Montane scrub comprises the vegetation on the summits and upper slopes and ridges of trachyte formations on the island of Tutuila. These areas of trachyte (a soft, fine-grained, generally light- species are apparently restricted in Samoa to these trachyte formations on Tutuila, including the small tree Melicope richii and the screwpine Pandanus reineckei.

## Summit Scrub

This unique scrubby vegetation covers the very wet summit area of Ta'u from about 2000 to 3170 feet (600 to 930 m) elevation. Cooling, moisture-laden southeast winds ascend the steep cliffs on the south side of the island to produce prodigious amounts of precipitation, resulting in the upper portion of the island on most days being cloaked in clouds during the daytime. Instead of montane or cloud forest that would be expected at this elevation, the vegetation comprises scattered scrubby, epiphyte-covered trees growing in a dense matrix of ferns, shrubs, and sprawling vines. Epiphytes, especially mosses, are abundant, since the heavy rainfall, high humidity, and cool weather are ideal for their growth. No other vegetation type quite like this has been reported from elsewhere in the region. The area is virtually untouched by man, because it is too remote, too wet, and too cool to be used by villagers. It is found mostly within the National Park and, consequently, is not recommended here for inclusion in the Forest Legacy Program.

## Secondary Forest

Secondary forest comprises forest typically dominated by fast-growing trees with small, easily dispersed seeds that require relatively sunny conditions for germination and/or establishment.
It covers large areas of American Samoa, particularly in the lowlands below about 1650 feet (500 m) elevation, especially on Tutuila. Although superficially similar in structure to the climax forest types discussed above, its population structure and flora are quite different. Secondary forest trees dominate the canopy, but other species-particularly ones that can germinate and become established in shady conditions-typically dominate the smaller size classes. Without further disturbance, the sunny conditions required for germination and establishment of the secondary forest species will no longer be present, and the slower-growing canopy tree species of primary forest that dominate the smaller size classes will eventually prevail when the larger secondary forest trees of the canopy age and die. After a long period, the climax forest that develops will be virtually indistinguishable from primary forests in the area. Several different types of secondary forest may be recognized in American Samoa, the most common being one dominated by Rhus taitensis (tavai), sometimes together with Alphitonia zizyphoides (toi). Rhus secondary forest exhibits a characteristically stratified canopy easily recognizable in aerial photographs and is particularly common on the lowland hills and ridges of Tutuila. It apparently becomes established in areas of former cultivation or where severe disturbance (e.g., a hurricane) has occurred. Another type of secondary forest occurs on talus slopes where landslips and falling rocks lead to continual disturbance. An example of this can be found on the cliffs that line the eastern and western coasts of Ta'u, and on the steep slopes or cliffs on the northern side of Pago Pago Harbor.

## Aesthetic and Scenic Values

The forests of American Samoa have a great deal of aesthetic and scenic value, alone or as part of the vegetation that covers the rugged islands. Most of the north coast of Tutuila is uninhabited and numerous forest-covered ridges extend from the central spine of the island northward to the sea. Other attractive areas of forest include Fagatele Bay, Mt. (Three of these are already afforded some federal protection: Fagatele Bay is a Federally protected National Marine Sanctuary; the central part of the north coast of Tutuila is part of the National Park of American Samoa; and the scenic south coast of Ta'u with its spectacular Laufuti canyon is also part of the National Park. However, none of the rest of the Territory, except for the south coast of Ofu [National Park] and wetlands, has any federal protection.) The Naumati Forest (Ottoville, Tafuna, AS) is all that remains of a majestic lowland forest that once covered the Tafuna plain. Its flat terrain is very scenic to traverse and is easily accessible since it is surrounded by suburbs.

## Wildlife Habitat

The islands of American Samoa differ from most other oceanic islands in the Pacific (i.e., those unrelated to the land masses of Asia and Australia) in that they are relatively pristine, with 40% uninhabited parts of the Territory, especially on Ta'u and on the north coast of Tutuila. This is in stark contrast to places, such as Hawai'i, where much of the lowland native forests have been destroyed. One of the most important uses for the forests of American Samoa is as a habitat for flora and fauna. Nearly 500 native species of vascular plants, many of them endemic to the archipelago, are found in the Territory, and most of these occur in the forests. Virtually all the native land birds and reptiles use the forests for their survival. Consequently, it is important to preserve as much of American Samoa's remaining native forest as possible, so that the native species can be preserved in a viable ecosystem, especially for threatened and endangered species.

## Tourism and Recreation

American Samoa has always had high potential for tourism but has yet to develop much of its potential. The islands offer breathtaking scenery, great snorkeling/diving, an abundance of hiking, and the people are friendly, but there has been very little development of a local tourism industry. American Samoa is bypassed for the other areas in the region, such as Fiji and independent Samoa. ## Forest Products and Timber Management

There is currently no timber industry in American Samoa, nor are there any significant tree plantations. A major timber industry flourished in independent Samoa between the 1960s and the 1980s, slowing considerably now that the majority of the lowland forests have been systematically cut down. In a report on the forests of American Samoa, 
Nelson (1964)
 recommended that a small portable mill might be useful, but this recommendation was apparently never implemented. The amount of timber available is low since most of it is on rugged, inaccessible ridges on the northern half of Tutuila. The territory is too small, too rugged, and the accessible forests too few to have a successful timber industry. The main use for local timber was traditionally for house posts and canoes, but now, with western-style houses and imported boats being the norm, the only currently significant timber tree is probably the introduced Flueggea flexuosa (poumuli), which is commonly planted in villages and is used for posts of simple houses and sheds. One of the most important functions of American Samoa's forests is the service they provide as watersheds. The Territory consists of very mountainous islands (particularly Tutuila) with steep slopes and sharp ridges. Most of the rugged interior is covered with native rainforest that protects the fragile mountain soil from erosion caused by heavy rainfall. Intact rainforest also retains rainwater in the soil, from where it slowly percolates through the ground or into the streams during the dry season, providing for a more even stream flow during the year. When forest vegetation is removed, the land is exposed to more rapid erosion processes, and without the intact forest soil, the resulting rapid run off can lead to serious flooding and sedimentation problems. The rapid run-off causes floods during heavy rains, and the loss of soil causes droughts during the dry season, as insufficient water flows out of the interior of the island. Consequently, the watershed value of intact forest on the mountainous parts of the island is very high.
Also important is the role the forest plays in the supply of potable water. With rapid run off after rainfall, the amount of water retained in soil to ultimately recharge groundwater and aquifers is diminished. The amount of potable water available for use has decreased over the years because of the loss of forests. The maintenance of forests in the mountainous interior of Tutuila (on which the vast majority of the population resides) is critical to the effort to find enough useable water for the population. Therefore, it is crucial that the remaining native forests, which are restricted mostly to the mountainous interior, are protected so as not jeopardize the available water supply.

## Cultural Resources

## Plant Cultural Resources

In his report on the uses of forest products of American Samoa, 
Nelson (1964)
 noted that in addition to the use of trees for house posts and canoes, the inhabitants use the forest resources for "bowls and other implements; fuelwood; fruits and nuts; bark and other fiber materials; tree sap for glue; etc. The Samoans continue to depend on the native forest for many of their needs although imported substitutes play an increasing role." The last statement is prophetic, as American Samoans today make very little use of their forests for timber or non-timber forest products. There is little carving presently being done; fruits and nuts are rarely harvested from the forest; bark and other fiber materials are scarcely needed any more; and tree sap is no longer used for glue. Most of the non-timber products used today are obtained from the village, its plantations, or agroforestry land rather than native forest, partially because of the difficulty of accessing the remaining native forest.
Virtually the only non-timber product that Nelson mentioned that is still being used is fuelwood.
It is a continuing tradition that umu (earth ovens) are used on Sunday mornings for fixing the to'ona'i (the main meal of the day), which is served after the morning church service. The umu is made by interspersing rocks with firewood; the burning wood heats the rocks to red hot, the food is put on them, and the whole thing is covered with mats and large leaves to steam for a few hours. Firewood is obtained mostly from dead trees on plantations and in villages. This use is commonplace today, and although electric ovens are now often used instead, there is a continuing influx of immigrants from independent Samoa, who are likely to be more traditional (and poorer) and more likely to require firewood well into the future.
Additional uses of the forests not mentioned by Nelson include utilization of plants for medicine and fragrances. American Samoa has a vibrant system of local healers who use medicinal plants to treat their patients, particularly children. A total of 84 medicinal plants were cited by 
Whistler (1996)
 as being commonly used in the practice of traditional medicine in Samoa. While most of these plants are found in villages or plantations, about a third could be considered to occur predominantly or exclusively in forests. The need for these medicinal plants is expected to remain fairly constant, possibly with a decreasing reliance on them being balanced by an increasing population. The most popular fragrance plant in American Samoa is Alyxia bracteolosa (lau maile), whose bark-peeled with the leaves attached-is made into fragrant leis. These leis are not as common as they once were, but sometimes are seen during school graduations and traditional dances. The fragrant flowers of Aglaia samoensis (laga'ali) are sometimes also used for fragrance and for scenting coconut oil.

## Archaeological Sites

Archaeological sites can be found throughout the islands of American Samoa. These sites are studied and preserved to help us understand past human societies and how they changed over time. The most significant sites are those studied by the National Park of American Samoa within park boundaries. However, there still remain many historical remains that have yet to be studied and preserved. Within a to-be-proposed Forestry Legacy project -the Naumati Forest, is located a Ti'a Seu Lupe star mound which was an ancient pigeon-hunting snare that was used for a cultural activity. Aside from the large Ti'a Seu Lupe located right at the edge of the Naumati Forest along with an informational plaque, there are many other star mounds located within the forest.
Another forested area in the village of A'oloau called Manono, contains historical burial sites and more Ti'a Seu Lupe. Unfortunately, Manono is a potential site for a wind-turbine farm which means that the historical, cultural, and ecological importance of this forested area is at risk.
Protecting and maintaining forested areas that contain archaeological sites is important in order to further understand and preserve the history of American Samoa.

## Outstanding Geological Features

Samoa is volcanic "oceanic" in origin, i.e., the archipelago was formed from basalt rising from a "hot spot" on the ocean floor of the Pacific plate beyond the continental islands to the west. The archipelago was formed in isolation and has never had a connection to any other land area. The islands originated in the Pliocene Epoch over two million years ago, and were formed generally in an easterly direction, with the oldest one on the western end of the chain (Savai'i) and youngest on the eastern end (Ta'u). (Rose Atoll is not geologically a part of the archipelago, nor is Swains.) The most recent volcanic activity in American Samoa was a submarine eruption between Ta'u and Olosega in 1866 and again a few years ago, and this is slowly building another island. The only signs of geologically recent volcanism on Tutuila are the Tafuna Plain, and several adjacent tuff and cinder cones, including the island of 'Aunu'u, but these eruptions occurred thousands of years ago.
Tutuila was created by a series of volcanic eruptions that eventually formed a sinuous island elongated in an east-west direction. This was followed by stream erosion and submergence, Eight areas of Tutuila are classified as trachyte plugs-cores of ancient volcanoes that remain after the crater rims have eroded away, the two largest two of which are the aforementioned Le Pioa and Matafao 
(Stearns 1944)
. These plugs comprise a fine-grained, highly eroded, cream-colored "trachyte" rock. The soil formed on these formations is generally low in important minerals, which results in a stunted, scrubby vegetation called montane scrub on their slopes and summits 
(Whistler 2002)
.
The islands of Manu'a were formed by a different series of volcanic activity 
(Stice and McCoy 1968)
. Ta'u originated mostly from a single shield volcano whose caldera was located on the south side of the present island. The summit of the rim of the old caldera is called Mt. Lata, which reaches an elevation of 3170 ft (966 m) making it the highest point in the Territory. A subsequent series of collapses formed the scenic escarpments, sea cliffs, and terraces that comprise the southern coast of the island. A short, spectacular, steep-sided canyon called Laufuti cuts into the largest terrace (Li'u Bench), forming a series of waterfalls near the coast. Volcanic activity after the formation of the main shield left a series of volcanic cones and pit craters, the largest of which is Luatele Crater (Judd's Crater) on the northeast corner of the island. Marine erosion has produced the majestic cliffs and talus slopes that surround the island, and erosion and deposition created the flat and narrow bench that extends the length of the eastern coast of the island several feet above sea level. The islands of Ofu and Olosega are the remnants of another shield volcano, the sunken caldera of which lies to the south of the islands. A reef flat now spanned by a bridge connects the two islands. The old volcano was eroded over a long period of time so all that remains now is the northern rim. Traditionally, native forested areas were available for the expansion of agroforestry and agriculture. One of the most serious threats is the increasing need for agriculture land. Currently much of the food consumed in the Territory is imported, and the rest is grown in villages and plantations. But with an increasing population, shifting agriculture ("slash and burn") is occurring all too commonly on the steep forest slopes. There is simply nowhere to go but up into the forests (or into the mangroves, which as previously mentioned, is not possible). This type of cultivation is not sustainable, and leads to soil erosion (e.g., landslides), damage to the watershed, and openings in the forest that are soon covered by invasive alien species.

## Urban Development

Traditionally and historically, villages were centered in a few coastal locations, leaving forest and agroforest around and above them. Now another serious threat to the forests is their conversion to urban development. 

## Public Works Projects

Another threat to the forests of American Samoa comes from public works projects. The Territory is often awash in Federal money to be spent on major construction projects, which are subject to extensive environmental review through the NEPA process. Still, these projects can involve the clearing of forest since there is so little available suitable land in the territory.
Recently, there has been a new push to develop renewable energy projects. While some solar power development projects have been approved on previously disturbed agro-forest areas, a new proposed wind power development is being planned on the relatively flat area on the top of the mountains of Tutuila near the village of Ailaou. This project is in a secondary forested area and is included within the proposed Forest Legacy Area. This area is currently not proposed for a Forest Legacy Project because landownership of the area is still under dispute.

## Invasive Plant and Animal Species

Another serious threat to American Samoa's forests comes from alien plant and animal species. 

## Deforestation

Over 90% of the archipelago was originally covered with tropical rainforest, but as the islands became settled and the human population increased, the virgin rainforest came under a slow but deliberate onslaught as trees were felled in swidden (slash and burn) plots. This clearing proceeded slowly, since the early Samoan farmers lacked steel tools with which to fell the trees:
girdling and fire were the main methods at their disposal. However, this changed dramatically with the advent of Europeans and their technology. The new arrivals introduced steel tools to cut down the forests and plow the land, and established commercial coconut plantations by felling most of the lowland forest growing on suitable agricultural land. Because of introduced diseases, the American Samoa population underwent a drastic decline from the middle of the 19th century to the first quarter of the 20th century. The population subsequently increased rapidly from 20,000 to 57,000 in American Samoa between 1960 and 2000, with the density on the main island, Tutuila, being by far the greatest. Census data indicates the population of American Samoa has been declining in recent years, decreasing from 55,519 in 2010 to 49,710 in 2020. Even with the reduction of population, deforestation pressure remains high due to the limited land area available for development.

American Samoa is formed entirely from basalt and its erosion products, and consequently there are no mineral deposits in the islands. The useful mineral materials on the islands are cinder and rock, which is used for road building, sea walls, and other construction projects. ## Coastal Management Act

The American Samoa Coastal Management Act (A.S.C.A. Title 24, Chapter 05) provides a framework for a system of environmental review to ensure that environmental concerns are given appropriate consideration in the land use decision-making process. The act created the Project Notification and Review System (PNRS) and board to evaluate and issue land use permits.
The act also allows for areas to be designated as a "Special Management Areas" (SMA), a designation that imposes increased scrutiny and special restrictions on development, but this designation does not necessarily prevent the removal of forest or prohibit development . Rather, it only controls the nature and extent of development that will replace the existing vegetation.
Two areas within the proposed Forest Legacy Area, the Nu'uuli Pala Lagoon mangrove area and the Leone Pala mangrove area are currently recognized as SMAs. These areas are not currently under consideration for Forest Legacy Projects due to their land ownership designation. ## Land Tenure

American Samoa uses a land tenure system that consists of five ownership types : communal land owned by 'aiga (extended families) of a village, government land owned by the American Samoa government, freehold lands and individually owned lands owned by private land owners, and church lands owned by religious institutions. For communal lands, the traditional Samoan matai (chiefs) system is used to manage these lands; the matai that leads each 'aiga is given the authority to make decisions regarding the lands (more detail below), but the land is essentially private rather than government land. Both "communal" (also called "customary") and "individually owned" lands are called "Native lands", and may be acquired only by the the Western-introduced and judicially evolving concept of adverse possession and registration, under which "virgin bush land" is particularly susceptible to clearing and planting to claim ownership (Memea

The FAC and stakeholders also expressed concern or interest in the conversion of communal lands to individual lands for inclusion in the FLP. ## Goals and Objectives

The overarching goal for the American Samoan Legacy Program is to protect unique or environmentally important areas, currently belonging to private owners (communal lands currently excluded), that are threatened by conversion to non-forest uses and that fit established program eligibility criteria.
There are five main goals of the AS FLP:
1. Conserve the biodiversity of the Territory by preserving ecologically important, rare, or threatened representative areas.
2. Conserve and restore forest areas to protect watersheds, protect fresh water sources, and protect coastal areas from increased sedimentation.
3. Conserve and protecting forest in a natural state (as close to "primary forest" as possible)
or restore forest areas it to its native state.
4. Conserve forest areas to protect traditional and cultural forest practices and resources.
5. Conserve forest areas to enhance and promote public education, recreation, and ecotourism.
The recommended Forest Legacy Area (FLA), and later, specific projects within it, are selected by the American Samoa Forestry Advisory Council (FAC). By establishing a conservation program under the Forest Legacy Program, the forests of the Territory can be protected to ensure the continued use of forest by the people of American Samoa for traditional uses, conservation education, and to preserve ecological communities.

## Project Selection Process

A Forest Legacy project application (for one or more specific parcels or tracts of land) goes through territory, federal, and nationally competitive evaluation and selection processes to qualify for FLP funds. The evaluations entail a transparent, competitive, and defensible ranking process that is well communicated to program participants and partners and that ensures fair, equitable, and thorough review of all projects at the regional and national levels. At the federal level, USFS conducts a competitive evaluation process to produce a prioritized project list.
Depending upon the Congressional budget line item, funding decisions may be made within months (Inflation Reduction Act) or after project-specific Congressional appropriations taking over a year. After grant award, the acquisition process may take up to three years depending upon Congressional expectations and project complexity.
