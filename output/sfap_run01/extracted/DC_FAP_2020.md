It is also the third largest city in the region according to 2019 population estimates 1 , with a population of over 706,000 residents. This number increases with a daily influx of 661,251 commuters who live in surrounding counties. The process began with Andrew Ellicott and Benjamin Banneker surveying the new boundaries of the 100 square mile diamond, which would become the District of Columbia. A smaller area was also laid out within this square to be the City of Washington. In the later part of 1791, the surveyors' roles were complete, and L'Enfant was able to begin the design of the Washington DC federal core that is seen today.
L'Enfant's design of DC was influenced by European cities, such as Paris and Versailles, where a capital, federal buildings and monuments were constructed so as to seamlessly integrate into the natural features of the largely undeveloped land. The result was a system of intersecting diagonal avenues superimposed over a grid system that radiated away from the two largest seats of federal power and was dotted with monuments and greenspaces. In his notes, L'Enfant specified that the avenues should be grand thoroughfares lined with trees to reflect the natural beauty of the District. He also created 15 large open spaces at the intersections of these avenues and indicated that they would be divided among the states and planned that each of these greenspaces would feature statues and memorials to honor worthy citizens. He felt that the open spaces were as integral to the capital as the buildings to be erected around them. During this process, L'Enfant was relieved of his duties by George Washington and Andrew Ellicott and Benjamin Banneker finished building Washington DC.

## DC Vegetation

Because Washington DC straddles two ecoregions (see DC Ecoregion section), its geography and original vegetation consist of characteristics of both the Piedmont and the Southeast Plains ecoregion. The ecoregions consist of gently sloped land. The Piedmont changes to a level slope upland and is characterized by a stream with steep valley walls. The Southeast Plains levels where it joins the Piedmont area on the southeast side of the Anacostia River. The area where the two ecoregions meet is called the Fall Line and, in the District, runs north to south through Rock Creek Park.
The dominant native vegetation on most of the well-drained loamy soil of the Piedmont is deciduous forest

In the moderately or poorly drained soils of the Piedmont, the dominant species are beech, red maple, bitternut hickory, yellow poplar, black walnut, and black gum. The understory consists of dogwoods, holly, laurel and rhododendron with swamp oak, river birch, white ash, white willow, and hornbeams present in the floodplains.
The native vegetation for the Southeast Plains in the District is mostly hardwoods with some softwoods. In well-drained areas, chestnut oak, white oak, black oak, blackjack oak, sassafras and Virginia pine are dominant. In moderately or poorly drained soils, the dominant species are sweet gum, ash, elm, birch, sycamores, black gum, hickory, willow oak, yellow poplar, and beech. The understory is made up of holly, dogwood, laurel, red cedar, persimmon, sassafras, and sumac. There is birch, elm, alder, willow, swamp maple, sycamore, and beech in the floodplains.

## DC Soil Makeup

Due to its urban nature and centuries of development, most of DC's original soil has been removed and what exists today has been placed by humans or as a result of dredging, which is the case for the soil along the Anacostia and Potomac Rivers. Along the rivers, the soil is alluvial in nature and relatively fertile. In the developed and downtown areas of DC, most of the soil is dry and highly compacted.
The US Department of Agriculture's Natural Resources Conservation Service
foot_5
 reports that there are 128 soil types in Washington DC. These are predominantly the Christiana-Urban land complex, Manor loam, Manor-Urban land complex, Sassafras-Urban land complex, Urban land-Sassafras complex, Sunnyside-Urban land complex, Udorthents and Urban Land. Urban Land and Udorthents make up a majority of the soil types.
The Udorthents land mapping unit is characterized as heterogeneous earth fill material that has been deposited on poorly drained to somewhat excessively drained soil. It is composed of about 80 percent earthy material and 20 percent other matter. The soil of Udorthents is a mix of sand, gravelly, clayey, silty, and micaceous soils. Other matter may include bricks, trash, wire, metal, boards, cinders, industrial waste, incinerator ash and pieces of concrete and stone.
The Urban Land mapping unit is described as areas where more than 80 percent of the surface is covered with impervious surfaces, such as asphalt, concrete, or buildings. It includes miscellaneous fill and is often built over streams, swamps, floodplains, or tidal marshes.

## Storm events

The DC metropolitan region is vulnerable to three types of flooding: riverine, coastal, and interior. Riverine flooding occurs when heavy rain or snowmelt upstream of the District causes flooding in the Potomac and Anacostia River watersheds as it flows downstream. Coastal flooding occurs when a tropical storm or hurricane passes through the region causing immediate flooding in the areas it reaches. Interior floods or flash floods occur when there is heavy rainfall, and the water is forced to stay on land rather than being absorbed into the groundwater system or drained in the sewer systems. ## DC Wildlife Action Plan

The District of Columbia's State Wildlife Action Plan is a comprehensive, citywide plan and framework for managing and conserving the District's diverse animal wildlife and their habitats. The District is a part of a federal grant program that funds efforts to prevent the extinction of rare species and prevent common species from becoming rare. This plan is part of the State Wildlife Grant program (SWG).
The State Wildlife Grant program supports the conservation and management of non-game animal wildlife and their habitats. As part of this grant program, states, and territories, including the District, are required to create State Wildlife Action Plans (SWAPs). These plans identify rare and declining species and designates them as Species of Greatest Conservation Need (SGCN). The SWAP then identifies the threats to both the SGCNs and their critical habitats and creates conservation actions that will be implemented to reduce and mitigate threats to SGCNs. Actions include habitat restoration, land acquisition, wildlife inventory and new or updated regulations.
The District of Columbia developed its first SWAP in 2005 and was implemented by the Fisheries and Wildlife Division (FWD) which resides in the Natural Resources Administration of the Department of the Energy and Environment (DOEE). FWD consists of three branches: The Fisheries Research Branch, Wildlife Research Branch, and the Aquatic Education Branch. Collectively these components serve to conserve the District's aquatic and wildlife resources for the use of DC residents and wildlife. Tree canopy can be used by wildlife, even in urban and residential areas. Because of this, DOEE works with the District's Department of Transportation and Urban Forestry Division to identify patches of high canopy areas across the District that could be used as wildlife corridors and areas in the public right-of-way that could be restored to support wildlife, and plan where tree canopy can be increased to support endangered habitats. The District has an interesting dynamic the interface between humans and wildlife. It is home to both a bustling metropolis as well as a retreat for wildlife and recreationalists. Today, the District boasts more than 900 acres of city parks and more than 6,700 acres of national parkland 
46
 . DOEE updated the District's WAP in 2015 (WAP 2015) as part of SWG program requirements. The vision of the 2015 Wildlife Action Plan is "to conserve the wildlife and habitats of the nation's capital by focusing on ecosystem-based wildlife resource management actions that address the unique issues that wildlife face in an urban city and the significant challenge of climate change" 
47

## Climate Ready DC

The Climate Ready DC plan was created in 2016 in response to the goals established in the Sustainable DC plan and the recognition that Washington DC must begin to take steps to prepare and adapt for a changing climate. Climate Ready DC is the District's strategy for achieving this goal while helping to ensure that our city continues to grow greener, healthier, and more livable. Led by the Department of Energy and Environment (DOEE) in consultation with stakeholders from District agencies, as well as regional organizations and the federal government, the team assessed the impacts of climate change on our businesses and residents, especially those most physically and economically vulnerable during emergencies to ensure that the goal of providing equitable access to services, resources, and economic opportunities remains at the forefront of this and future initiatives.
As a result of climate change, DC is expecting to experience much warmer average temperatures, up 2-3 times as many dangerously hot days, longer, hotter, and more frequent heat waves, more frequent and intense heavy rain events, and higher tides as a result of rising sea level. The vision of this plan is to "thrive in the face of change". The goal of having this plan is to be able to direct the city's actions to increase considerations for things like resiliency and sustainability as we move forward in order to build a more climate ready city. It does this by looking not only at the environmental changes that will come with a changing climate, such as floods and heat waves, but also the stresses that come with these changes, such as economic hardship or deepening social inequality. In order to address these things in Resilient DC, the plan was created using elements from 11 different existing plans The two main focus areas of this plan are resilient rivers and equity in governance. The four goals are inclusive growth, climate action, safe and healthy Washingtonians, and smarter DC. Within these, there are 16 objectives that outline the work necessary to meet each of the goals and 68 initiatives that are specific, coordinated efforts and commitments to adapt to and leverage change. This plan encourages partnerships for greenspace management, discusses the coordination of future environmental research on issues including the effects of climate change on the District's urban forest, and addresses the impacts increased stormwater runoff would have on the District's water quality.
Resilient DC addresses the need for the District to reach its 40 percent tree canopy goal in two ways: it discusses the need to use data in order combat the growing urban heat island in DC, including increasing outreach for programs that increase tree planting in the District. It also suggests investing in infrastructure that provides multiple benefits, including nature-based approaches, such as tree planting.

## Priority Issues

Priority Issue 1: Expand, Preserve and Improve the Urban Forest Sub-issue 1: Increase Urban Tree Canopy across all Ownership
In Washington, D.C. the Urban Forestry Division (UFD) is the governing body primarily responsible for managing the urban forest in the public space. They are charged with tasks such as planting, pruning, removing, and maintaining the health of the District's tree canopy, all with the goal of helping the District reach its 40 percent tree canopy goal. They manage approximately 175,000 street trees as well as trees on DC parkland and recreational properties.
According to the US Forest Service, street trees make up 9 percent of District tree canopy 
52
 .
Managing an urban forest provides a unique challenge. Trees growing in cities face many different stressors compared to their rural counterparts. They may be trampled by people and heavy equipment, reducing pore space in root zones; soils can be poor or mineral deficient; impervious areas may prevent water from percolating into the ground; and limited soil space may stunt growth. Trees may be defaced by stapling or nailing signs and notices to trunks or girdling them or injured by cars, trucks, or construction equipment. These all lead to the declining health of a tree, making it more susceptible to pest and disease or damage during strong weather events. An urban forest is managed for the people and can be a challenge for forestry agencies and other municipalities and departments involved in urban forestry to keep up with and balance an evolving city with the growth and health of the urban forest.
However, the benefits that trees provide have been well studied and reported. Communities and neighborhoods with a thriving urban forest enjoy the multiple social, economic, ecosystem and health benefits that can be directly or indirectly attributed to trees. Mitigating the heat island creates areas comfortable for exercising, improving physical health, a dense tree canopy can help decrease depression and anxiety and trees placed near the entrance of a building can improve both indoor and outdoor air quality. Expanding the tree canopy is about both enhancing the District's urban forest and residents' wellbeing.

## Urban Forest Preservation Act

In 2002, the D.C. Council passed the Urban Forest Preservation Act. This law established that any nongovernmental entity that wishes to remove a tree 55 inches in circumference or larger, must obtain a Special Tree Removal Permit. Fees for the removal of Special Trees were assessed at a rate of $35 per inch of circumference, unless the tree was determined by a certified arborist to be Hazardous, or of a species identified in regulation to be invasive 53 . It also established that it is illegal, unless a permit is obtained to "top, cut down, remove, girdle, break, or destroy any Special tree" 
54
 . In 2016, this law was amended with the passage of the Tree Canopy Protection Amendment Act. This change decreased the minimum circumference of a Special tree from 55 inches to 44 inches and increased the assessment rate of Special trees to $55 per inch circumference. The amendment also created an additional designation for protection: Heritage trees. Heritage trees are any tree with a circumference of 100 inches or more and it is illegal for a nongovernmental entity to top, cut down, remove, girdle, break, or destroy a Heritage tree, unless it is deemed Hazardous.
Approximately 50 percent of the total trees in DC fall within the boundaries of Special or Heritage tree size. The majority of these, roughly 45 percent, are estimated to be Special Trees, while approximately five percent fall within the Heritage Tree designation.
Over the life of DDOT Urban Forestry Division's administration of the Urban Forest Preservation Act of 2002, nearly 8,750 permits have been issued for the removal of 15,500 Special and/or Heritage Trees. The majority of the trees permitted for removal have been in a hazardous condition, with only 21 percent of total trees removed having been non-hazardous (Figure

## Sub-issue 2: Protect and Improve Environmental Health

The interconnectedness of the environment means that a healthy tree canopy supports the health of the overall environment and those living in it. In an urban setting, this is especially important because the air purification and water filtration that trees provide not only impacts local wildlife, but it also impacts the health of the city's residents.

## Water Quality

Background DC Water, formally DC Water and Sewer Authority (DC WASA), operates a wastewater collection system composed of separate and combined sewer systems (CSS). The separate systems are comprised of two independent piping systems: one for sanitary sewage and one for stormwater. A combined system is one pipe that conveys sanitary sewage and stormwater. Approximately one third of DC is still on a combined sewer system.
During normal weather conditions, the sanitary waste collects in the CSS and is diverted to the Blue Plains Advanced Wastewater Treatment Plant. During periods of significant rainfall, the capacity of a CSS may be exceeded. When this occurs, stormwater can overwhelm the system, leading to a mixture of stormwater and sanitary waste to be discharged. Regulators within the pipe system are meant to divert the discharge directly into the Anacostia River, Rock Creek, Potomac River, or other District tributaries. This is called combined sewer overflow (CSO).
There are 53 CSO outfalls listed in the National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System permit (NPDES) from the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) (Figure

## DC Stormwater Tree Canopy Agreement

The DC government owns and operates its own Municipal Separate Storm Sewer System (MS4), which discharges stormwater during wet weather events into CSO outfalls. The Department of Energy and Environment (DOEE) is the agency responsible for this. As part of an agreement with the US EPA, DOEE is responsible for implementing a series of enhancements in order to improve overall water quality within the MS4 region. In August of 2008, the District and EPA reached agreement on a modification to the District's MS4 Permit. This modification established, with increased detail and specificity, the activities the District will undertake to better manage stormwater pollution, including planting 4,150 trees annually.
In the 2019 MS4 annual report, DOEE reported planting 9,073 new trees. Despite these increased efforts, ongoing projects by DC Water and collaboration with other agencies on stormwater management projects, DC is still experiencing water quality issues. The District's urban tree canopy reduces stormwater runoff, particularly during the smaller rains that are more frequent and carry high concentrations of pollutants. Additionally, trees that overlap impervious areas tend to have a greater ability to mitigate stormwater. This highlights the importance of planting and maintaining the trees in the public Right of Way (ROW). Having 100 percent stocking of tree spaces in the ROW is one way to make sure that the areas where the most harmful pollutants are, also have the most green infrastructure in place to prevent the pollution from reaching vital water sources.

## Soil Quality

Biochar is the substance that results from burning forest waste during pyrolysis. In this process, organic material is burned in a container with little oxygen, reducing the amount of contaminating fumes it releases. The act of burning the woody material in this way creates a charcoal-like substance that still holds the carbon contained in the original biomass, extending the carbon sequestration benefits that trees provide. Biochar has been found to be useful for restoring degraded soil and helping with nutrient leaching and water holding capacity.
Sub-issue 3: Expand Resiliency Across the District using Urban Forestry Urban Heat Island Effect Heat islands are described by the US EPA as urbanized areas that experience higher temperatures than outlying areas as a result of structures, such as buildings, roads and other infrastructure, absorbing and re-emitting the sun's heat more than natural landscapes. It is referred to as the Urban Heat Island Effect (UHI) because it is prevalent in urban areas. There are five main strategies for combating the UHI: increasing tree and vegetative cover, installing green roofs, installing cool roofs, installing reflective or permeable pavements, and utilizing smart growth practices.
In Washington DC, there can be an up to 22-degree difference in temperature across the District (Figure 
40
). Trees and vegetation decrease the severity of the urban heat island by providing shade, and cooling through evapotranspiration. Leaves and branches reduce the amount of solar radiation that reaches the area below the canopy of a tree or plant. Evapotranspiration cools the air by using heat from the air to evaporate water. Together, these processes can reduce the peak summer air temperature. Planting on long neglected public spaces with an emphasis on areas where there is high heat vulnerability or high UHI, will help mitigate these effects. As we experience more extreme heat days, having shade to prevent the urban heat island effect has become increasingly important (Figure 
41
). Between 1981 and 2020, DC averaged 39.74 inches of precipitation annually, with a majority of that being between May and July. However, with slow warming trends in the air and water, it is expected that storms, such as nor'easters, that are created by mixing of high and low temperature air, and hurricanes, that are formed by combining warm water and humid air, will increase in frequency and/or magnitude. Annual precipitation has been trending upward and it is expected that today's 100-year precipitation events could become 25-year precipitation events by mid-century and 15-year events by the 2080s (Figure 
42
). Based on scenarios developed by the DOEE and using data from NOAA and the USACE, water levels for the Potomac and Anacostia Rivers is expected to increase by 3.4 foot by 2080. This increase will put the District and many of its residents at greater risk for coastal flooding in the future (Figure 
43
). Reaching 40 percent tree canopy will increase this. With about 11,000 DC residents living within one of these potential flood zones, being able to mitigate stormwater using not only gray infrastructure, but also green, will help prevent long term storm damage.

Sub-issue 4: Management for a More Resilient Forest

## Climate Adaptation

Trees build resiliency in a city, but the urban forest itself must also be climate resilient. To create a forest that can withstand the inevitable impacts of climate change, two actions should be taken: new trees must be thoughtfully planted, putting the right tree in the right place, and proactive management to mitigate risk to residents and the forest at large.
Planting the right tree in the right place means putting trees in areas where they will not affect existing infrastructure as it grows (Figure 
46
). This means not planting large canopy trees under power lines or trees with deep roots near water lines. On the opposite end of the spectrum, this means planting trees in places where they would become vital infrastructure, such as areas prone to flooding. Planting cultivars known for high water absorption can decrease stormwater runoff during high water events, decreasing the potential for significant flooding in neighborhoods such as Navy Yard, Waterfront, and Barry Farms.
Proactive management to prevent both tree and human harm during times of stress for trees can also help manage the District's urban forest to make it more resilient to climate change.
Currently, the Urban Forestry Division (UFD) accomplishes this by inspecting forest health, monitoring survivability, and working with local utilities to proactively manage potential damage to both trees and infrastructure.
As trees age, their supporting structures can weaken. Under increased stress, a tree limb, or even the entire tree, may break. UFD performs routine maintenance of trees under their jurisdiction as well as through service requests submitted by residents. Performing tree proactive I inspection allows foresters to monitor a tree's structural stability and identify and prioritize work on trees at high risk for breakage. This allows them to proactively remove potential dangers that a tree may pose to the people and property around it. In DC, municipal arborists routinely monitor District trees and residents are encouraged to report trees that they are concerned may be hazardous. UFD is also making a conscious effort to publicize this service, so that all residents feel empowered to report tree concerns. In Fiscal Year 2019, UFD responded to 14,882 service requests for tree planting, pruning, inspections and removals. Understanding, identifying and taking steps to reduce risk is one way that the District monitors its tree canopy to ensure that it stays healthy and thriving.
The Urban Forestry Division also has partnerships with Pepco, an Exelon utility, to manage trees that could impact electric service lines. Following a severe Derecho in June 2012, the overhead power network within the District of Columbia was severely impacted. Trees, both public and private, caused widespread damage. Thousands of residents were without power during the height of the summer heat, with outages lasting for more than a week in some places. The Derecho revealed aging infrastructure and hundreds of serious tree and utility conflicts. Working in close partnership, UFD and PEPCO began a systematic process to mitigate these conflicts. While tree removal and heavy pruning practices were employed in some instances, more tree-friendly approaches were also delivered. Such collaboration continues still today, with a range of practices including the application of growth regulators to extend pruning cycles, re-engineering overhead power lines to preserve high value trees, and pre-planning of all upcoming utility vegetation management. These strategies have combined to dramatically increase overhead power reliability in the District. It is notable that these reliability improvements have come at the same that tree canopy has increased citywide.

## Pest Control, Disease Prevention

The urban forest is productive as long as the trees remain healthy. Protecting trees from pests and disease maintains both a healthy canopy and a productive urban ecosystem. In some areas of Washington DC, streets are lined with even-aged tree cohorts, which may be limited to one species. These nicely canopied avenues are striking to observe and enjoyed by residents and visitors alike. However, such low diversity in species and age puts these areas at risk to potential introductions of non-native pests and pathogens.
Urban areas are increasingly subject to introductions of non-native insects, in part due to high rates of commerce 61 . The Washington DC metropolitan area ranks 8th out of 25 urban areas for current and projected establishment rates of exotic forest insect species 62 . The costs of such introductions largely fall to local governments and individual homeowners 
63
 . In an effort to detect and limit introductions of exotic forest insects, Urban Forestry Division (UFD) deploys traps throughout the District as part of the Early Detection Rapid Response (EDRR) Program with the US Forest Service. The EDRR Program aims to detect early introductions of exotic bark and ambrosia beetles in order to prevent their establishment in the urban forest and in surrounding forested areas. In 2016, UFD set up traps in partnership with the US Forest Service, National Park Service, National Zoo and US National Arboretum. Thirty seven species of bark and ambrosia beetles were identified, 13 of which were new state records 
64
 .
In addition to this program, the Urban Forestry Division implements the Pest Vulnerability Matrix (PVM) 
65
 which enables municipal arborists and urban foresters to evaluate the overall vulnerability of their urban forest, to display this information and communicate it to others, and to evaluate the potential effects of emerging pests and diseases. In 2015, UFD partnered with Dr. Sunshine Brosi (Frostburg State University) to assess the diversity of the street tree population and the vulnerability to pests and pathogens. Dr. Brosi adapted Laćan and McBride's PVM to help guide UFD planting efforts and identify which neighborhoods are most vulnerable to pests and pathogens. The PVM reports a vulnerability index, or PVM score, for all pests and pathogens but can also be calculated at different scales, for example at ward level.

## Urban Forest Composition

As the climate continues to change, the Urban Forestry Division, as managers of the Districts urban forest, must adjust their tree planting and maintenance strategies to ensure that the forest is not just managed for the people, but for long term growth. More intense extreme heat days and strong weather events put significant strain on DC's urban forest and in response UFD will limit planting of common trees such as sugar maple and emphasize planting of more resilient tree species, including the Shumard oak which is tolerant of heat and drought. Existing trees, such as the hackberry can stand up to the changing climate as it is tolerant of drought and other environmental stressors. Today, UFD uses more than 125 species and varieties. Diversifying tree species creates a more hardy tree canopy and increases the urban forest's resiliency to pest and disease.
In addition to increasing biodiversity within DC's urban forest, expanding the age class of trees across the District can help build resilience to climate change. The District will likely experience more frequent and extreme weather events 
66
 . Such disturbance events may impact trees differently depending on their age. Mature trees may be more susceptible to damage from such extreme weather events compared to younger trees 
67
 . With increasingly erratic heat and precipitation, diversity in age not only lowers the impact a strong weather event or disease outbreak may have on the overall forest, it also spreads out tree losses, decreasing the effect removed trees may have on residents living in the urban forest.

47

). While there are many resources for residents to use to teach themselves more about their local urban forest, these are frequently unknown by the public. The District's Urban Forestry Division (UFD) works with diverse government agencies and local stakeholders in order to build capacity for urban and community forestry (UCF) programs. This is done by collaborating with local institutions, such as schools and parks, to organize tree planting events and outdoor learning activities and attending street festivals in order to interact with local neighbors and engage them in the work that UFD and other District agencies do to support the urban forest.
The goal of expanding the urban forest is to plant trees where the need and opportunity are greatest. Working with residents and community stakeholders raises awareness of the benefits trees provide and generates demand for tree canopy expansion across all landscapes.
Sub-issue 2: Make Urban Forest Benefits Accessible
The urban forest provides many different benefits that support a community's social, environmental, and economic wellbeing. However, limited access to the urban forest can prevent neighborhoods from experiencing these benefits.
The Food Forest program plants fruit trees to combat food deserts. Community food forests offer fresh, healthy food to residents that may not have access to it as well as reduce food costs, enhance physical activity, and create a space for residents to expand their social connections and for kids to gain hands on outdoor learning experiences. Additionally, expanding the District's urban forest increases exposure to forestry, not only as something that they can benefit from, but as a career they can build themselves. As part of their curriculum, elementary age students in DC schools learn about trees. UFD staff support these efforts through classroom visits and coordinated school tree plantings. UFD arborists support DCPS curricula by teaching students about the benefits of trees, tree biology, and urban forestry as a career. UFD uses established environmental education activities from Project Learning Tree to support informal science education through participation with environmental education events such as the Anacostia Environmental Youth Summit and Nature Fest at Kenilworth Park and Aquatic Gardens. Urban forestry is not just the trees, it is also the professionals that grow, maintain, and sustain them. Exposing residents to urban forestry as a profession is something that can only be done if there is access to an urban forest. UFD works to increase awareness of urban forestry as a profession by participating in career days at elementary and high schools.
Cultivating an accessible and sustainable urban forest comes from more than the professional staff, it also must come from the residents around it. Community stewardship is another way to promote long term social and environmental benefits at a neighborhood level.
Priority Issue 3: Multi-State Priority Areas Sub-Issue 1: Mid-Atlantic Shared Stewardship Urban forestry is a vital component to livable, well-planned communities, which are also important to open space conservation. An urban tree canopy reduces stormwater runoff, particularly during the smaller rains that are most frequent and often carry high concentrations of pollutants. Based on current models, trees that overlap impervious areas tend to have greater ability to mitigate stormwater. Urban tree canopy also has the side benefit of decreasing air temperatures to improve air quality.
Having a healthy urban forest in the Mid-Atlantic region is important because of the significance of the Chesapeake Bay to both humans and wildlife. The Chesapeake Bay is the largest and most productive estuary in North America. More than 64,000 square miles of land drain into the Chesapeake Bay, including parts of six states -New York, Pennsylvania, Maryland, Delaware, West Virginia, and Virginia -and DC. There are over 17 million citizens in the Chesapeake watershed, especially along the Baltimore-Washington-Richmond corridor, and it is home to over 3,600 species of plants, fish, and wildlife. The tributaries and aquifers in the watershed are a source of drinking water for residents across the region.
Washington DC's Urban Forestry Division works closely with other Mid-Atlantic states within our forestry region to link urban forestry and environmental conservation with larger regional forestry goals. Together, these agencies and their nonprofit partners protect and promote the health and safety of the Mid-Atlantic forestry region. Currently, 58 percent of the Chesapeake Bay watershed is forested, but those areas continue to be at risk of being lost to land use conversion.
UFD collaborates with local, state, regional and federal organizations including, DOEE, DC Water, DPR, US Forest Service, USDA APHIS, DNREC, MD DNR, NYS DEC, PA DEP, VA DEQ, and WV DEP, to protect and improve the Mid-Atlantic forests. This includes participation in the Chesapeake Bay Shared Stewardship Agreement and Chesapeake Tree Canopy Network, the Mid-Atlantic cooperative of forest health operators, and the Mid-Atlantic chapter of the International Society of Arborists.
As part of the Chesapeake Bay Shared Stewardship Agreement, Washington DC agrees to commit to the goals laid out in the Chesapeake Forest Restoration Strategy. Updated in 2020, this document is written in conjunction with foresters representing each state within the Chesapeake Bay watershed, the US Forest Service, and the Chesapeake Bay Program. This Strategy is a guide for joint action in:
-Restoring riparian forest buffers, -Supporting community tree canopy expansion goals, -Increasing tree planting on rural and urban lands, and -Identifying innovate and collaborative approaches for targeting restoration in areas of greatest opportunity and benefit, focusing on wildlife and fisheries habitat, mine lands, agroforestry, urban and community forestry, and contaminated lands (e.g., brownfields)
The Chesapeake Tree Canopy Network supports the Tree Canopy Outcome and Strategy adopted in the Chesapeake Bay Watershed Agreement. The District collaborates and provides leadership in the Network through developing and practicing best management practices in tree preservation, planting, tree equity, funding, inventory, maintenance, outreach, urban wood utilization, stormwater management and tree canopy analysis.
The spotted lanternfly (SLF) is a new non-native, invasive species, first observed in Pennsylvania in 2014 and has since spread with active infestations in eight Northeast and Mid-Atlantic states. It can cause damage to trees from feeding that include wilting, dieback, and excessive honeydew production leading to growth of sooty mold. The Urban Forestry Division works with the USDA APHIS to monitor for introductions of the spotted lanternfly through trapping. UFD also works with other local state and federal agencies, such as DOEE and local NPS staff, in a coordinated effort to prepare for the possible introduction of this insect pest to the District. This includes developing a management plan for SLF, mapping its preferred host (Ailanthus), and participating in the Urban Forest Strike Team (see Subsection 2: Interjurisdictional Coordination in Urban Forestry for more information about this topic).
In addition to SLF, regional foresters work together to combat the following threats:
-High white-tailed deer populations which eat seedlings and young trees, keeping the forest from regenerating and selectively browsing for food, which changes forest composition and depletes species diversity -Porcelain berry, Multiflora rose, Oriental bittersweet, mile-a-minute weed and other invasive plant species. Washington DC is a member of the Metropolitan Washington Council of Governments (COG).
The COG has representatives from 24 local governments from Maryland and Virginia, DC, and the federal government. This partnership of governments works collaboratively to forward regional goals. Currently, UFD has representatives on the Community Forestry Network committee which addresses and develops solutions to urban and community forestry problems.
Washington DC also has representatives on the National Capital Planning Commission (NCPC), a regional body that provides planning guidance for federal land, reviews designs for federal and certain local projects, and oversees long term planning for future development in the national capital region. The District Department of Transportation and Urban Forestry Division are consulted as a part of the planning, development, and guidance processes to ensure the long-term health and resiliency of the regional urban forest.
In addition to working with federal officials at COG and NCPC, UFD works with NPS, GSA, AOC, USDA, and other federal entities to coordinate tree management across District and federal boundaries. One third of the land within DC proper is owned by the federal government, making this collaboration an important part of ensuring all trees in DC are managed in the same way.
As members of the Northeast-Midwest State Foresters Alliance (NMSFA), the District works with foresters from 20 New England, Mid-Atlantic, and Midwestern states to achieve joint regional efforts related to forest health, invasive insects and pests, urban forestry, and best practices for water quality. Through NMSFA and the National Association of State Foresters (NASF), the DC Urban Forestry Division participates in the Urban Forest Strike Team and Forest Action Strike Team. The Urban Forest Strike Team coordinates regional foresters to help communities where their trees have been impacted by a natural disaster, such as large storm or fire, inventory damage and manage recovery. The Forest Action Strike Team performs similar work, though focused on responding to pest and disease outbreaks. Participating foresters assist communities where their trees have been impacted by pests or disease, in identifying hazardous trees, and mitigating existing and future damage.
On a larger scale, Washington DC's participation in Cities 4 Forests has allowed it to connect with cities across the world dedicated to restoring, managing, and preserving their trees. This program provides technical assistance training for members and allows for international knowledge sharing and collaboration.

While many of our urban landscapes' habitats are built space, they still provide habitat for wildlife and are important areas for conservation planning. Within our extremely urbanized setting, the natural areas could provide important wildlife habitat and migratory corridors. There are several options for transforming urban landscapes into habitat, including using native plants in landscaping, strategic mowing, limiting pesticides, turning off lights in buildings and educating the public as to keeping pets inside and as to the value of wildlife48  . DOEE states that because the District has a large acreage of urban landscapes, it has a responsibility to conserve species that specialize in urban habitats. The District of Columbia is 69 square miles in total area. It is 78 percent developed land; 10 percent open water; and 12 percent undeveloped forest, shrubland, or grassland (Figure36). Figure 36: Area and Percent of Developed Land and Habitat Areas in the District Categorized by Formation Class from the Northeast Terrestrial Wildlife Habitat Classification SystemDeveloped land makes up the largest proportion of the District. This includes industrial and commercial areas, roads and other paved areas, residential areas, and mowed grasslands such as athletic fields and roadside rights-of-way. The forests in the District are in the Northeastern Upland Forest and Northeastern Wetland Forest Formations. Most forested areas are found in National Park land in Rock Creek Park, National Capital Parks-East and Chesapeake and Ohio National Historical Park. Shrublands, emergent wetlands, and meadows are typically found in these parks as well. The natural areas in the District include a broad range of habitat types, including a globally rare plant community (Gravel Terrace Fall-Line Magnolia Bog) and the diverse ice-scour forest communities of the Potomac Gorge ecosystem. The Anacostia and Potomac Rivers make up a large portion of the open water of the District and several medium and small-sized creeks are tributaries of both larger water bodies. Rock Creek is a tributary of the Potomac River, while Oxon Run, Watts Branch, and many other creeks are tributaries of the Anacostia River.*

Trees naturally clean the air by trapping and absorbing pollutants. The removal of these pollutants in addition to the reduction in these pollutants provide an overall healthier environment for DC residents. The size of the tree also matters. A large, healthy tree removes approximately 70 times more air pollution every year than a small one 55 . Trees can be a good tool to help decrease ground-level ozone that results from vehicle emissions and other greenhouse gases (GHG). Based on an Urban Forests Effects Model analysis, District trees remove 490 metric tons of air pollution per year. Specifically, District trees are estimated to remove the following pollutants annually 56 :
-23 metric tons of carbon monoxide -65 metric tons of nitrogen dioxide -196 metric tons of ozone -66 metric tons of sulfur dioxide -140 metric tons of particulate matter
Poor air quality can damage the cardiovascular and respiratory systems and exacerbate chronic lung conditions, such as asthma. Using trees to improve the air quality improves the health of the residents who live around them.
