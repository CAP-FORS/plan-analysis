Anthropogenic habitats or surrogate communities are habitats that may be similar to and at least partially mimic the structure and function of natural native communities. Today's anthropogenic habitats may be inhabited by an assemblage of species that historically used structurally similar natural communities. For example, old mines are often used by bats, bridges are used by phoebes and cliff swallows, dredge spoil islands are used by terns and other colonial nesting birds, chimneys on houses are used by chimney swifts, and some tall buildings are used by peregrine falcons as nest sites. Other habitats such as agricultural fields and restored grasslands are used by a wider variety of bird species as nesting and feeding areas.
High quality surrogate grasslands or planted prairies and backyard gardens with a diversity of native forbs provide the floral resources needed for some of our most imperiled pollinators, such as the rusty patched bumble bee. Some anthropogenic habitats are very important for wildlife and should be considered for protection (e.g., old mines). Some anthropogenic habitats provide important habitats for many SGCNs and changes in management can have important effects. For example, the conversion of hayfields and pastures to agricultural row crops is believed to be a contributing factor in the decline of grassland birds and pollinators.
Anthropogenic habitats can be used to create wildlife corridors between natural communities and to buffer the effects of human land and water uses by creating and managing lands immediately surrounding natural communities.

## Grassland habitats

• Enhance seed mixes that include only native plant species and that provide floral resources from spring to fall. Consider including host plants for SGCN insects that are an ecological priority per landscape and natural community associations.
• Increase awareness about best herbicide and pesticide practices, including label and legal requirements relating to listed species. Avoid broad-cast spraying in and around habitats supporting SGCN pollinators.
• Reduce tree and brush cover on surrogate grasslands through burning, grazing, or mowing enables larger patches of suitable grassland habitat for grassland bird SGCNs.

Impounded rivers and lacustrine mud flats
• Restore and maintain natural banks where shoreline and riparian areas to restore SGCN habitat and to buffer variations in water levels and wave action.
• Continue Mississippi River restoration projects that aim to mitigate the impacts of impoundment and navigation, including the rebuilding of lost islands (themselves former high points within the floodplain), removal of sediment from backwater lakes to increase habitat complexity and preserve fish habitats, protection of islands, marshes and shorelines from wind-and wave-driven erosion, and similar actions to promote the water quality, habitat and wildlife of the river ecosystem. Connectivity of habitats is also a consideration in restoration projects.

## Shrub and forested habitats

• Restore barrens, pine-oak forest, and shrub-dominated habitats on sites such as old fields and pasture lands with the goal of expanding and connecting existing stands.
• Develop clear goals for desired future condition at a regional scale, considering forest type and age class, as well as the spatial arrangement of different types of forest and plantations on the landscape.
• Research ways to enhance landscape connectivity (e.g. through forest patch size, arrangement, corridors, etc.) between patches of young, mid-seral, and old forest for species that require large blocks of forested habitat.
