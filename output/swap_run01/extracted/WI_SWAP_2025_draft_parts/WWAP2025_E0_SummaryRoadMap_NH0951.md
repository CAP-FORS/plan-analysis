## Contributors

Over 300 experts and professionals both within and outside the Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources were invited to contribute their knowledge to the content of the Wisconsin Wildlife Action Plan. The data, comments and guidance received were critical to revising the Plan.
The development of the 2025-2035 Wisconsin Wildlife Action Plan was made possible, by funding from the Wisconsin DNR Bureau of Natural Heritage Conservation.

## Background

In 2001, Congress authorized the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service to implement and fund a program to help states proactively address the needs of declining wildlife species before they required listing as Endangered or Threatened. The State Wildlife Grants (SWG) Program provides federal grant funds for native wildlife, including species not hunted or fished. Wildlife, as defined by this program, is any species of wild, free-ranging fauna including fish and invertebrates. The SWG Program continues to be the nation's core program for preventing wildlife from becoming endangered.
States must have an approved State Wildlife Action Plan (SWAP) to receive the federal apportionment or competitive grants through the State Wildlife Grant program

How the elements are addressed is largely left up to the needs and circumstances of each state. Use and implementation of the information and conservation actions described in the WWAP are voluntary. ## The Eight Required Elements for State Wildlife Action Plans

(1) Element 1 Species [Species of Greatest Conservation Need (SGCN) and Species of Greatest Information Need (SGIN)]: Information on the distribution and abundance of species of wildlife, including low and declining populations as the State fish and wildlife agency deems appropriate, that are indicative of the diversity and health of the State's wildlife; and,
(2) Element 2 Habitats and Natural Communities: Descriptions of locations and relative condition of key habitats and community types essential to conservation of species identified in (1); and,
(3) Element 3 Threats: Descriptions of problems which may adversely affect species identified in (1) or their habitats, and priority research and survey efforts needed to identify factors which may assist in restoration and improved conservation of these species and habitats; and, (4) Element 4 Actions: Descriptions of conservation actions proposed to conserve the identified species and habitats and priorities for implementing such actions; and,
(5) Element 5 Monitoring: Proposed plans for monitoring species identified in (1) and their habitats, for monitoring the effectiveness of the conservation actions proposed in (4), and for adapting these conservation actions to respond appropriately to new information or changing conditions; and, (6) Element 6 Review and Revision: Descriptions of procedures to review the strategy at intervals not to exceed ten years; and, (7) Element 7 Partner Involvement: Provisions for coordination during the development, implementation, review, and revision of its Plan with Federal, State, and local agencies and Indian tribes that manage significant land and water areas within the State or administer programs that significantly affect the conservation of identified species and habitats; and, (8) Element 8 Public Involvement: Provisions to provide the necessary public participation in the development, revision and implementation of the Plan.
Currently, states receive funds from the State Wildlife Grants program on an annual basis, which are allocated according to a formula that considers each state's size and population. A portion of grant money is also allocated to competitive grants. Plans are submitted to a regional review team (RRT) comprised of regional representatives from state wildlife agencies and the USFWS, which makes recommendations to the Regional USFWS Director to approve (with or without condition) each revised plan.

## Purpose

This Plan, which has been prepared with input from experts and partners across the state, provides information about species and their habitats to support meaningful conservation decisions regardless of whether conservation is the primary objective or incidental to other objectives.

## Wisconsin's Approach to the WWAP

The WWAP is a comprehensive inventory of the nature and status of rare and declining species, referred to as Species of Greatest Conservation Need (SGCN), natural communities and ecological landscapes within our state.
The WWAP's application goes beyond that of meeting a federal grant requirement. It is a plan for all of Wisconsin to help rare and declining species so that they and the habitats where they live are sustained for future generations. The Wisconsin Wildlife Action Plan should not only inform, but be informed by the knowledge, experience and success of its users. Elements of the WWAP are part of a continuous effort of assessment, improvement and implementation.
It is our goal to make this third digital version of the WWAP more useful and accessible to all DNR programs, as well as individuals and organizations outside the DNR that carry out conservation actions in our state regardless of whether they are part of or secondary to their primary objectives. We hope that the WWAP will serve as a living document that both guides conservation priorities for SGCN and their habitat AND is guided by the progress, knowledge gained, and success toward achieving those goals. Together with partners and stakeholders that have a conservation interest, we are looking forward to continuing implementation of the WWAP with better tools to use the plan and monitor the success of our efforts.

## Summary of Outcomes for the 2025 Wisconsin Wildlife Action Plan

Although there have been no significant changes to the approach taken to each of the eight elements in the 2025 WWAP, there have been important outcomes and conclusions from this revision.
• The current approaches used to fulfill major elements of the WWAP: NatureServe's conservation status methodology and ranking factors; input from people with knowledge and expertise; association scores to link SGCN, natural communities and landscapes; identification/description of Conservation Opportunity Areas as the best places to carry out conservation; and an organized classification and reporting of priority threats and actions, have persisted as a sound foundation for each iteration of the WWAP.
• Because the overall approach has not significantly changed, the 2025 WWAP and future WWAPs can focus on consistency and tools that take advantage of increasing amounts of data and information to improve accessibility, analysis and outcomes for each element.
• Increasing emphasis on digital or online formats for gathering information (e.g., communitybased monitoring) offers continuous and broad opportunities for input. It also offers access to important data and outcomes that conservation decisionmakers can use to meet their own objectives.
• Plants have been added as SGCN and SGIN to the 2025 WWAP. They have also been assigned association scores, are part of the COA attributes, and Threats and Actions Reports. ## Summary Element 4 -Conservation Opportunity Areas

Conservation Opportunity Areas (COAs)
foot_6
 are the best places in Wisconsin to implement conservation actions in the Wisconsin Wildlife Action Plan (WWAP) to protect and conserve SGCNs and their habitat. They are places on the landscape with significant ecological features, natural communities or SGCN habitats for which Wisconsin is responsible for protecting and conserving. Focusing conservation actions in these prioritized areas can increase the likelihood of long-term success, maximize effectiveness over larger landscapes, improve funding efficiency, and promote voluntary cooperative efforts across ownership boundaries.
Those undertaking conservation actions in our state, whether they are interested individuals, landowners, businesses, conservation groups or resource agencies may focus their actions in conservation opportunity areas. Because of how they are defined, these areas imply greater likelihood of success in helping SGCN and their habitat. COAs are mappable sites relying on readily available georeferenced information and do not identify all the places important or necessary for conservation in our state. Conversely, the boundaries of COAs do not imply that the same level of opportunity for conservation exists throughout them.
The same criteria originally used in the 2005 WWAP have been updated with more current and concise language. Each of the 193 COAs fulfills one or more of these criteria.

## Summary Element 1 -Species of Greatest Conservation Need

The process by which Species of Greatest Conservation Need (SGCN) are selected has not changed from the 2015 WWAP, although an effort was made in the current revision to introduce greater consistency and provide easier access to data. Internal and external experts and professionals and internal teams used this process to produce a new list of SGCNs as well as Species of Greatest Information Need (SGIN), which are plants and animals that either need more information before they can be fully assessed or are borderline SGCN and need to be monitored.
Each major taxonomic group was reassessed. Many species were assessed with the use of NatureServe's Conservation Status Assessment methodology (also known as Natural Heritage Methodology) to derive state and global ranks (SRank and GRank). Ten factors were used to assess conservation status, grouped into three categories: rarity, threats, and trends. In addition to SRanks and GRanks, additional filters were used to place emphasis on characteristics relevant to Wisconsin, including high or moderate vulnerability to climate change, genetically isolated species, cultural significance, regional status, or species with unique habitats in our state. SGIN that could not be assessed were also identified. The same approach was applied to invertebrates, vertebrates and plants 3 . The SGCN selection process ensures a well-documented and consistent assessment of species, which can be readily updated as new information becomes available. Tables

For each SGCN, the critical habitats needed to support healthy populations within Wisconsin and the distribution of those habitats in the state were identified in the form of association scores with Natural Communities and Ecological Landscapes (SGCN-NC and SGCN-EL scores rated from 0 = none to 3 = high). The DNR's Natural Heritage Inventory program 4 was used to identify and define most terrestrial, wetland and aquatic community types used in this analysis. Ecological landscape definitions were taken from the Ecological Landscapes of Wisconsin Report 5 .
All 111 vegetation community types are grouped within eight major habitat groups: anthropogenic, aquatic, barrens and savannas, bedrock and bare soil/sand, prairies and grasslands, northern forest, southern forest and wetland. ## Summary Element 5 -Monitoring

Monitoring focuses on opportunities to measure the implementation of priority conservation actions. Given the breadth and complexity of the WWAP elements and the diversity of partners and WWAP users who implement conservation actions, it is not feasible to effect comprehensive WWAP monitoring. Rather Element 5 as it relates to SGCN, habitats, threats and actions, is currently fulfilled by NHC and the Department through multiple types of monitoring:
• Wisconsin Forest/Field Inventory and Reporting System (WisFIRS) allows foresters, biologists, and ecologists to store data collected in the field, plan for and track completed treatments.
• Annual Property Implementation Plans (APIP) and Plan Monitoring Reports share with the public and partners the major scheduled and completed forest and habitat management treatments, recreation and infrastructure development projects and other property management actions. • The Natural Heritage Inventory program applies a standard methodology for collecting, characterizing and managing data, making it possible to combine data at various scales to address local, state, regional and national issues. • Grant and Other Project Reporting such as the Wildlife Tracking and Reporting Actions for the Conservation of Species (TRACS), a reporting system used by the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service's (USFWS) Office of Conservation Investment (OIC) that captures information about conservation and related actions funded by its grant programs. • Surface Water Integrated Monitoring System (SWIMS) provides science-based monitoring, analysis and regulation of discharges to surface waters to protect and maintain the water quality in Wisconsin through management actions including aquatic plant management, aquatic invasive species initiatives, and lakes, rivers, and wetland grants.
