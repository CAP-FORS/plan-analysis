Protecting or conserving SEFs does not translate to protecting specific SGCNs, but rather biodiversity in general, recognizing that the distribution and abundance of species and their habitat is changing.
Each SEF is assigned to a geographic scale of uniqueness based on common expert knowledge: global, continental, Midwest-regional or state. These categories can assist users to communicate priorities in different contexts. Place examples are provided for each SEF.

## SEF 1. Bedrock Communities Geographic scale of uniqueness: State

Bedrock communities can be found throughout the state in a variety of forms and landscape settings, and often support unique and extreme microclimates. Some natural communities that are present with these features are extremely rare, including Alvar (also treated under SEF 8. Great Lakes and their Shorelines), Algific Talus Slope, Bedrock Glade, Glaciere Talus, and Talus Forest. Wisconsin has two cliff communities included in this feature that, while not rare, support many rare or declining plant and animal species (e.g., timber rattlesnake on Dry Cliffs and federally listed northern monkshood on Moist Cliffs). Priority opportunities exist where bedrock communities occur within a matrix of supportive natural cover types such that essential microclimates are preserved (e.g., sufficient canopy cover growing above and around Moist Cliff, Algific Talus Slope and Glaciere Talus). Sites with diverse microhabitats conferred by a variety of slopes, aspects, and microclimates should also rank higher in conservation assessments as they allow species to shift through space and time as environmental conditions change. The Niagara Escarpment is a bedrock feature that is highlighted as a separate Significant Ecological Feature given its very broad extent and influence on the regional landscape.

## SEF 3. Caves, Abandoned Mines and Tunnels

Geographic scale of uniqueness: Midwest Regional
Wisconsin has approximately 150 natural caves, abandoned mines, and former rail tunnels that have become hibernacula and mating sites for Wisconsin's four hibernating bat species, all of which have been impacted by White-nose Syndrome. As of 2025, more than 40% of the U.S. population of little brown bats--nearly 100,000 individuals --overwinter in only three of these sites: Maiden Rock Mine and Bay City Mine along the Mississippi River, and Neda Mine on the Niagara Escarpment 
(WI DNR Bat Program, unpublished data, 2025)
.
A few smaller hibernation sites in the Penokee Range, Door Peninsula, and Central Sands remain important for significant numbers of little brown bats and tricolored bats. Certain hibernacula, including former rail tunnels and natural caves in the Driftless Area and Central Sands region serve large numbers of big brown bats along with other species. Despite severe population declines from White-nose Syndrome, Northern long-eared bats are still found using several hibernacula around Wisconsin each winter, though site choice by these individuals is difficult to predict.

## SEF 7. Glacial Lake Wisconsin

Geographic scale of uniqueness: Midwest Regional
The bed of Glacial Lake Wisconsin occurs in central Wisconsin and is a biodiversity hotspot. Barrens and pine-oak forests occur on level to rolling uplands and are juxtaposed with large wetland complexes, both of which are occasionally punctuated by towering sandstone buttes, ridges, and pinnacles. The habitat diversity combined with the location along the climatic Tension Zone and supports a unique mixture of southern and northern species. Many SGCN, especially habitat and area-sensitive species, thrive in the area. Due in part to sandy soils unsuited for row crops, large blocks of public land are found here. This large expanse of public land provides some of the best opportunities to manage for the full continuum of pine-oak forest to barrens to wetlands in the upper Midwest. 

## SEF 8. Great Lakes and their Shorelines Geographic scale of uniqueness: Global

The Great Lakes are the largest freshwater lakes in the world. Their shorelines and nearshore waters provide hundreds of miles of mostly continuous habitat for hundreds of species. Known for supporting endemic plants and animals like dwarf lake iris and Piping Plover, the Great Lakes are also part of the one of the major migratory flyways of North America and are utilized by millions of birds each year. Sandspits and coastal barrier spits such as Long Island and Wisconsin Point, as well as the restored Long Tail Point in Green Bay are especially important and support large concentrations of migratory birds while tiny islands provide habitat for nesting colonial waterbirds. The sandscapes of Lakes Superior and Michigan are also unique with beaches and dunes providing critical habitat for rare plants and invertebrates.
Islands in the Great Lakes provide unique habitat. The Apostle Islands have exceptional examples of old growth forests, beach and dune complexes, coastal wetlands, and bedrock features that support a tremendous regional repository of rare biota. The Door Peninsula and Grand Traverse Islands that stretch northeastward have high concentrations of rare species associated with the calcareous soils and exposures of dolomite that characterize shorelines.
The freshwater estuaries on the south shore of Lake Superior in Wisconsin are among the highest quality in the entire Great Lakes region, having better water quality, more intact natural communities, fewer invasive species, and less development in the surrounding landscape than most sites along Lakes Michigan, Huron, and Erie. Several Great Lakes ridge and swale complexes can also be found in Wisconsin, though they are more abundant in Michigan.
Containing alternating series of parallel wetlands and forested upland ridges, they support incredible diversity in a small geographic area and provide important habitat for a variety of species, including plants, herptiles, and nesting and migrating birds.

These minimally fragmented complexes of high-quality habitat in proximity provide exceptional benefit to wildlife. Large blocks are also more resistant to catastrophic windthrow than small, isolated stands. In most areas, older forests are embedded in a relatively intact matrix of forest managed for timber, providing additional habitat of mixed successional stages and age classes. The broad areas of relatively continuous forested habitat also serve as a regionally important climate migration pathway.
The Menominee Reservation has vast relatively unbroken hemlock-hardwood forests, scattered lakes, and ecologically important streams within forested watersheds. Large white cedar swamps are common in the eastern portion, where marl lakes supporting calciphilic plants occur. Prominent exposures of granitic bedrock occur along the Wolf River. Most of the forest is older than average for the state and supports significant populations of forest interior species that have become scarce in forests elsewhere. The Upper Mississippi serves as a vital habitat for diverse wildlife, including fish, migratory birds, and other aquatic organisms, supporting rich ecosystems along the northern portion of the largest river in North America. The Lower Wisconsin is the longest free-flowing stretches of river in Midwest and supports both plants, fish, and aquatic invertebrates that depend on its mostly natural dam-free hydrologic regime. The Lower Wolf River is one of the few remaining rivers with a high degree of natural meandering, required by some aquatic species including rare mussels. The St. Croix River is also home to a disproportionally large number of mussel species, including several that are endangered. These river systems also support large floodplain forests and associated riverine lakes and sloughs, which provide highly connected habitat for migratory and breeding birds, herps, and plants. As corridors with abundant natural habitat, they are also important for climate migration and larger landscape-level resiliency. Due to geologic history and geomorphology, all floodplain complexes associated with large rivers also occur in the Driftless Region and could be treated under either category depending on whether viewed from a terrestrial or aquatic conservation perspective.

## SEF 13. Medium-sized Rivers and Streams

Geographic scale of uniqueness: Midwest Regional Wisconsin supports numerous medium-sized rivers of Midwest significance. Medium-sized rivers and streams are moderate to large but are still wadeable. In the Wisconsin DNR riverine natural classification, they are classified as cool, cold and warm mainstem streams with annual 90% exceedance flow ranges from 1.0 -110 ft 3 /s. River systems such as the Upper Wolf River, Namekagon, Lemonweir, Bark and Pine-Popple fall into this category. Some of these rivers support some of the best populations of globally imperiled wood turtles in the Great Lakes region. These riparian areas also contain significant populations of fish and rare invertebrates such as mussels and dragonflies, though often have fewer species than their larger counterparts. Medium-sized rivers also serve as major migratory bird stopover areas and often abundance of white-tailed deer has left structural relicts of savannas with scattered opengrown oaks and decimated ground layers. The Southern Unit of the Kettle Moraine State Forest, portions of the Central Sand Hills and Central Sand Plains Ecological Landscapes, and some places in the Western Coulees and Ridges Ecological Landscape, are areas where significant management opportunities exist for these communities. Bedrock Glades are also included here because they support similar structure, characteristic species, associated SGCN, and threats; Wisconsin has the best conservation opportunities in the world for conservation of certain types of this community such as quartzite bedrock glades of the Baraboo Hills within the Western Coulee and Ridges.

## SEF 15. Niagara Escarpment

## Geographic scale of uniqueness: Global

The Niagara Escarpment is a bedrock feature composed mostly of Silurian dolomite that arcs from Lake Champlain in the northeastern United States westward across the Bruce Peninsula of Ontario through the Upper Peninsula of Michigan and southward through eastern Wisconsin.
Its length and influence on the landscape warrant highlighting it as a separate feature, apart from Bedrock Communities. Here the Escarpment is exposed from the islands off the northern tip of the Door Peninsula southwest for over 150 miles into southeastern Wisconsin before it is buried beneath glacial deposits. The Escarpment supports many rare species, including providing habitat for globally rare snails, and hibernacula for bats in addition to being an important north-south migratory corridor with stopover habitat for migratory birds and bats.
Cliffs along the Escarpment also support the oldest trees known in Wisconsin with some individuals over 1,000 years old. Important natural communities associated with the Escarpment include globally rare alvar as well as moist cliffs, dry cliffs, caves, talus forests, along with a type of oak woodland that borders on limestone bedrock glade specific to the Niagara Escarpment.

## SEF 16. Northern Highland Kettle Lakes and Pine Forest

Geographic scale of uniqueness: Midwest Regional
The Northern Highland region boasts one of the highest densities of glacial kettle lakes in North America surrounded by extensive red and white pine forest and high-quality wetlands. While lakes are common in many glaciated areas across the northern U.S. and Canada, most concentrations occur on bedrock driven landscapes, such as the Canadian shield. The sandy pitted outwash of the Northern Highland region supports some of the region's only oligotrophic seepage lakes, whose circumneutral waters, exceptionally low nutrients, and firm bottoms support a unique aquatic plant flora and rare invertebrates. Other lakes and low-gradient streams support lush beds of wild rice, which are important both ecologically and culturally. While many lakes in the region have been developed or hydrologically modified, a significant number of wild lakes still occur here, and these are the focus of conservation efforts.
This region presents an opportunity to conserve and manage large-scale white pine and red pine forests, which thrive in the cool climate and nutrient-poor sandy soils. Wildlife species associated with coniferous forests are especially well-represented here. Some of the most unique pine forests occur adjacent to lake margins and large peatland complexes, where periodic surface fires and drying winds have maintained a more open and heterogeneous structure than is typically found in fire-suppressed stands. Pine regeneration is currently lacking in many sites due to overly dense and shady subcanopies, but prescribed fire as well as silvicultural techniques can be used to manage and restore these forests along with the species that depend on them. Northern mesic forests dominated by hemlock and northern hardwoods also occur here and are an important part of the extensive forest cover that provides diverse wildlife habitat and opportunities for species migration related to climate change.
Northern Highland -Historically, the Northern Highlands were the site of Wisconsin's greatest pinery, which are recovering in some areas with large trees and natural regeneration. The Northern Highland-American Legion State Forest offers Wisconsin's best opportunity to protect, manage, and restore forests of large white and red pines. Site capability, historical events, scattered remnants, current patterns of succession, and a large public land base make this possible at multiple scales. Forest restoration priorities have been identified here for eastern white pine, red pine, eastern hemlock, tamarack, and jack pine. The Northern Highland EL also boasts a density of lakes rivaled in few other parts of the world, making this EL globally important for this resource. Seepage lakes are the most abundant type here, with soft and very soft types particularly unique and important for conservation initiatives. ## SEF 20. Southeast Glacial Plain Marshes

## Geographic scale of uniqueness: State

An aggregation of deep marshes and hemimarshes in the Southeast Glacial Plains EL supports significant resident wildlife populations (particularly waterfowl, marsh birds, and colonial waterbirds) and hosts major concentrations of migratory birds in spring and fall. While many may not qualify as high-quality wetlands from a floristic standpoint, they offer essential habitat including food, cover and shelter for numerous SGCN. These extensive deep marshes and hemimarshes provide an opportunity to manage a broad range of SGCN in concert with other programs focused on waterfowl. These communities can be managed to maximize SGCN diversity in addition to waterfowl targets. Adjacent natural communities can be managed to buffer core areas from the surrounding croplands that dominate this landscape, as well as to expand functional habitat for area sensitive species and meet the needs of species that migrate between uplands and wetlands throughout their life history (e.g., some herptiles).
