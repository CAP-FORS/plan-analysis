## Threat/Issue H2-Convergent Effects of Development, Water Management and Recreation

## CMP Threat Classification H2

1.0 Residential, Commercial & Recreation Areas 6.1 Recreational Activities 7.2 Dams and Water Management/Use Housing, urban, commercial and recreational development is the second largest threat to Herptile according to the Herptile threat assessment. It is a significant issue for SGCN Herptile because it results in loss of breeding and foraging habitat or changes to the composition of habitat. The residential development of shorelines is a significant issue for lake dwelling species because it degrades or eliminates habitat. Specifically, development causing regional extirpations.
Dam infrastructure and soil erosion measures (e.g., riprap) related to shoreline development can both directly and indirectly affect Herptile in Wisconsin. Riprap and erosion matting is known to entrap and kill small Herptile (especially turtles and snakes). Dams can modify and degrade riverine habitat to the extent that fish and turtle species that rely on clean water and constant flow no longer can be found using such areas or upstream of such dams. Dams and soil erosion measures additionally prevent naturally occurring erosion of riverine corridors.
Human intrusion and disturbance in the form of recreational activities exacerbate the effects of development. Wakes created by motorized boating or general creation of wakes in lake and riverine systems on lakes/rivers without wake zones, severely impact amphibian and fish habitats near the shorelines. Recreational overuse of public lands and waters, causing erosion and disturbance from bike and ATV trails, noise and wakes from boats. These activities contribute noise and light pollution that disturb natural behavior and physiology.

## Conservation Planning

Improve plans for preserving isolated populations and connecting them to other populations (example, Spotted Salamander in SE Wisconsin); utilizing local species status assessments in regional planning.
Improve planning for limits on use of trails and designating natural areas without trails to reduce disturbance. Disease and invasive species was cited as a major threat for 48 herptile species during the threat assessment. This includes viral, bacterial, and fungal diseases and parasitism. Invasive species can negatively impact herptile in a variety of ways. For example, invading reed canary grass and giant reed grass may simplify habitats, including lowering wetland and shoreline habitat quality by eliminating the soil and surface conditions that allow crayfish to burrow. Burrows created by native crayfish provide the primary overwintering shelter for the Eastern massasauga rattlesnake. Seasonal use by Eastern massasauga rattlesnakes of reed canarydominated areas is also much lower than that of areas with native wetland vegetation. In general, invasive plants change habitat structure and diversity that in turn affect insect and invertebrate populations that form the food base for reptiles and amphibians. Alterations of aquatic habitats may favor increasing pathogen (trematodes) vectors such as snails, resulting in an increased incidence of malformations, potentially affecting recruitment rates. An increase of meso-predators in addition to invasives species encroaching on formerly suitable nesting habitat negatively impacts nesting success for turtles. All types of land and aquatic management activities are needed to prevent and control the damaging effects of invasive plant species that disrupt and degrade natural relationships between herptiles and food sources. The Wisconsin DNR's website provides a detailed explanation of prevention and control measures for aquatic and terrestrial invasive species. However, to address threats to Herptile, the location of invasive species where they overlap particularly vulnerable herptile food sources, nesting sites, dens and other essential life history resources needs to be mapped.

With the assumption that invasive species have negative outcomes for ecological processes, research should be focused on whether and to what degree management measures are successful in reestablishing healthy populations of insects and other invertebrates for reptiles and amphibians.

## Basic Research and Status Monitoring

New and emerging diseases will continue to jeopardize herptile SGCN. Monitoring efforts are needed to help identify new disease cases, to track the spread of existing cases.
Research to identify causes, mechanisms of transport, etc. is needed for new and emerging disease. Disease is a threat to amphibians and reptiles. For example, snake fungal disease has affected several Wisconsin snake species and research is ongoing to learn more about the extent and spread of this disease. herptiles, particularly in urban environments, to encourage preservation and restoration by landowners.
Where landownership permits, protect and manage nesting sites for habitat suitability (i.e., control encroachment by invasive plant species), and to minimize nest predation. This may include restrictions on recreational use of important sites during mid-May through June. Local status assessments, planning and direct contact with landowners help to incentivize people to implement these actions to benefit their community. The net impacts of climate change and extreme weather events, including expected warmer and drier conditions in our state, will negatively impact many herptile SGCNs. This was mentioned as a major threat for 32 species of herptiles during the threat assessment. Poor water quality (e.g., low dissolved oxygen) may be a limiting factor for cricket frogs, which seem to be especially sensitive to this. Competition among native species (green frogs, bullfrogs v. mink frogs) may occur if green and bullfrogs advance further into mink frog range with average increases in water and air temperature. Mink frog embryos have limited tolerance to warmer water temperatures and consequent lower oxygen diffusion rates. Prolonged flooding events have been found to drown species like eastern massasauga and turtle nests. Cold winters with little snowpack result in mortality of overwintering turtles and frogs. Limited mobility of most herptile species make it difficult for them to move to more favorable areas at a pace that keeps up with changes in climate. Moreover, while trends in environmental conditions can be measured and modeled, the spatial and temporal variability of changes in climate and weather are more difficult to project, making it difficult to determine where to target adaptation measures. Harvest of snakes, frogs, and turtles for research, education, pet trade, and for personal use continues to be an issue for some herptile species, especially turtles. This was mentioned as a major threat for 29 species of Herptiles during the threat assessment. Herptile enthusiasts alter habitat by flipping rocks, rolling logs, etc. and not putting them back the way they were. This decreases the micro-habitat elements associated with those features.

## Laws, Regulations & Codes

State and federal legislation and regulation establish prevention, controls and limits for the collection and harvest of herptile SGCN. Since many of these species are already listed as threatened or endangered, state endangered species laws prohibit their collection. Develop justifications for monitoring that must have a tangible positive conservation outcome. Require monitoring be tied to habitat management or research goals and not be pursued solely for public engagement.

## Law Enforcement & Prosecution

More widespread compliance and enforcement of trade restrictions is needed. In addition to the agriculture footprint, agricultural effluents that move offsite and contain sediments, nutrients and chemicals, can change or decrease water quality if they reach the wetlands and aquatic habitats where herptile SGCN live. Moderate to intensive grazing can cause shoreline disturbance and impact turbidity of water, which can lead to negative impacts on frogs, eggs, and tadpoles. Runoff of pesticides and herbicides, like atrazine, may threaten frogs directly by killing eggs, larvae, or adults. Indirect effects of pesticides may include alterations in behavior (frogs are less able to escape predators) and changes in the food base (invertebrates are killed by pesticides). Contaminants may also alter sex ratios of amphibians, resulting in reduced reproductive success. This is particularly important for amphibian SGCNs and in those parts of the state where intensive agriculture occurs near wetland and warmwater aquatic communities.
Biological resource use is also a frequently cited as an issue for herptile SGCN. One of the subcategories identified in this category is wood harvest and related practices. Wood harvest within riparian habitats that results in a loss of large and fine woody debris (loss of structural complexity) in forests results in lack of habitat for the woodland salamander species, turtles and snakes.

## CMP Conservation Actions Classification H6

1.1 Site/Area Stewardship Comprehensive management considers the full suite of protection, preservation and restoration activities to sustain and improve habitat for herptile SGCN. This is most applicable to public lands and conservation lands that are established to prevent habitat loss and fragmentation. In these areas it is important to preserve and manage connections between wetlands and uplands to facilitate movement of herptile SGCNs between these habitats (e.g. turtles seeking nesting sites, snakes moving from basking areas to den sites). Herptile use a wide variety of habitats from sand prairies to streams to ephemeral ponds; restoration, management and protection of these diverse habitats are the primary actions proposed for conserving herptile Species of Greatest Conservation Need in Wisconsin. A wide variety of efforts will be needed to restore, conserve and protect these habitats, from management of prairies to reduce impacts of natural succession, to reducing densities of invasive plants, such as reed canary grass in wetland areas. Examples of management practices include expanding wetland buffers, controlling runoff/preserving topsoil, limit chemical use, restoring drained wetlands, using winter cover crops, no till practices, etc.

## Ecosystem & Natural Process (Re)Creation

Seek to replace and improve the habitat elements that have been degraded or lost because of past forest management practices as well as agricultural development and pollution from agricultural effluents. This action category presumes that multiple objectives or uses exist on the landscape. That is, connected upland, aquatic and wetland habitats for herptile SGCNs persist in an agricultural matrix. Conservation projects or practices can be targeted at a specific aspect(s) or process that is important for herptile SGCN habitat such as restoring riparian vegetation, diversity of vegetative structure, leaving adequate distances between disturbance and wetland or aquatic habitats, or upland nesting sites and preserving enough woody debris around ephemeral ponds and streams in managed forests to sustain salamanders, turtles or frogs.
For many herptiles, it is important to restore and maintain open canopy low vegetational areas near rivers and lakes, interspersed with some shrubs, and full canopy and minimize succession of our open canopy habitats into those with a full canopy. Turtles and their nesting grounds need disturbance regimes (whether natural or human generated) to maintain habitat for them, otherwise, they go to less favorable "sink" habitats such as roadways to nest. This is happening on a large scale with wood turtles, and to some extent ornate box turtle and Blanding's turtle.
Continue to work with private landowners and public land agencies at discretely identified sites to protect turtle nests and maintaining these nesting habitats that are multiple use areas for nesting, thermoregulation, and foraging. Especially in high turtle mortality areas, and areas with SGCN turtle species. Gain landowner assistance to protect these resources and prevent access for the purpose of illegal turtle and other herptile trade.

## Laws, Regulations & Codes

Best Management Practices (BMPs) for the forestry and agricultural sectors establish important elements for conserving and protecting habitat for SGCN herptile. Individuals and organizations that apply these standards and practices can consider how closely their production and resource use objectives can be aligned with conservation objectives for herptile SGCNs.

In landscapes containing natural communities with moderate or high association for herptile SGCNs, engage landowners and the public in community-based monitoring of turtles. ## CMP Conservation Actions Classification H10

## Basic Research & Status Monitoring

A combination of inventory and monitoring is needed as well as research on transmission and ecology of herptile diseases. Information is concentrated in publicly protected or preserved lands. More inventory work is needed on private lands through landowner assisted monitoring or other surveys, depending on the target species. Creation and support of herptile landowner assisted monitoring projects, including periodic inventory surveys are important to assist filling information gaps.
Reptiles have little or no long-term monitoring taking place. It is crucial for status assessments and identifying viable populations, which leads us to identify conservation actions and opportunity areas.
For some of our most endangered species, there is a need to conduct focused research to answer question that will inform management and decision-making. For example, we need to determine the long-term viability of extant populations of ornate box turtles via quantitative surveys, modeling, mark-recapture studies, and other appropriate scientific methods.
