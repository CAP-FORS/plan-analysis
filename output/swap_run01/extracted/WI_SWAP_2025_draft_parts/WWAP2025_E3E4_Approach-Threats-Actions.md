The nomenclature for the classification of threats
foot_3
 to SGCN and their habitats is based on the source of the impacts that a conservation target may be exposed to. They are the proximate activities or processes that impact SGCN or the habitat being assessed (e.g., unsustainable harvest practices). Threats lead to effects or stressors, which act upon the species, habitat, natural community or system (e.g., genetic erosion due to over harvesting). The source of the threat may be intimately mixed with and difficult to distinguish from its effects on the conservation target. Threats can be past (historical, unlikely to return or historical, likely to return), ongoing, and/or likely to occur in the future.
The nomenclature for the classification of actions includes direct and indirect responses to threats and their effects.
Because of the complexity inherent in describing each threat, one threat may be assigned to multiple corresponding CMP threat categories and multiple conservation action categories.

## E3-E4.2.2 Conservation Opportunity Areas

For the 2025 WWAP a team of NHC staff with diverse expertise in natural communities, invasive species and spatial databases was convened to discuss the scope of updates and improvements to COAs.
Initial discussions and interviews with other NHC staff yielded the following areas of improvement for COAs:
• The overall definition and characteristics of a COA can be made clearer and relevant to 2025. • It is important for users to readily access information that tells them why an area is a COA.
• It is important for users to receive some guidance or be aware of the most important actions to implement at a COA to benefit SGCN and their habitat. • There are different perspectives on the role of COAs, particularly boundaries and how rigorously COA boundaries should be defined. • Many more spatial datasets and software tools, including Midwest models, exist that can support local expertise and field verification to delimit biological and landscape characteristics that define COAs.
The scope for updating COAs for the 2025 WWAP included the following tasks.

## E3-E4.5 Results -Conservation Opportunity Areas

## E3-E4.5.1 Updated Definition

Conservation Opportunity Areas (COAs) are the best places in Wisconsin to implement conservation actions in the Wisconsin Wildlife Action Plan (WWAP) to protect and conserve SGCNs and their habitat. They are places on the landscape with significant ecological features, natural communities or SGCN habitats for which Wisconsin is responsible for protecting and conserving. Focusing conservation actions in these prioritized areas can increase the likelihood of long-term success, maximize effectiveness over larger landscapes, improve funding efficiency, and promote voluntary cooperative efforts across ownership boundaries.

## E3-E4.5.2 Updated COA Criteria

These criteria are the same as those used to define COAs for the 2005 WWAP and Implementation Plan. They have been updated with more current and concise language. Each COA fulfills one or more of these criteria.

## Table E3-E4.1 Criteria for Conservation Opportunity Areas

Conservation Opportunity Area Criteria Significant ecological features present. Landscape level groupings or combinations of Natural Community and physical characteristics that collectively exhibit distinct attributes. Recognition of these features focuses conservation not only on biological targets (species and natural communities) that may rearrange in response to a changing climate, but also on the physical factors that create diversity in the first place. Combinations of bio-and geodiverse areas. A significant ecological feature is unique to Wisconsin, or a relatively significant portion of the feature exists within the state.

## Conservation Opportunity Area Criteria

Presence of large populations and/or concentrations of SGCN. Sites with large populations of one or more SGCN, multiple SGCN, or high concentration of SGCN offer relatively more opportunity to benefit SGCNs. Even if populations or species diversity are declining, this assumes at least moderate viability such that conservation actions within the COA can help to sustain the species over time. While this is intended to limit single-species conservation, sites that support the few remaining populations of species, regardless of their viability should be considered. These characteristics can help to define the boundary of the COA. Presence of natural communities or habitats that support SGCN. Natural communities within the site are associated with known or probable SGCNs (e.g., based on association scores). Site quality contributes to the ecological function of a site and includes vegetation structure, composition and, for natural communities, the degree of hydrologic or soil disturbance. For restoration and enhancement to be feasible and sustainable (i.e., successful), natural communities exhibit low to moderate degradation, low to moderate environmental stressors. Identified as a priority conservation site in other initiatives or plans. Recognizing other initiatives or plans in sync with the definition and purpose of COAs, including relevant threats and actions, adds weight to their relevance as a conservation target. Other plans, maps or initiatives may overlap other COA criteria (e.g., priority natural communities). Contributes to a network of climate resilient cores and corridors. This criterion emphasizes how resilient the site is to climate change and whether it has features of landscape connectivity important for sustaining a climate resilient ecosystem. Resilient sites are more likely to maintain biological diversity and ecological function as climate change occurs. This includes sites that have potential for greater resiliency through additional management. These "biodiversity strongholds" have more variable topography and are more locally connected by natural land cover than other sites with similar soils/underlying bedrock. Such geodiverse sites have been correlated with high rare species richness and are useful tools for conserving both rare and common species. Sustaining biological diversity and ecological processes across the region are more likely if the resilient sites are embedded in a larger network of connected lands that allow for dispersal and movement between sites. Conservation efforts may be more successful at sites that are already moderately or highly resistant or resilient against climate change. Large, minimally fragmented, ecologically functioning system. This criterion focuses on the ability of a site to readily support and maintain ecological processes and a diverse community of organisms in the face of inputs and disruptions in addition to climate change, based on size and lack of fragmentation. Terrestrial ecosystems are complex combinations of plants, animals, soils, and other abiotic factors that provide critical ecological benefits, such as water quality improvement, flood control, carbon storage, climate regulation, aesthetic enjoyment, and biodiversity. Sites with moderate to high ecological integrity provide valuable information for assessing condition and conservation opportunity and effectiveness. The structure, composition, function, and connectivity of the ecosystem continue to operate within the bounds of natural disturbance regimes.

While the reasons are straightforward, the process and the criteria used to identify priority sites for conservation are complex, varied and controversial. When viewing the reports, differences in how COAs are defined and identified for the 2025 WWAP and other states or conservation plans should be acknowledged.
Wisconsin COAs are weighted toward public state-owned properties or sites where the WDNR has a partnership role. Naturally, we have the most information about these areas with respect to the criteria described in E3-4.5.2. More importantly, these sites are protected in perpetuity and as such are permanent reservoirs of biodiversity.
COA boundaries are deliberately imprecise because the criteria used to identify them are general and largely come from large-scale data layers; the boundaries are not field verified. Not all the area within a COA presents the same level of opportunity for successful conservation; conversely, opportunities exist outside COA boundaries.
The species lists for individual COA reports (see below) are based on occurrences confirmed within a five-mile radius of the COA boundary and probable occurrences. The latter is based on the SGCN-Natural Community and SGCN-Ecological Landscape association scores and represent "potential" for the site. Species associated with the natural communities present in the COA or the landscape in which the COA may not be present, or they could be established by implementing conservation actions.
COAs are areas that we know have habitat value and are likely to have successful outcomes. It may result in underrepresenting sites that are important but need restoration or protection to realize their ecological value to SGCN and their habitat (e.g., restorable wetlands under the wetland mitigation program). Sites that meet the criteria of a COA certainly exist on private lands or other conservation properties that are not accessible. The SGCN-Natural Community, SGCN-Ecological Landscape and Natural Community-Ecological Landscape scores help individuals and organizations to recognize characteristics of potential COAs on their properties.
