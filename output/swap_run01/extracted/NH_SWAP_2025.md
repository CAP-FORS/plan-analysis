## New Hampshire State Wildlife Action Plan

The 2025 New Hampshire State Wildlife Action Plan (NH SWAP) presents a fully updated strategy to conserve healthy wildlife populations across the Granite State. Developed through collaboration among biological experts and stakeholders, the plan incorporates the latest data to identify Species of Greatest Conservation Need (SGCN), key habitat types, major threats, and targeted conservation actions. Through its implementation, the New Hampshire Fish and Game Department and partners will restore and manage habitats, recover threatened and endangered species, and proactively prevent future declines-ensuring a vibrant future for New Hampshire's wildlife. Engaging a broad range of SWAP users is essential to making the 2025 NH SWAP relevant, accessible, and effective. The New Hampshire Fish and Game Department remains committed to strengthening long-standing partnerships while building new connections with communities and organizations statewide.
To support this goal, we implemented a targeted and inclusive outreach strategy during the 2025 revision process-featuring focus groups, an online survey, a public comment period, newsletter articles, social media outreach, and presentations at local events. The valuable feedback we received helped shape the NH SWAP's content, format, and future implementation, ensuring it reflects the needs and priorities of those it serves.
The 2025 NH SWAP identifies 138 wildlife species and 188 plant species as SGCN. All native wildlife and plant species were considered for inclusion on the 2025 SGCN list. Species were evaluated by biologists using a rigorous, data-driven methodology to determine those species most at risk-due to declining populations, vulnerability to threats, or potential future declines. The result is a compilation of species covering various taxonomic groups: mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish, insects, freshwater mussels, marine invertebrates, and plants. In addition, the plan includes a list of Assessment Need Species, which are those species requiring further data and information to determine and assess their conservation status in New Hampshire.
The 2025 NH SWAP identifies 28 key habitat types that span the state's diverse landscapes, from alpine peaks to coastal waters, and forests, wetlands, rivers, and streams in between. These habitats are vital to the survival of SGCN and the broader wildlife community. To support regional collaboration and large-scale conservation, New Hampshire's habitat classification system aligns with the Northeast Terrestrial and Aquatic Habitat Classification frameworks. ## Revision Process

Over the past 2 years, NHFG, along with its many partners, has undertaken the extensive process of updating our SWAP for the third submission to the US Fish and Wildlife Service. For this iteration, New Hampshire sought to make a plan that is accessible, supported by science, and widely available. We were guided by the fact that the successful revision and eventual implementation of the NH SWAP requires input from a wide array of audiences.
We identified the goals and expected outcomes that are needed to guide the revision and future implementation of the NH SWAP as follows:
1. Residents who are aware of New Hampshire's wildlife and plant species and their contribution to the environmental, economic, and social fabric of the state, and that actively support conservation efforts; 2. An informed network of partners actively prepared to engage in implementing key conservation actions that protect the state's wildlife and plant species; 3. A suite of conservation actions that consider biological, social, and economic factors, with specific guidelines to conserve the state's wildlife and plant species.
To assist in the 10-year update of the 2025 NH SWAP, NHFG again called upon those with a broad range of expertise in the state to work as collaborators. Since 2006, after the first NH SWAP submission, the Wildlife Action Plan Implementation Team (WAPIT), which is made up of biological and technical experts from NHFG and partners from NH Audubon (NHA), NH Natural Heritage Bureau (NHNHB), University of New Hampshire Cooperative Extension (UNHCE), and The Nature Conservancy (TNC), has worked to ensure that the conservation plans outlined in the SWAP are being prioritized and implemented by NHFG and its many partners. This team developed the approach to revising the NH SWAP and did most of the writing. WAPIT members also served as liaisons for collaboration with experts across the region-they shared updates on the Plan revision process with other biologists, requesting input on key components, including SGCN selection, threats assessments, and conservation action development. The broad range of biological expertise on WAPIT helped to ensure that all taxonomic groups are represented consistently within the NH SWAP. Even after Plan submission to the USFWS, WAPIT continues to meet monthly, collaborating on NH SWAP implementation goals and strategies, continually ensuring that the Plan is utilized to its full potential over the next 10 years. ## SGCN Selection

While the SGCN selection process is determined by each state, the Northeast Lexicon provides a regionally consistent definition of SGCN and State Assessment Priority Species (New Hampshire's "Assessment Need Species"). The Northeast Lexicon also provides recommendations for SGCN selection data sources and taxonomic standards. See Chapter 2 for details on New Hampshire's SGCN selection process.
Species of Greatest Conservation Need -Defined by each state fish and wildlife agency in its Wildlife Action Plan, typically a native species with declining populations or vulnerabilities expected to benefit from strategic conservation attention.
State Assessment Priority Species -Species for which more information is needed to fully understand status and trends in order to determine the level of conservation concern or SGCN status. ) and then amended by the Northeast Association of Fish and Wildlife Agencies Fish and Wildlife Diversity Technical Committee. Within the Lexicon, threats are categorized in a hierarchical structure, levels 1-3, with level 1 being the broadest category and level 3 being the most specific. Along with threat categorization, the Lexicon provides a system for defining threat characteristics, such as the spatial extent, severity, and immediacy of a particular threat. We used this framework in our threats identification and ranking processes (see Chapter 4).

## Actions

Like the threats classification, the action classification system within the Northeast Lexicon is based on the CMP version 2.0 (CMP, 2016), which was modified by the Quebec Department of Forestry, Wildlife, and Parks (Ministère des Forêts, de la Faune et des Parcs, 2021). This system is also hierarchical, with levels 0-3 being broad to specific, respectively. Level 0 action categories include "Active restoration and stress reduction measures, " "Encourage beneficial human behavior, " and "Create favorable conditions for conservation" 
(Crisfield & the Northeast Fish and Wildlife Diversity Technical Committee, 2022)
. In addition to naming conventions, the Northeast Lexicon provides descriptor categories for each action. This ensures that actions listed in a SWAP include all relevant information needed for implementation. New Hampshire adopted a subset of these descriptor categories.

## Chapter 4: Evaluating Threats to Species and Habitats

Chapter 4 addresses the threats that adversely affect wildlife and their habitats based on the expert opinions of professionals and published literature. We used a structured process based on the Northeast Lexicon Threat Classification system (as described above) to organize and label the threats identified for each SGCN and habitat.
In keeping with New Hampshire's tradition of teamwork, NHFG invited species and habitat experts from across the region to participate in the threats assessment process. WAPIT members identified and worked in partnership with these biological experts throughout the threats assessment process, gathering feedback on threat identification, descriptions, and ranking. This process is described in detail in Appendix E.
To the extent that expertise and information were available, the final threat ranks, as determined by the "threat characteristics" (spatial extent, severity, immediacy, certainty, and threat trends) were peer reviewed and cross referenced to scientific literature. The highest-ranking threats to species and habitats are discussed in the profiles (Appendix A and Appendix B).
In the comparative phase of the process, scores from all of the threat assessments were compiled in an Excel spreadsheet. This allowed us to analyze and compare the levels of risk among species/habitats and also among the broader threat categories. Through this process, we ensured that threats were categorized and ranked consistently across taxonomic groups and habitat types. This also enabled us to summarize threats in a consistent, standardized format that will be used to help prioritize actions for implementation.

## Planning for the Future

Once the 2025 NH SWAP is approved, the process of funding and implementation begins. The benefits of investing in the SWAP-or any wildlife conservation activities-go well beyond "saving" rare species. The economic benefits are clear: wildlife-associated recreation is a significant economic engine for New Hampshire. The 2022 National Survey of Fishing, Hunting, and Wildlife-Associated Recreation determined annual expenditures for all wildlife-associated recreation in New Hampshire to be nearly $5.2 billion. Fishing brought in an estimated $1.4 billion, hunting an estimated $580 million, and wildlife watching $3.3 billion (NORC, 2022). Outdoor recreation is a $2.7 billion industry in New Hampshire, employing over 28,000 workers in the state (New Hampshire Department of Natural and Cultural Resources, 2024). Any downturn in participation in these activities will have a negative impact on the state's economy, whereas efforts to improve wildlife and habitat in New Hampshire will likely bring more revenue into the system from hunters, anglers, and wildlife watchers.
The economic issue goes well beyond wildlife-associated recreation. New Hampshire's ecological framework is itself a hidden economy, untranslatable into dollars and cents. People live in and visit New Hampshire and spend money in the state in large part because it is a place of great natural beauty. The conversion of forest and other habitat into roads, houses, and businesses degrades the land's value to New Hampshire's wildlife, its residents, and its visitors. New Hampshire can support new people, and it can offer them places to live, work, and recreate. The NH SWAP helps accomplish this by pointing to where the most vulnerable species and habitats are in relation to the rapidly transforming landscape.
It starts with smart planning, which is at the heart of this plan's strategies. When people are able to clearly see the connections between good wildlife management, clean air and water, sustainable economic growth, and our quality of life, wildlife habitat conservation actions will naturally be brought to the forefront of planning decisions.
Through existing and new partnerships, NHFG is moving forward with implementing the Wildlife Action Plan. Prompt action is crucial-not only for the health and diversity of wildlife and habitats in the state, but also to ensure that future generations will have the opportunity to experience and enjoy the wild New Hampshire we love and appreciate today.

## Wildlife Habitats

The word "habitat" can be interpreted in many ways, even within the NH SWAP. Commonly, habitat either describes the specific needs of a particular species/guild or is a classification of vegetation or other features that occupy a particular portion of the landscape. In New Hampshire, 28 distinct habitats were identified, representing the range of landscapes our SGCN and other wildlife occupy-from the alpine habitats of the White Mountains, to the salt marshes, sandy beaches, and dunes of the New Hampshire coast. By taking both a species-level and landscape-scale perspective, we can ensure that all of New Hampshire's wildlife and plant species are encompassed in conservation planning.

## Integrating Habitats with Natural Communities and Systems

While the NH SWAP focuses on SGCN, it is intended to serve as a plan for all of New Hampshire's wildlife, both common and rare, including many species about which very little is known. Surveying for all wildlife species, including the thousands of invertebrates, is impossible. In order to find broad surrogates for all of these species, a classification of natural communities and ecological systems (hereafter, "systems") was developed. Natural communities are recurring assemblages of plants and animals found in particular physical envi ronments 
(Sperduto & Nichols, 2011)
; systems are groups of natural communities that repeatedly co-occur in the landscape and are linked by a common set of driving forces, such as landforms, flooding, soils, and nutrient regime 
(Sperduto, 2005)
.
The underlying assumption behind this approach is that, by identifying and protecting high-quality examples of all of New Hampshire's natural communities, all of the state's native wildlife species will have access to intact habitats. The systems of the NHNHB classification are roughly equivalent in scale to the ecological systems of the Northeastern Terrestrial Wildlife and Habitat Classification System 
(Gawler, 2008)
, which were used to create the NH SWAP habitat map. However, because they were developed with New Hampshire-specific data, NHNHB system descriptions tend to more accurately reflect vegetation types as they are encountered in the state. A crosswalk between the NH SWAP habitats, Northeast Terrestrial Wildlife and Habitat Classification ecological systems, and NHNHB systems can be found in Appendix C.

It is important to note the limitations of the species richness data. Information about the diversity of plants and an- imals in a given location is often limited. ## Landscape Context

Information about landscape context was calculated directly from the spatial arrangement among and between habitats. The spatial arrangement is a metric of how habitats occur across a landscape/area. It is an important metric used in understanding which places species can live and travel in and through. Examples of data include the total area of a habitat patch, the distance to the nearest patch of the same habitat type, similarity of neighboring habitat types, and other landscape features. Landscape context data variables provide us with information to de scribe the predicted interactions among habitat patches, such as the dispersal of wildlife and abundance of habitat in terms of area and intactness. Some of the landscape context data variables that were analyzed include:
1. Total area of the habitat 2. Wetland area 3. Distance to nearest similar habitat type 4. Similarity of neighboring habitat types Several regional geospatial condition assessments completed by TNC and the University of Massachusetts-Amherst (UMass) were also included as data variables under the landscape context habitat condition category. These include landscape complexity 
(Anderson et al., 2016)

## Species and Habitats Threats Assessment

## Abstract

Threats assessments were conducted for 28 habitats, 138 wildlife Species of Greatest Conservation Need (SGCN), and 188 plant SGCN using a standard methodology adopted by the Northeast states. Twelve different broad-level threat categories were ranked in terms of their potential impact on each species and habitat throughout New Hampshire. Vernal pools, beaches/dunes, Appalachian oak-pine forest, and pine barrens habitats had the highest number of high-ranking threats. Pollution was identified as the threat category that most frequently affects wildlife, followed by: Residential and Commercial Development; Climate Change; and Invasive and Problematic Species, Genes, and Diseases. Similarly, Pollution was the threat category that most frequently affects habitats in New Hampshire, followed by: Climate Change; Invasive and Problematic Species, Genes, and Diseases; Natural System Modifications; and Residential and Commercial Development. Invasive and Other Problematic Species, Genes, and Diseases was identified as the threat category that most frequently affects plants, followed by Natural System Modifications, Human Intrusions and Disturbance, and Residential and Commercial Development.

## Overview

New Hampshire's habitats and wildlife are affected by many challenging issues, rang ing from broad-scale threats such as a changing climate to local-scale threats such as undersized culverts blocking fish passage. Conservation and management programs depend on an objective assessment of the degree of risks posed to species and habitats of greatest concern.
Wildlife populations and habitats are exposed to enormous pressure from human population growth and recreational activities. Residential and commercial development is one of the most challenging issues for most of New Hampshire's wildlife and habitats. Many habitats are rapidly dis appearing or are fragmented by roads and dams, and many ecosystems are disrupted by human activities.
In addition to intact habitats, wildlife in New Hampshire requires clean air and water. Polluted runoff, industrial and agricultural chemicals, and noise pollution are among the factors negatively affecting a wide diversity of species and habitats, particularly aquatic species and environments. Moreover, interconnected with and exacerbating other threats is the increasing concern related to a changing climate.
This chapter addresses Element 3 of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Eight Required Elements for State Wildlife Action Plans (see Introduction), which requires that states identify, "the problems which may adversely affect Species of Greatest Conservation Need (SGCN) or their habitats, and priority research and surveys needed to identify factors which may assist in restoration and improved conservation of SGCN and their habitats. "

## Threat Classification System

State Wildlife Action Plans (SWAPs) must include descriptions of the threats adversely affecting SGCN and their habitats. Threats come from many different sources, and impacts can be observed at different spatial, temporal, and biological scales. As a result, the risk of the impacts is wide ranging, as are actions taken in response. New Hampshire used the Northeast Lexicon to classify threats, as was done in 2015. In addition to threats 1-11, there are also threats to species and habitats in the form of management challenges, such as data deficiencies, lack of resources, and species or habitat characteristics that may impede conservation success. These are classified under the Northeast Lexicon threat category Other Options, which includes the need for data collection, monitoring, funding, education and outreach, and new regulation and policy. While these are not threats in the typical sense of the term, these needs can be a key aspect of precluding a species or habitat from recovery. Virtually all SGCN and habitats have these needs, therefore these are primarily addressed in Chapter 5.
In addition to naming threats, understanding threat characteristics can help highlight opportunities for species and habitat management or protection. The Northeast Lexicon uses threat characteristics of spatial extent, severity, immediacy, threat trend, and certainty. Each threat identified for a species or habitat was further defined by ranking these threat characteristics in a standardized way. Using a standardized threat characterization system across the region will help states prioritize threats (and associated actions) for regional coordination.

## THREATS

Identifying a wide range of threats that are both intensifying as well as newly emerging is a key objective of the standardized threats assessment process used in New Hampshire and the other Northeast states. Addressing underlying causes or factors that pose a risk to wildlife, rather than waiting to manage dwindling populations or habitats after the fact, is a powerful and preemptive long-term approach, and more cost effective in the long run. Therefore, this should be considered when assessing low-ranking threats; these threats may be ranked as such due to the long-term nature of their impacts on species and habitats, lack of information surrounding the threat and its impacts, or the localized nature of a threat. Importantly though, proactive conservation planning and action will prevent future worsening of such threats; taking a preventive approach to conservation planning benefits many species and habitats.

## Threats Assessment Ranking Process

The species and habitats that showed signs of decline in New Hampshire were identified in Chapter 2; threats assessment methods were completed for each of these SGCN and New Hampshire habitats. A structured approach was developed, both to understand the most prevalent risk factors for these declines and to work toward their recovery. The first step in this process was to identify the threats (using the Northeast Lexicon Threat Classification System; Crisfield & the Northeast Fish and Wildlife Diversity Technical Committee, 2022) for each species and habitat. Next, species and habitat experts completed scoring forms that ranked five "threat characteristics"-spatial extent, severity, immediacy, threat trend, and certainty-for each known threat. The scores given were based on standardized criteria and were subject to a peer-review process. Evidence to support or refute scores was carefully evaluated by Wildlife Action Plan Implementation Team (WAPIT) biologists. Finally, scores were cross tabulated and summarized, resulting in a "final threat rank, " to clarify which sources pose the greatest threats to spe cies and habitats and which species and habitats are at greatest risk. Of the five threat characteristics, threat trend was not included in the calculation of the final threat rank for consistency with the 2015 threat ranking methodology, since threat trend is a new threat characteristic. The process allowed biologists to criti cally analyze the range of expert opinions and focus on critical problems. Numerous partners (e.g., government, NGOs, academic scientists, taxa or habitat experts) were contacted to complete the ranking process, drawing on professional experience and a review of published and unpublished sources (see Chapter 1).
The final threat rank is a planning and decision-making tool, not a true quantitative measure. The purpose of the ranking process was to provide a consistent basis for comparing threats across all species and habitats, and for placing those factors into categories of appropriate conservation action. The ranking process formed the basis for the threats assessment summaries presented in this chapter. Each step of the ranking process was clearly described and fully transparent, allowing the New Hampshire Fish and Game Department (NHFG) to assess and revise ranks as new information emerges. The frequency of overall threat ranks was tabulated by species, habitats, and threat categories (Northeast Lexicon levels 1-3).
The following process was used for all SGCN and habitat threats assessments. See Appendix E for more information.
Step One: Identifying Threats to Habitats and SGCN
Working from a list of standardized threat from the Northeast Lexicon (Appendix F), biological and technical experts compiled a list of all of the threats relevant to each SGCN and habitat. Threats evaluated for SGCN and habitats during the 2015 revision were provided as background, however, the Lexicon framework for threats and ranking methodology has been revised slightly since the last NH SWAP.
Step Two: Ranking Threats Threats were ranked using categorical criteria to assign scores (H = high, M = medium, L = low). Each threat assigned to an SGCN or habitat received four scores: spatial extent, severity, immediacy, and certainty. These are described briefly here and more fully in Appendix E.

## Frequency of Threat Categories and Action Prioritization

The resulting distribution of ranks (where high-ranking threats occur with the lowest frequency, while low-ranking threats are the most prevalent) allows for an appropriate prioritization of actions based on threat rank. That said, it is recognized that overall threat ranks are simply a tool for assessing risk and developing actions, and that threat ranks may change based on a variety of conditions. We present the frequency of high-, medium-, and low-ranking threats as a tool for assessing risk to habitats and species. Also, actions taken for a low-or medium-ranking threat may prevent that threat from becoming a high-ranking threat in the future. As such, developing actions for lower ranking threats is both appropriate and important.
Low-ranking threats are not necessarily a minimal or non-threat. As mentioned above, threats could be ranked low due to a variety of factors (see Appendix E for detailed ranking methodology). It is recognized that threat ranks will change over time as new information becomes available and that localized threats (which may not rank as high) warrant action, especially for threatened or endangered species.

## Change in Risk: NH Wildlife Action Plan 2015 to 2025

New Hampshire evaluated threats for habitats and species in both 2005 and 2015 using a systematic and repeatable approach. The threat-ranking methodology was similar between these years, with deviations mostly due to the adoption of a slightly new regional approach during 2015. In the 2025 Northeast Threats Lexicon (Crisfield & the Northeast Fish and Wildlife Diversity Technical Committee, 2022), however, threat classification and ranking methodology were updated to be consistent with the CMP classifications system (CMP, 2016). As such, a comparison of New Hampshire's threats assessments between 2015 and 2025 must be kept broad. Given this inconsistency in methodology, changes in threats at the species and habitat level are not necessarily indicative of definite change. See individual species and habitat profiles (Appendix A and Appendix B) for specific explanations of current threats assessments and trends. However, by making a high-level evaluation of the change in threats, we can begin to understand the success of the 2015 Plan over the last 10 years, and determine where threats have improved or worsened, on the whole.
In 2015, 10.5 percent, 32.2 percent, and 57.3 percent of threats ranked high, medium, and low, respectively. The 2025 threats assessment process resulted in 19.7 percent, 27.7 percent, and 52.6 percent of high-, medium-, and low-ranked threats, respectively, illustrating a slight increase in the percentage of high-ranking threats over the past 10 years. In terms of the most commonly identified threats across species and habitats, Pollution; Climate Change; Natural System Modifications; Invasive and Other Problematic Species, Genes, and Diseases; and Residential and Commercial Development were the top threat categories identified in 2015 (New Hampshire Fish and Game Department, 2015). For 2025, the most frequently identified threat categories were: Pollution; Invasive and Other Problematic Species, Genes, and Diseases; Climate Change; Residential and Commercial Development; and Natural System Modifications. The most frequently identified threats between 2015 and 2025 remain unchanged, apart from the order (based on their frequencies). In terms of threat categories with the most high-ranking threats identified (across species and habitats), there is similarity between the years as well. In 2015, Natural System Modifications; Residential and Commercial Development; and Invasive Species, Genes, and Diseases had the most high-ranking threats. In 2025, however, it is: Residential and Commercial Development, Natural System Modifications, and Climate Change. These results may indicate that Residential and Commercial Development and Natural System Modifications are two consistently prominent threats affecting New Hampshire's wildlife and habitats. This 10-year comparison is key to prioritizing conservation actions and adaptively planning for the future.

## Taking Action

Twelve major threat categories are evaluated and summarized in this chapter. Actions identified to address these threats are identified in Chapter 5 and within species and habitat profiles (Appendix A and Appendix B). Conservation actions developed for species and habitats focus predominantly on high-and medium-ranking threats. It is also recognized that some longer-term threats, such as climate change, require both research and actions in the near term. These research-based actions for low-ranking threats are critically important for the future protection of under-studied and newly vulnerable species.

The question then is, how can wildlife and habitat protection be balanced with the need for more housing in New Hampshire? The rural, natural environment of New Hampshire is a core part of the natural heritage of our state and an important driving force behind people moving here. ## Agriculture and Aquaculture

Agriculture and Aquaculture is defined by the Quebec Department of Forestry, Wildlife, and Parks (Ministère des Forêts, de la Faune et des Parcs, 2021) as:
Threats from agricultural activities, such as the expansion and intensification of agriculture and livestock farming, including silviculture, mariculture and aquaculture and related infrastructures. This includes the initial conversion of habitat (deforestation, filling/excavation, draining of wetlands, etc.) that is associated with cultivation or infrastructure development, as well as uses and practices (intensification of agricultural practices, use of machinery, etc.).
This category excludes the transport of the resources that are produced (see Transportation and Service Corridors), crop irrigation (see Natural System Modifications), or pollution (see Pollution).
Level 2 threats under this category identified in the New Hampshire Threats Assessment include the following:
2.1 Annual and Perennial Non-Timber Crops 2.2 Livestock and Poultry Farming 2.3 Marine and Freshwater Agriculture

## Threats Assessment Summary

A total of 51 threats were categorized under Agriculture and Aquaculture across SGCN (wildlife and plants) and habitats. Of these 51 threats, 10 ranked high, 10 ranked medium, and 31 ranked low. Thirty-six individual wildlife SGCN, 1 plant SGCN, and 5 habitat types identified Agriculture and Aquaculture as a threat.
Threats related to the timing and frequency of mowing are ranked high for grassland habitat and grassland species such as monarch, bobolink, and wood turtle. Mowing practices, such as haying before August 1, are in use throughout the state and are known to negatively affect grassland nesting birds. Hayfields are often mowed two to four times during the summer to provide high-quality forage for livestock. The peak nesting period for grassland nesting birds is mid-May through mid-July, coinciding with the first and second hay crops. Because of this, reproduction success is reduced through direct mortality of eggs and nestlings or subsequent egg and chick loss caused by nest abandonment or predation on exposed nests 
(Bollinger et al., 1990)
. Death by collision with mowing equipment is also a localized threat for some reptile and amphibian species. In addition, conversion of forested habitats to agriculture on the southern wintering grounds (South and Central America) of migratory birds is often a major if not the major threat to these species.
Without the work of farmers and landowners, however, important grassland habitat would succeed to forest, eliminating critical habitat for many wildlife species. The University of New Hampshire Cooperative Extension (UNHCE) provides multiple resources for managing these early-successional habitats with wildlife in mind; Managing Grasslands, Shrublands and Young Forests for Wildlife: A Guide for the Northeast and the Grassland Habitats Brochure are two examples.

## Transportation and Service Corridors

Transportation and Service Corridors are defined by the Quebec Department of Forestry, Wildlife, and Parks (Ministère des Forêts, de la Faune et des Parcs, 2021) as:
Threats from developing, using and maintaining transportation corridors 
(roads, pipelines, power lines, etc.)
 and their rights-of-way. These types of facilities may create obstacles or hinder the natural movement of species in addition to causing disturbances during maintenance (e.g., disturbance of falcon nests during bridge maintenance). This threat also includes vegetation control during rightsof-way maintenance and collisions with wildlife.
This category excludes contaminants associated with transportation systems (see 9.0 Pollution).
Level 2 threats under this category identified in the New Hampshire Threats Assessment include the following: This threat refers to the removal of biotic components of the environment for human consumption or benefit. The impacts of Biological Resource Use on species and habitats are often direct, physically removing individuals from the ecosystem. The exception to this is logging, which indirectly affects many species by removing, fragmenting, and otherwise altering habitat. Regardless, these removals have major impacts on the individual, population, community, and ecosystem levels. Under this threat category, the individuals removed are typically intended for human use or benefit, as opposed to the mortality of flora and fauna as a result of some other threat factor. These removals can be either intentional, where the species is the target, or unintentional, where the species is collected/affected incidentally along with a target species. Many of these forms of Biological Resource Use are regulated, but some species, especially invertebrates and amphibians, may lack formalized protections at state, national, or international levels. Other species are targeted despite legal prohibitions on their collection. For example, plant SGCN such as ram's-head lady's-slipper and American ginseng are targets of illegal collection, while eastern hognose snake, northern black racer, timber rattlesnake, and wood turtle are threatened by illegal collection, poaching, and persecution. Due to the human dimensions of this threat, coordinated actions, consistent messaging, and shared regulatory decisions are needed across the Northeast for successful management (Terwilliger Consulting, Inc. and the Northeast Fish and Wildlife Diversity Technical Committee, 2023).
Historically, many species in the Northeast were negatively affected by different forms of biological resource use.
Hunting for sustenance led to significant declines in many iconic mammals and birds in the region 
(Foster et al., 2002)
. Persecution of large predators and other "nuisance" species, including bounty systems, led to declines and regional extirpations 
(Foster et al., 2002)
. Historic overfishing contributed to population crashes and declines of many marine fish species and coastal ecosystems 
(Jackson et al., 2001)
. Logging and the collection of plant THREATS species altered habitat on large scales across the region, contributing to widespread species declines and shifting wildlife communities better suited to agricultural landscapes 
(Foster et al., 2002)
. Human use of biological resources has had a strong influence on the ecosystems in the Northeast (Terwilliger Consulting, Inc. & the Northeast Fish and Wildlife Diversity Technical Committee, 2023).

Air pollution can be from point and nonpoint sources. Perhaps the most impactful form of air pollution in the Northeast has been acid rain. Airborne pollutants from industrial and energy activities can mix with oxygen and water in the atmosphere to form sulfuric and nitric acids, which are then deposited through precipitation. These acids lower pH levels in water and soils, which can have severe impacts on a variety of wildlife species and habitats.
Another form of pollution is that of garbage and solid waste, of which, plastic has shown to have the greatest potential to harm the environment and wildlife due to its longevity, durability, and increasing volume accumulating in ecosystems (United States Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.). ## Other Options

The Other Options threat category was added to the Northeast Lexicon (Crisfield & the Northeast Fish and Wildlife Diversity Technical Committee, 2022) as a way for states to highlight the "threat" of "needs" related to species and habitat conservation, i.e., those situations in which the lack of something is a threat to the species' survival or habitat's preservation. When there are limited or inadequate data, monitoring, funding, education, or regulations and policy, species and habitats are at risk of disappearing before we are able to plan and implement the necessary conservation actions.

## Species Management

Species management includes the actions taken to influence interactions among wildlife, their habitats, and humans, with the goal of achieving objectives such as the protection of a specific species or habitat or enhancing biodiversity. This is accomplished through a science-based approach utilizing applications of wildlife, ecosystem, or habitat management and addressing threats such as poaching and controlling invasive species. Through management, specific actions are implemented to protect or enhance resources. Monitoring efforts are conducted to test the effectiveness of the management actions and refine management practices based on results (adaptive management) and to identify long-term needs and objectives.
By permanently securing intact forests, wetlands, riparian corridors, and other habitats, land conservation helps prevent habitat fragmentation and loss-key threats to wildlife across New Hampshire. This strategy is particularly vital for safeguarding the habitats of SGCN, many of which depend on specific habitats within connected landscapes. In the face of a changing climate, conserved lands enable species to adapt to shifting conditions and help maintain ecological connectivity. As development pressure increases, strategic land conservation can help to ensure that future generations of both people and wildlife benefit from New Hampshire's rich natural resources. • 3.1.3 -Support the improvement of existing conservation prioritization models, including NH SWAP maps, and the development of new prioritization models that incorporate wildlife and habitat data and information.

## Education, Outreach, and Engagement

Conservation education and outreach are powerful tools for inspiring public engagement and driving behavioral change through actions that benefit SGCN. Through effective strategic implementation, these efforts can increase awareness, build knowledge, improve ecological literacy, and empower individuals and communities to participate in meaningful conservation actions.
Public feedback gathered through surveys and an education-focused stakeholder group clearly demonstrated strong support for enhanced educational efforts to address the wide array of wildlife issues identified in the NH SWAP. Educators who participated in the focus group expressed a need for supplemental materials in alignment with the NH SWAP, allowing them to bring relevant, real-world conservation topics into their educational settings.
By reinforcing core concepts in ecology and wildlife habitat, educators lay the groundwork for exploring more indepth concepts and complex issues such as shifting habitats, recreational impacts, habitat connectivity, and invasive species. With a broad network of educators, these concepts can be integrated into existing programs and expanded through new curricula and educator training that directly support NH SWAP priorities, equipping educators with the tools and confidence to inspire meaningful action.
It is vital that outreach efforts meet the public where they live, recreate, and engage within their communities. By fostering a sense of place and connection to local wildlife, education initiatives can cultivate lasting conservation ethics within communities.
These strategies will be accomplished through the development and implementation of projects that include citizen science initiatives, hands-on nature-based learning experiences, and specifically targeted education resources. These efforts aim to engage people of all ages and empower them to take meaningful, informed actions that support wildlife conservation and improve conditions for SGCN statewide.

## Monitoring, Implementation, and Performance Evaluation

## Abstract

This chapter provides an overview of the means through which monitoring of Species of Greatest Conservation Need (SGCN), habitats, and the effectiveness of the identified conservation actions will be accomplished. Implementation of conservation actions and monitoring is completed by both the New Hampshire Fish and Game Department (NHFG) and our many partners across the region, as detailed below. Monitoring programs are ongoing for many taxa groups and habitats. Data collected as a part of these monitoring efforts provide valuable information on population status, trends, and habitat conditions, as well as insight into the efficacy of State Wildlife Action Plan (SWAP) conservation actions. By evaluating the results of NH SWAP implementation, we can identify alternative conservation actions, adjust hypotheses, and adapt our work based on our findings. A 5-year review is conducted to identify gaps in implementation and develop additional strategies for execution. A 10-year summary is also completed, including the percentage of actions implemented with a focus on high-priority actions, number of species assessed for federal listing and outcome, change in habitat acres, amount of conservation in priority areas, number of partners assisting with implementation, and the estimated amount of money brought in through federal grants and other funding sources.

NHFG and our many state, federal, and local partners use a variety of techniques and established programs to monitor changes in wildlife populations and habitats. ## Plants and Natural Communities

The NH Natural Heritage Bureau (NHNHB) conducts ongoing inventories for natural communities and plants. These data are tracked by NHNHB in their conservation database, which includes locations for wildlife species of conservation concern, rare plant species, and exemplary natural community systems in New Hampshire.

## Invertebrates

Targeted species monitoring is conducted for a number of SGCN invertebrates. The NHFG Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program and partners conduct targeted surveys for threatened or endangered invertebrates including but not limited to: dwarf wedgemussel, brook floater mussel, cobblestone tiger beetle, endemic alpine butterflies, and Karner blue butterfly. The NHFG Marine Division, along with the Great Bay National Estuarine Research Reserve (GBNERR), conducts ongoing monitoring programs for American lobsters, horseshoe crabs, oysters, and several other species of crabs around Great Bay and coastal New Hampshire.

## Birds

New Hampshire participates in both national (Breeding Bird Surveys [BBS] and Christmas Bird Counts [CBC]) and state-coordinated programs, as well as intense local surveys of birds. New Hampshire Bird Records/NH eBird (NHBR) is a state-reporting system and database that gathers bird-sighting records from volunteer observers and is primarily used to collect distribution information. Long-term intensive monitoring of occupied locations and population conditions has been conducted for several threatened and endangered species, including common loon, piping plover, least tern, peregrine falcon, common nighthawk, upland sandpiper, roseate and common terns, cliff swallow, and grasshopper sparrow. Habitat-based bird surveys have been conducted to varying extents for grassland, salt marsh, freshwater wetland, high elevation spruce fir, and floodplain forest habitats. Several game birds of conservation concern have been monitored annually, including drumming surveys for ruffed grouse and singing ground surveys for American woodcock. Additional data on bird species are collected from eBird, a global database managed by the Cornell Lab of Ornithology. Data are submitted by observers and vetted by ornithology experts to ensure data quality.

## Fish

Migratory fish species are monitored annually by NHFG, National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS), and U.S Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) staff during spring spawning runs. The Marine Division of NHFG has a number of ongoing monitoring programs as part of the multi-state management of marine fisheries administered by the Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission (ASMFC). Programs include a young-of-the-year American eel survey, a rainbow smelt spawning survey, and a juvenile finfish seine survey. The Marine Division also cooperates with the Maine Department of Marine Resources on the Inshore Trawl Survey, which has been monitoring marine fish populations in the Gulf of Maine since 2000. The Inland Fisheries Division of NHFG conducts surveys to assess the condition of fish in the state's streams, rivers, ponds, and lakes. The NHFG Fish Survey Database has over 7,000 records of fish survey data from 1983 to the present. Following the development of the first NH SWAP in 2005, biologists with the Fish Conservation Program initiated surveys to investigate the status of certain fish species of concern, including the bridle shiner, banded sunfish, redfin pickerel, swamp darter, and American brook lamprey. The Fish Conservation Program collaborates with the Marine Division on diadromous fish restoration monitoring efforts, including surveys for American eel and juvenile river herring. Surveys are conducted semi-annually by Inland Fisheries biologists to monitor the populations of recreationally fished species such as lake trout. Inland Fisheries biologists with the Fish Habitat Program also participate in the Eastern Brook Trout Joint Venture, a multi-state collaborative to assess, protect, and restore wild brook trout populations throughout their historic range.

## U.S Forest Service Forest Condition Data

The Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) Program provides the information needed to assess America's forests. FIA reports the status and trends in: forest area and location; size and health of trees; total tree growth, mortality, and removal by harvest; wood production and utilization rates of various products; and forest land ownership. Data are collected annually and include information on soils, understory vegetation, tree crown condition, coarse woody debris, and lichen community composition in a subsample of their plots.

## Water Quality Tracking

The NH Department of Environmental Services (NHDES) coordinates several water quality monitoring programs. Lakes and ponds are monitored via the Volunteer Lake Assessment Program (VLAP). Initiated in 1985, VLAP establishes a volunteer-driven lake sampling program to assist NHDES in evaluating lake water quality and provides volunteer monitors and lake residents with reports on lake health. This cooperative effort allows biologists and lake associations to make educated decisions regarding the future of New Hampshire's lakes and ponds.
The NHDES Surface Water Quality Assessment Program produces an integrated surface water quality document every 2 years. The Integrated Report describes the quality of New Hampshire's surface waters and an analysis of the extent to which all such waters provide for the protection and propagation of a balanced population of shellfish, fish, and wildlife, and allow recreational activities in and on the water.
The NHDES Wetlands Bureau tracks acreage of wetland fill, wetland creation/restoration, and wetland protection. The NHDES Wetlands Bureau is developing wetlands-specific water quality standards and a methodology for monitoring conditions. NHNHB has developed a wetland condition assessment protocol based on plant communities and ecological integrity (Ecological Integrity Assessment).

## Invasive Species

The mission of the Invasive Plant Atlas of New England (IPANE) is to create a comprehensive web-accessible database of invasive and potentially invasive plants in New England that will be continually updated by a network of professionals and trained volunteers. Similarly, there is a web-accessible database for aquatic invasive species (operated by the Northeast Aquatic Nuisance Species Panel) available.
The Early Detection and Distribution Mapping System (EDDMapS) is an online mapping tool for recording invasive species observations. EDDMapS allows users across the U.S. to contribute to a national network of invasive species distribution data, which can be used by researchers, conservation biologists, land managers, and any interested individuals. UNH Cooperative Extension provides instructions on how to participate in this monitoring effort. New Hampshire-specific invasive species information is available on the EDDMapS website.

## Estuarine Habitat Monitoring

GBNERR, a division of NHFG, supports and manages a variety of water-quality and habitat monitoring programs and is part of the National Estuarine Research Reserve System (NERRS) System-Wide Monitoring Program (SWMP). Some of these programs include: a partnership with UNH's Jackson Estuarine Laboratory, through which water quality and weather data are collected; a partnership with the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) to measure precision tidal data; and estuarine habitat monitoring, including saltmarsh habitat condition, mudflats, eelgrass, and oyster beds. GBNERR conducts a variety of estuarine habitat monitoring and research projects and provides valuable data on long-term habitat change and resources for habitat restoration and climate adaptation.

## Implementation

The 2025 NH SWAP will guide wildlife and habitat programs in the state until 2035. Implementation will involve department staff, other agencies, and the many partners who assisted with the Plan's revision. Partners who helped write, review, and edit the Plan have a good understanding of its components and have a vested interest in its successful implementation. Additional partners will be identified and engaged to assist with implementation of the conservation actions in the Plan. Having adequate resources to implement the Plan will be critical. Below is a summary of the planned implementation of the 2025 NH SWAP. Many components of the implementation process will likely span multiple years, with many being continually implemented over the 10-year period. However, the steps of implementation are broken out into 2-year increments below, in order to scaffold this multifaceted process.

## Years 1-2

The first step in NH SWAP implementation, following submission to USFWS, will be to incorporate the NH SWAP onto the NHFG Department website and the Taking Action for Wildlife (TAFW) website, in such a manner as to make the species and habitat profiles, risk assessments, maps, data, and actions easily discoverable by anyone wishing to help implement the elements of the SWAP. Species and habitats whose conservation requires regional or national participation will be highlighted to attract a broader coalition for implementation. This includes working with other states on ways to provide this information in a regional context.
Data on SGCN, habitats, threats, and conservation actions from the updated 2025 NH SWAP will be entered in the Northeast SWAP database. This will allow the 14 Northeast states and the District of Columbia to compile key SWAP information and identify region-wide patterns and priorities regarding SGCN, habitats, threats, and conservation actions, further supporting and maintaining Northeast landscape-scale conservation efforts.
The TAFW Program, a technical assistance program developed in partnership with UNH Cooperative Extension, will serve as the primary distribution network for implementation at the landowner, town, and regional scale, including maps of habitats and habitat quality rankings. The TAFW website will be updated to reflect changes in the Plan, and additional tools to help implement the Plan will be developed.
A principal need is the prioritization of NH SWAP actions. This task will be aided by the threats assessment scores (Chapter 4, Appendix A, and Appendix B) and input of the Wildlife Action Plan Implementation Team (WAPIT) and other partners. Further, agencies and organizations need to be identified that have the interest and ability to take on leadership roles in the more complex interagency policy actions. NHFG will be a leader on many strategies, but others may lend their leadership as ap propriate. Specific coordinators should be established for the implementation and monitoring of conserva tion strategies. Contact with state and federal agencies will be made to integrate the priorities and strategies of the SWAP into their plans and operations, including State Forest Action Plans, Landscape Conservation Cooperative projects, Climate Center projects, and many others.

The Tracking and Reporting Actions for the Conservation of Species (TRACS) system will be used as the primary tool for monitoring NHFG implementation of NH SWAP conservation actions that involve grants from the USFWS. Information about SWAP efforts led by other agencies and organizations will be acquired through direct contacts from WAPIT members. WAPIT and TAFW teams will meet monthly and quarterly, respectively, to regularly update information and keep the adaptive management process moving forward (see Performance Evaluation, below). [FIGURE: the goal of gathering the New Hampshire public's views and opinions on the current and anticipated future condition of wildlife and habitats in the state, the University of New Hampshire Survey Center conducted a survey on behalf of NHFG, the Carsey School, and UNHCE. The survey sought feedback from stakeholders and interested individuals in New Hampshire about protecting wildlife and habitats, input on ways NHFG can better share the NH SWAP with the public in the future, and ways the NH SWAP can be improved. The link to the online survey was distributed by NHFG, the Carsey School, and UNHCE via social media posts, newsletters, and email blasts. One thousand, two hundred fifty-six (1,256) respondents completed the survey between October 21 and December 2, 2024. Respondents were spread out relatively evenly by county, and there was at least one respondent from 212 of New Hampshire's 234 towns and cities (Figure1-1). More than half of the respondents said they are landowners or interested individuals, while three in ten considered themselves to be volunteers, and nearly one in four were local board or committee members (Figure1-2).]

## Threats Assessment Summary

Transportation and Service Corridors were identified as a threat for 54 individual wildlife SGCN, 10 individual plant SGCN, and 21 of 28 habitat types. There was a total of 110 threats under this category, and of these, 19 ranked high, 45 ranked medium, and 46 ranked low.
This threat includes the construction, use, and maintenance of roads, railroads, utility and service line rights-of-way (ROWs), and shipping lanes. Along with habitat loss and degradation, a key component of this threat is the resulting fragmentation of habitats, leading to increased mortality from vehicle strikes and isolated populations. As the human population in New Hampshire continues to increase (see Residential and Commercial Development section), so too does increasing development, road density, road widening, and higher traffic volume of effected areas.
There are many resources that have been developed to help land use planners, land trusts, and municipalities develop roads and protect habitat in ways that emphasize connectedness between landscapes and wildlife populations and minimize habitat fragmentation, isolated populations, and wildlife road-crossing mortalities.

## Conservation Actions

## Introduction

There are hundreds of conservation actions that have been identified for individual wildlife species, habitat types, and plants throughout the development of the 2025 NH State Wildlife Action Plan (NH SWAP) (see Appendix A and Appendix B for individual species and habitat profiles). These specific conservation actions are necessary for targeted recovery of the species that are most imperiled, as well as the broad suite of habitats occurring in the state. However, in order to successfully protect the state's biodiversity, we must also develop and implement broader conservation actions which help protect our ecosystems and communities of plants and wildlife at a statewide and landscape scale.
To develop holistic, broad-scale conservation actions, we created six broad conservation action "themes, " which arose out of expert assessment and our public outreach and stakeholder engagement efforts (see Chapter 1). The six themes are:
1. Habitat Restoration and Management 2. Species Management 3. Land Conservation 4. Land Use Planning 5. Education, Outreach, and Engagement 6. Policy and Governance
In this chapter, each theme includes an overview of its significance to implementing the NH SWAP. This is followed by associated goals and the strategies needed to achieve them. Strategies are crafted to clearly outline how goals can be put into action, while remaining flexible enough to accommodate the diverse roles that agencies, organizations, and the public may play in achieving them (NHDNCR, 2020). These strategies are not intended to represent all possible conservation actions but are instead designed to remain relevant over the next 10 years, allowing for adaptive conservation planning as conditions and priorities evolve.
We also aim to present these goals and strategies in a way that enables all Granite Staters to see where they can contribute and play a role in the NH SWAP's success. These strategies serve not only as a conservation roadmap for the New Hampshire Fish and Game Department (NHFG) over the next decade, but they represent the priorities that we've identified for which our partners can lead the way, take action, and drive meaningful change. The successful implementation of these strategies depends on partner and stakeholder support, additional funding, adaptive management, and monitoring of our efforts.
To develop these goals and strategies, we compiled recommended conservation actions from a variety of sources including the 2015 NH SWAP (Chapter 5), recommended conservation actions from various Northeast regional committees (e.g., Northeast Fish and Wildlife Diversity Technical Committee, Northeast Association of Fish and Wildlife Agencies Disease Coordinator), and NH SWAP revision focus groups and public web survey input (Chapter 1). Goals and strategies were formatted by the NH Wildlife Action Plan Implementation Team (WAPIT) to ensure they are actionable and to allow for periodic performance evaluation. ## Habitat Restoration and Management

Habitat restoration and management are needed to improve the condition of habitats and include terrestrial, aquatic, and coastal habitats. Effective management and restoration efforts can mitigate the damage to habitats resulting from development, habitat alteration, and disrupted natural processes. Initiatives could include programs such as backyard landscaping to improve wildlife habitat, replacing culverts to restore stream flow and wildlife passage, managing grasslands to improve benefits to target wildlife, creating and maintaining early-successional stages of vegetation, and allowing late-successional conditions to develop on selected tracts of forest. Habitat management involves federal, state, non-governmental organizations, towns, and private landowners.

## Policy and Governance

The NH Fish and Game Department is the primary state agency with the authority and mission to conserve the state's wildlife resources. The NH Department of Natural and Cultural Resources (DNCR) Natural Heritage Bureau's (NHN-HB) mission is to determine protection and conservation measures and requirements necessary for the survival of native plant species in the state, to investigate the condition and degree of rarity of plant species, and to distribute information regarding the condition and protection of these species and their habitats. State and federal governments and agencies must work with each other and with other non-governmental organizations to maintain and enhance healthy wildlife populations and their habitats and maintain the state's economic vitality, which is dependent on these resources. Improved coordination among agencies removes obstacles and creates opportunities to maintain and restore wildlife health and establishes a more transparent process for the public.

## Mammals

Bat hibernacula surveys, acoustic monitoring, and volunteer counts are conducted regularly across the state. Targeted population surveys have been conducted on some SGCN, including Canada lynx, New England cottontail, and northern bog lemming. NH Rabbit Reports, a citizen science project, has had over 5,000 records added.
NHFG manages populations of big game species (i.e., white-tailed deer, black bear, moose, and turkey) in accordance with a Big Game Management Plan through the annual collection of biological and hunter survey data (i.e., species observations and hunter effort). Collectively, these data are used to maintain indices that track species abundance and population change over time. The Department is working toward establishing long-term management plans for select small game and furbearer species. Since 2022, NHFG has contracted with the University of New Hampshire (UNH) to utilize a statewide array of remote cameras to monitor moose and furbearer populations. Given the success of this methodology, NHFG is in the process of extending this work and incorporating this technique as part of routine moose and furbearer management. In time, these camera arrays may be used to monitor other species.

## NH Wildlife Sightings Reporting Website

A data collection tool, New Hampshire Wildlife Sightings (NHWS), was developed in cooperation with a number of government and non-government entities. NHWS is a website for the collection of species occurrence data. Reptiles and amphibians have been a primary focus of reporting, but all taxonomic groups are potentially reportable through the website. Web hosting for NHWS is provided by the UNH Complex Systems Research Center. Staff within the Wildlife Division at NHFG perform quality control of all data. After quality control is complete, data are forwarded to NHNHB to be incorporated into the rare wildlife, plant, and natural community database. Development of an updated database following the 2025 NH SWAP revision, with enhanced usability and accessibility, is necessary to continue this important work.

## Monitoring Habitat Quality Conservation Lands

The New Hampshire conservation lands GIS data layer from NH GRANIT, New Hampshire's statewide GIS clearinghouse, is updated twice a year with data from NHFG. These data provide information on land ownership in the state and are useful for monitoring habitat conservation efforts.
