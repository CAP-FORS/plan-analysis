For thousands of years, Indigenous peoples have lived along the Great Lakes, using these forested shorelines for food, shelter, and cultural practices. Today, any efforts to protect and manage Michigan's boreal forests should be done in close partnership with Indigenous communities, who have deep knowledge of and long-standing relationships with these lands and waters.
What is the health of boreal forests?
Boreal forests are uncommon features of the northern Great Lakes region, occurring sporadically in the northern Lower Peninsula and infrequently in the Upper Peninsula. Analysis of General Land Office survey notes in Michigan reveals that boreal forest once occupied approximately 385,000 hectares (955,000 acres; 
Comer et al. 1995)
. Recent estimates of boreal forest in Michigan reveal a significant decrease in extent with approximations for balsam fir and white spruce forests of about 88,000 ha (215,000 ac; MIRIS 1978) and more general estimations of mixed upland conifer and other upland conifer forest of approximately 230,000 ha (575,000 ac; 
Michigan DNR 2001a
, 2001b)
. Twenty-five high-quality occurrences of boreal forest, which constitute about 1,242 ha (3,000 ac) have been documented by Michigan Natural Features Inventory in Michigan's Natural Heritage Database 
(MNFI 2025)
. Turn-of-the-century logging of Northern white-cedar and other conifers from boreal forests and widespread fires following the harvests favored the conversion of mature and old-growth boreal forest to early-successional forest dominated by deciduous species (e.g., trembling aspen and paper birch; Campbell and Campbell 2000, 
Reich et al. 2001
, Frelich 2002)
. Where conifer trees were eliminated or drastically reduced, hardwoods have persisted 
(Bergeron et al. 2004b
). Many inland sites formerly dominated by boreal forest and occurring on moist to wet lacustrine soils were drained and converted to agriculture, especially in Chippewa County.
To conserve biodiversity in these systems, and when feasible, large tracts of boreal forest should remain unharvested to allow natural disturbances, such as fire when appropriate, windthrow, insect outbreaks and lake-level fluctuations, to function stochastically and generate diverse successional stages. Dead and dying wood should be retained to form snags, stumps and logs, enhancing structural complexity and habitat value 
(Morisette et al. 2002
, Harper et al. 2005)
.
Helpful resources for identifying and understanding Michigan's natural communities include Michigan Natural Features Inventory's natural community classification 
(Cohen et al. 2015
, Cohen et al. 2025
) and natural community abstracts, both of which are available online along with photos, comprehensive descriptions, distribution maps, and thorough references from the scientific literature. MNFI's most detailed bibliography on Michigan's natural communities can be found in the publication "Distribution Maps of Michigan's Natural Communities" 
(Albert et al. 2008)
, which is also available for viewing and downloading at the MNFI website.

## Papaipema aweme

## Special Concern

These medium sized moths have light wood brown forewings and contrasting dark brown spots and a sinuous dark postmedial line 
(Covell 1984)

## Special Concern

The black-backed woodpecker is a medium sized, three-toed woodpecker with a solid black back and white underparts. Adults of both sexes possess a distinct white stripe that extends from the bill to the nape and males possess a prominent and unmistakable yellow crown patch

## Iris lacustris

## Federally and State Threatened

Dwarf lake iris is a blue-violet (rarely white), perennial flowering plant less than 6 inches tall. It reproduces primarily by cordlike, yellow-green rhizomes that are usually less than 0.2 inches thick and grow within a few inches of the ground's surface. Dwarf lake iris is endemic to the Great Lakes region, found only within a few miles of Lake Michigan and Lake Huron in Michigan and Wisconsin, USA and Ontario, CA, in boreal forest, mesic forest, fens, glades and several shoreline communities. ## Lepus americanus

Snowshoe hares are medium-sized lagomorphs (3 -5 lb) that are an important prey item for many predators within boreal food webs 
(Burt 1948)
. Specifically adapted to winter landscapes, their namesake large hind feet allow efficient travel in deep snow and hares undergo coat color molts (white-to-brown in the spring and back to white in the fall) to maintain camouflage in seasonally snowy environments. Snowshoe hares may be found in a wide range of habitats but prefer sites with dense understories, which may be represented by low-lying conifers (e.g., white-cedar swamps (Thuja occidentalis)) within the boreal core of their range, but hares often use structurally similar habitats such as young aspen (Populus spp.) along southern portions of its range. Snowshoe hares reach their southern distributional limit in Michigan and this southern range boundary has shifted northwards in recent years 
(Burt et al. 2017
). This species is not tracked in the state's Natural Heritage Database.

• Establish and maintain suitable habitat across current distribution. [PSH; IFGM]
• Better understand impacts of forest management on extant populations.

## Protecting boreal forest refugia

Boreal forests in the region represent the southern edge of a cold-adapted ecosystem, supporting species that rely on persistent snow cover, cooler temperatures and high moisture levels. These forests and their associated wildlife are particularly vulnerable to changing weather impacts such as warming temperatures, increased drought and diminished snowpack. The loss of consistent winter conditions poses a significant threat to the long-term viability of boreal-dependent species. However, in the near term, lake-effect weather patterns along the north coast of Lake Michigan continue to provide cold, snowy refugia that help sustain boreal conditions. Protecting and managing these climate refuges will be essential for maintaining biodiversity and ecological function in boreal forest systems.
