## Chapter 1: Introduction

In 2001, the Wildlife Conservation and Restoration Act was passed by the U.S. Congress and signed into law, initiating the State Wildlife Grant (SWG) program. The primary goal of the federal SWG program is to prevent additional wildlife species from being federally listed as threatened or endangered by implementing conservation actions before the species becomes critically imperiled. The SWG program is administered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS), which disburses annual Congressional funding allocations by formula to states and territories. To receive SWG funding, states were required to complete a Comprehensive Wildlife Conservation Strategy (CWCS). The CWCS developed a list of Species of Greatest Conservation Need (SGCN), assessed threats to SGCN and their habitats, and described conservation strategies, monitoring plans, and public outreach efforts. The New York CWCS was approved on April 21, 2006.
To remain eligible for SWG funding, states are required to update their conservation plans at least every 10 years in a State Wildlife Action Plan (SWAP). The SWAP is a conservation plan for rare, declining, or at-risk species, with the goals of identifying important actions that will conserve wildlife diversity, improving coordination of state agencies and partners in conservation, and informing and engaging the public in biodiversity conservation. The last New York SWAP (hereafter "2015 SWAP") was approved by USFWS in April 2016. This SWAP is a revision of, and largely follows the structure of, the 2015 SWAP.
Although required by Federal law to remain eligible for SWG funding, this plan is designed to help New York conserve its rich and valuable biodiversity.
Maintaining a current SWAP is crucial for continued funding opportunities that support much of the wildlife conservation work that DEC engages in. Over the last ten years, DEC has implemented many SWGfunded projects to monitor, restore, manage and conserve SGCN and their habitats. The SWG program funds the coordination, administration and implementation of DEC's work on amphibians, reptiles, terrestrial and aquatic invertebrates, as well as rare fish and some marine species.

## Conservation Planning in New York

Conservation plans have been developed for numerous species and geographic areas in New York. The USFWS, DEC, and partners have developed conservation and recovery plans for more than 25 species, and work continues to plan and implement conservation at various levels. Conservation and management plans have also been developed for many geographic areas of New York. Watershed-based plans have been developed for all major watersheds in the state, often including an interstate and international steering committee to coordinate conservation with neighboring jurisdictions. The watershed plans identify important natural resources, conservation threats, and recommended actions within the watershed, and most have a sub-section specific to biodiversity conservation. Collectively, these species and watershed plans provide a solid foundation for comprehensive conservation planning at the landscape level.
In December 2022, New York enacted legislation to conserve 30% of state land and waters by 2030. In July 2024, DEC and NY Office of Parks, Recreation and Historic Preservation released the New York State 30x30 Draft Strategies and Methodology. Objectives include conserving lands prioritized in the Open Space Conservation Plan, and preserving biodiversity through habitat conservation and restoration, providing a means for wildlife migration. Currently, 22% of New York State's lands and waters are considered conserved. Once finalized, the approaches described in this document will provide a path towards achieving the 30x30 goal, conserving additional lands and waters, and benefiting SGCN across the state.

## Northeast Regional Conservation

The close proximity of many northeastern states (Virginia to Maine, including DC) has engendered a culture of cooperative and/or complementary management approaches amongst the region's fish and wildlife agencies. The Northeast Association of Fish and Wildlife Agencies (NEAFWA) traditionally has supported a strong technical committee structure to further wildlife conservation. Technical committees are species-or habitat-focused groups that exchange ideas and develop common approaches to wildlife issues. Typically, these conservation actions are implemented by individual states using their own funds; in some cases, however, additional funding has been made available through the directors of state fish and wildlife agencies in the Northeast.
In one such case, the Directors established the Regional Conservation Needs (RCN) Program, including the designation of Regional Species of Greatest Conservation Need (RSGCN), which is managed by the Northeast Fish and Wildlife Diversity Technical Committee. The RCN Program formalizes a cooperative approach to address SGCN and RSGCN needs across multiple states. The purpose of the RCN Program is to develop, coordinate, and implement conservation actions for issues, threats, and opportunities that are most effectively addressed at the regional and multi-state scale, building upon the many regional initiatives that already exist.
The RCN Program utilizes a funding mechanism equitable to all Northeast states and the District of Columbia, creating a base of funding for regional projects. A small percentage of each state's annual State Wildlife Grant allocation is used to address SGCN and RSGCN needs across multiple states. Since 2007, 59 different projects have been, or currently are, supported through this program. The resulting reports and products can be found at 
https://northeastwildlifediversity.org
.
Output measures related to the RCN Program include monitoring the number of conservation actions and research projects selected by the participating agencies, the number of projects completed by the funding recipients, and the number of articles, publications, and technical reports developed each year as a result of funded projects. New York will continue to participate in this and other regional-, watershed-, and landscape-scale cooperative approaches to address SGCN, RSGCN, and habitat conservation needs as appropriate.
New York's SWAP is designed to roll up to a northeast regional level, with standardized terminology for conservation threats and actions, as well as compatible habitat classifications. This enables the SWAP to describe the aspects of conservation which are unique to New York, while facilitating conservation at a broader regional level.

## National and International Context

The SWAP is written as a document for biodiversity conservation within New York State but recognizes that many SGCN cross interstate and international boundaries, either as part of their natural range, annual migrations, or as victims of illegal trafficking of wildlife. While the recommended conservation actions describe work within New York only, there will continue to be a need for inter-jurisdictional coordination of conservation efforts. Nationally, government and non-profit groups such as the Atlantic Flyway Council, American Fisheries Society, and Partners in Amphibian and Reptile Conservation help coordinate conservation and management actions for many species across larger regions of the country. The foundation set by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, and built upon by the Neotropical Migratory Bird Conservation Act, provides a model for international cooperation in conservation. The SWAP recognizes that these types of large-scale cooperative initiatives are essential to the conservation of some SGCN.

## Chapter 3: Identifying Species of Greatest Conservation Need

In evaluating wildlife SGCN, all mammals, fish, birds, amphibians, and reptiles present in New York were considered, with selected invertebrates, mollusks, crustaceans, and arthropods also reviewed. The process to revise the SGCN list in New York involved a comprehensive review of the status of each species. The first step was to determine the current status of the 479 species designated as SGCN in the 2015 SWAP, plus an additional almost 440 species nominated for evaluation by DEC staff, species experts, and the public. This information was incorporated into species assessments, which were drafted by DEC staff in the Division of Fish and Wildlife (DFW), Division of Marine Resources (DMR) and the New York Natural Heritage Program (NYNHP). The species assessments briefly describe life history, protected status, current status and trends in abundance and distribution, percent of New York where the species occurs, relative importance of the New York population to the North American population, and threats to each species.
This SWAP also includes plant SGCN for the first time. The first step in identifying plant SGCN was to evaluate the 509 plant species listed as of concern (S1 thorough S3) on the 2024 NYNHP Rare Plant Status Lists (
https://www.nynhp.org/rare-plant-lists/
). Mosses, lichen, and fungi were not included in this iteration. Vascular plants ranked as S1 or S1S2 which have been documented as extant in the state in the last twenty-five years were included as SGCN, along with a small number of plants ranked S2. State rankings are reviewed each year by NYNHP botanists, in collaboration with experts from across the state. Plant species status assessments are available at 
https://www.nynhp.org/plant-sgcn/
.
While plant species are not currently eligible for SWG funding, a proposed federal law, the Recovering America's Wildlife Act, would add funding for plants identified as SGCN if it passes in its current form. ## Category Definitions

## High-Priority Species of Greatest Conservation Need (HPSGCN)

These species may be listed as endangered, threatened, or special concern in New York, be habitat specialists, and/or have a restricted range in the state. The status of these species is known, and they have an established breeding, wintering, or migratory population in New York, and they have experienced a population decline in New York. These species have identified threats that put them in jeopardy, and they require timely management intervention to reverse or slow population declines and lead to recovery.

Without the implementation of conservation actions over the next 10 years, they are likely to reach critically low population levels in New York.

## Species of Greatest Conservation Need (SGCN)

These species may be listed as special concern in New York or not be currently state listed. The status of these species may either be known, and they have an established breeding, wintering, or migratory population in New York; or the status may be poorly understood, but they are suspected to have a breeding, wintering, or migratory population in New York. These species may have experienced historic population declines that are now stabilizing or on a positive trajectory; they may be declining or beginning to decline in the state; or there are features of their life history that make them particularly vulnerable to threats. There are one or more identified threats to these species, and there is uncertainty regarding their long-term viability or persistence in New York. These species would either benefit from the implementation of conservation actions to maintain stable populations levels or sustain recovery in New York; or research is needed to determine their actual status, trends, or specific management actions that could lead to maintenance of their populations in New York. ## Non-SGCN

Non-SGCN include species that were considered because they were designated as SGCN in the 2015 SWAP or nominated for evaluation as SGCN for the 2025 SWAP, but who are not known to require any immediate management intervention to maintain their current populations within New York State. This includes species for which current data are lacking, insufficient, not fully evaluated, or otherwise do not support SGCN status; species now thought to never have been present in New York or only rarely observed in the state; and extirpated species.
Extirpated species, while not extinct from the entirety of their range, no longer occur in a wild state within New York. These species may occasionally be documented in the state, but they do not exhibit patterns of traditional use for that species (i.e., there are no known breeding, wintering, or regular migratory populations in New York State).

## Changes to SGCN List

The total number of SGCN remained roughly the same in several taxon, with some increases or decreases in a few groups. No marine species were added or removed. Species from three taxonomic groups not represented in the 2015 SWAP were added (caddisflies, flies, and plants), as well as several new terrestrial invertebrate species in previously represented taxa. Several species that were kept on the list were moved into or out of the HPSGCN category. Fifty species, primarily birds, were removed from the SGCN list for a variety of reasons. Many species that were SGCN in 2015 now have recovered or stable populations, including red-shouldered hawk, common loon and most of the waterfowl species.
Others do not occur in New York or are only rarely observed, such as Cory's shearwater and little gull. Some are considered functionally extirpated from New York, meaning they no longer regularly breed or winter in the state, including loggerhead shrike and northern bobwhite. 

## EXTIRPATED LARGE CARNIVORES

The 2005 CWCS included wolves, cougars, and lynx as SGCN and recommended studies to evaluate the feasibility of reintroducing these extirpated species to New York. In writing the 2015 SWAP and this SWAP, DEC chose not to include extirpated large carnivore species as SGCN, so as to focus conservation resources on resident species that currently have a population in New York and are at risk of declining.
Proponents of restoration have noted research showing that loss of large predators can cause alterations of ecology at lower trophic levels, such as overabundant deer populations in some areas impacting forest regeneration. They have also provided economic justifications for restoring large carnivores, such as reducing the number of deer-vehicle collisions and increased opportunities for eco-tourism. Conversely, there is opposition to reintroducing large carnivores from people concerned about livestock depredation, possible impacts to game species, and the potential for attacks on humans.
Social surveys show there is some public support for large carnivores recolonizing New York through natural range expansion but less support for projects to reintroduce these species directly. The SWAP recommends actions to foster regional habitat connectivity, which could facilitate natural recolonization of New York by these extirpated large carnivores as conditions permit. Not including extirpated large carnivores on the SGCN list means that restoring them is not a conservation priority for DEC, but it does not prevent conservation partners from engaging in projects related to assessing the feasibility of restoration in New York. 

## Data Deficient Species

Updating the list of SGCN involved assessing the status of almost 900 species, including trends in abundance and distribution in New York and neighboring states and provinces. These data were used to identify SGCN and to designate high-priority SGCN. Population trend information was not available for some species because they have not been studied historically, are difficult to monitor, or are inherently rare. Because it was not possible to assess their actual status, the 2015 SWAP had identified such species as Species of Potential Conservation Need (SPCN). The SPCN category has been eliminated, and the definition of SGCN broadened to include data-deficient species. Species that were categorized as SPCN in the 2015 SWAP were either removed from the list or are now labeled SGCN or high priority SGCN.
While a few species are relatively well-studied in the state, comprehensive information on the status and distribution of many, including most invertebrate taxa, is lacking. There has never been a statewide survey of Lepidoptera species (butterflies and moths), and this group constituted the biggest gap in the Empire State Native Pollinator Survey 
(White et al 2022)
. Statewide surveys are also lacking for other understudied groups including fireflies, terrestrial snails, fairy shrimp and saproxylic and fungusdependent insects (species dependent on mature forests that require deadwood and robust fungi, particularly during larval stages, such as syrphid flies and some beetles). While a survey of Odonate species (dragonflies and damselflies) survey was initiated 20 years ago, updated information is needed to guide species conservation and habitat management. Other data-deficient species groups include small mammals, most salamanders, and freshwater mussels.
One conservation concern that impacts species from multiple taxa and has broad economic and ecological implications is the decline in pollinator species. Pollination is a symbiotic relationship between plants and animals where the animal receives food in the form of nectar or pollen, and the plant receives transfer of reproductive cells between individuals. Pollination is essential to the reproduction of roughly 75 percent of flowering plants worldwide. Declines in the populations of some pollinator species have been documented and traced to habitat fragmentation and loss, chemical pollutants such as pesticides and herbicides, and changes in the timing of plants' flowering relative to presence of pollinators on the landscape (e.g., plants flowering earlier or later than normal, or shifts in insect active periods that no longer align with normal flowering times) (NYSDEC and NYSAGM 2016). ## HABITAT MANAGEMENT AND RESTORATION

Habitat management and restoration is fundamental to achieving many wildlife conservation goals. Managing habitats most often involves manipulation of vegetative cover, either by removing invasive species or controlling the natural process of succession. In areas of New York State where the climax vegetative cover is forest, grassland habitat management involves periodic mowing and/or burning to prevent the growth of woody vegetation and invasive species. Forest management is more complex, with multiple types of forest and successional stages to be considered. Some wildlife species prefer mature forest, with large trees, closed canopy, and relatively little understory vegetation. Other species prefer young forests, with densely packed small trees and abundant ground vegetation. Therefore, forest management actions vary based on the target vegetation structure and species.
As farms in New York have reverted to forests and forests have matured, there is less habitat for grassland and early successional forest species, and their populations have declined. To stem these declines, DEC and partners implement grassland and forest management projects, improving habitat for dozens of SGCN.
Habitat restoration often involves planting or seeding native vegetation in areas that have been cleared of cover or otherwise impacted by human or natural causes. Programs such as the Hudson River Estuary's Trees for Tribs Program enhances riparian corridors by planting trees to stabilize streambanks, moderate water temperatures and provide food and cover for wildlife. Other examples of habitat restoration actions include planting coastal vegetation to stabilize beaches and dunes, and planting wild blue lupine and other vegetation adapted to barrens to create additional habitat and provide connectivity between patches.

Thus, each sample was assigned a good, fair, or poor rating based on the number of analyte readings exceeding the ERL and/or ERM.
The System-Wide Eutrophication Model is a water quality model that was used to assess habitat condition based upon levels of chlorophyll A, dissolved inorganic nitrogen, and dissolved inorganic phosphorus in open water. Applying this model to marine habitats, and using cutpoints from the EPA National Coastal Condition Report IV, provided additional metrics of marine habitat condition. Finally, to assess the condition of intertidal habitats, the Landscape Condition Assessment model developed by New York Natural Heritage Program staff was used to analyze human impacts to adjacent upland habitats.

A total of 3,913 Level 2 species-threat combinations were identified for HPSGCN and SGCN. The five most-frequently cited Level 1 and Level 2 threats are identified below. The number of times that each Level 2 threat was cited for HPSGCN and SGCN are shown in Threats in this category were identified to Level 3 a total of 46 times. The two Level 3 threats cited most often were "docks and marinas" (17 species, all freshwater mollusks), and "dense housing and urban areas" (10 species).
Development of housing and urban areas was cited most frequently, followed by tourism and recreational areas, and then commercial and industrial development. The difference between the number of times that residential development was identified as a threat, and the relatively few times that commercial development was noted is likely due to "suburban sprawl," where development of natural and agricultural land outpaces human population growth.
While the relative number of threats identified in each sub-category of development is informative, the overall impact of the cumulative effects of residential, commercial, and recreational development is difficult to quantify and mitigate for.
Development reduces the quantity, quality and connectivity of habitat for SGCN. As suburban sprawl occurs throughout New York, development removes available habitat from the landscape and degrades the ecological value of remaining habitat patches through habitat fragmentation. Negative effects of habitat fragmentation include loss of connectivity to patches of similar or complementary habitats, the creation of "sink" habitats in which meta-population dynamics are lost in isolated patches, increased susceptibility to predation or parasitism from edge effects, and a reduction in the types and number of species the habitat can support.

## Agriculture and Aquaculture

Impacts from agriculture and aquaculture were identified as a threat to HPSGCN 31 times and to SGCN 27 times. This threat category includes annual and perennial non-timber crops, plantations, livestock and poultry farming, and marine and freshwater aquaculture.
Threats in this category were identified to Level 3 a total of 15 times. The Level 3 threat cited most often was "annual cropping systems (field crops)" (10 species), with several other Level 3 threats cited for only one species.
Most of the identified threats were related to an intensification of agricultural activities, primarily the conversion of pasture, hay fields and fallow fields to row crops, as well as some clearing of forested lands for crop production. Sediment and nutrient runoff from agricultural operations, as well as the use of herbicides and pesticides, negatively impact waterbodies. These threats are noted separately under category 9 (Pollution). Aquaculture is a relatively small industry in New York and was identified as a threat to 4 species.

## Energy Production and Mining

Impacts from energy production and mining were cited as a threat to HPSGCN 79 times and to SGCN 61 times. This threat category includes oil and gas drilling, mining and quarrying, and renewable energy at Level 2. Most of these threats are related to renewable energy power development, and the potential development of hydraulic fracturing in New York.
Threats in this category were identified to Level 3 a total of 91 times. The Level 3 threats cited most often were "wind farms" (38 species) and "hydroelectric dams" (26 species, all freshwater mollusks).
Interactions with and habitat loss from both terrestrial and offshore wind turbine projects, and photovoltaic solar panels are an increasing threat to several species. The presence of hydroelectric dams and associated infrastructure fragment riverine habitats, disrupt natural water flow, and may cause direct mortality to fish and aquatic invertebrates. Nocturnal migrants and coastal bird species face the threat of collision with turbines in the marine environment, and construction and operation of offshore wind turbines could potentially pose a threat to SGCN sharks and marine mammals if these species avoid preferred feeding areas or migratory pathways as a result of turbine-related activities in their habitat. Construction of land-based turbines often results in habitat loss and fragmentation for bird species dependent on contiguous grasslands and interior forest cover, and all bird species are at risk of collision with turbines. While a wide range of species are known to collide with turbines in New York, it remains unknown whether wind energy development will have a population-level effect on birds over the long-term. Land-based turbines are the greatest known threat to migratory tree bats (eastern red bat, hoary bat, and silver-haired bat), as these species regularly collide with turbine blades, particularly during the fall mating and migration period. Over the last 10 years there has been a rapid increase in the number of solar project proposals and development in New York, occurring primarily in areas of grassland and early successional habitat. The conversion of thousands of acres of open green space to solar panels will result in habitat loss and fragmentation, threatening the persistence of grassland birds on the State's landscape.
Horizontal drilling (HDD) and high-volume hydraulic fracturing (HVHF) are processes used to extract underground natural gas from gas-bearing shale formations. This requires construction of drilling pads, wastewater containment facilities, construction of roads, and pipelines to transport the extracted gas. These activities can be a threat to wildlife in multiple ways: pollution, habitat destruction, water withdrawals, and the introduction of invasive species. HVHF was banned in New York in 2015. HVHF is still included in the threat analysis, and should the current ban be lifted during the 10-year timeframe of the SWAP, this threat information will again become relevant.
The threat of mining and quarrying impacts some SGCN, particularly snakes, as the activity often destroys or degrades parts of their habitat. Mining operations destroy central rat snake den habitats, while the activities often disturbed the basking sites of eastern copperheads, and both the basking and den sites of timber rattlesnakes may be affected. The eastern wormsnake is particularly affected by mining and excavation due to its fossorial (burrowing) nature.
Sand mining and dredging poses a threat to several coastal bird SGCN including ruddy turnstone, short-billed dowitcher, red knot, Bonaparte's gull, black rail, pied-billed grebe, saltmarsh sparrow, and seaside sparrow. Gravel mining is a threat to two species of tiger beetle that are SGCN, Appalachian tiger beetle and cobblestone tiger beetle. Changes in hydrology due to gravel and limestone mining threatens the habitat of watercress snail.

## Transportation and Service Corridors

Impacts from transportation and service corridors were identified as a threat to HPSGCN 119 times and to SGCN 126 times. This threat category includes roads and railroads, utility and service lines, shipping lanes, and flight paths at Level 2.
Threats in this category were identified to Level 3 a total of 185 times. The Level 3 threats cited most often were "bridges" (77 species), "roads" (49 species), and "dredging of shipping lanes" (24 species).
Roads and railroads pose the greatest threat, which may be a factor of their prevalence on the landscape. Utility and service lines are another corridor-type development that is prevalent throughout the state. While utility corridors can degrade and fragment forest habitats, these rights-of-way can be important habitat for some species. The Karner blue butterfly, for instance, will use utility and service lines if lupine is present, and occasionally as a corridor to travel between habitat patches and colonize new areas.
Roads and railroads are a threat to SGCN through both direct mortality and more frequently, habitat fragmentation and the adverse effects of road salt. Salt used for road de-icing affects wildlife directly and indirectly. Birds may ingest road salt crystals for grit, and consumption of even small amounts of salt can result in toxicosis and death for birds. Wildlife such as moose are also attracted to the roadway to ingest salt crystals, which leads to higher incidents of vehicular accidents and wildlife fatalities. Impacts to aquatic species resulting from runoff of road salt and other topical road treatments are noted separately under category 9 (Pollution).
Road mortality is a threat to reptiles and amphibians more than any other taxa. It was cited as a threat to 30 amphibians and reptiles. New York has an extensive road network and road mortality poses a threat to some birds, especially raptors, and was also identified as a threat to the least weasel. In addition to the high density of roads, high traffic volume increases the likelihood that an animal seeking to cross the road will be unsuccessful or be struck by a vehicle.
As the second-most prevalent transportation and service corridor threat, shipping lanes pose a direct hazard to marine life that may be struck by a vessel. There is also concern that the noise from ships may impact communication and impair critical behaviors of marine mammals.

## Biological Resource Use

Impacts from biological resource use were identified as a threat to HPSGCN 124 times and to SGCN 166 times. This threat category includes hunting and collection terrestrial animals, gathering terrestrial plants or fungi, logging and wood harvesting, and fishing and harvesting aquatic resources at Level 2.
Threats in this category were identified to Level 3 a total of 140 times. The Level 3 threats cited most often were "commercial fishing" (54 species), "poaching/persecution of terrestrial animals" (22 species, 17 of which being reptiles and amphibians), and "recreational or subsistence fishing" (22 species).
Use of biological resources through recreational hunting and fishing is common throughout New York. These activities are regulated by licensing, length of open season, bag limits, type of implement allowed, and minimum size requirements. Commercial fishing occurs primarily in marine waters and is also closely regulated to conserve resources. Legal, regulated hunting and fishing are carefully managed to ensure sustainable use.
Illegal take (e.g., poaching or collecting) is a threat to several species, particularly collection of live amphibians and reptiles for the pet trade. By-catch (the catch of species other than those targeted) was cited as a threat to several aquatic and marine species.
Logging and wood harvesting can cause habitat disturbance, loss and fragmentation. Siltation and erosion that results from improper logging practices are noted separately under category 9 (Pollution).

## Human Intrusions and Disturbance

Impacts from human intrusions and disturbance were identified as a threat to HPSGCN 81 times and to SGCN 76 times. This threat category includes recreational activities, war, civil unrest and military exercises, and work and other activities at Level 2.
Threats in this category were identified to Level 3 a total of 132 times. The Level 3 threats cited most often were "motor vehicles" (38 species), and "research activities (36 species).
Recreational activities that may impact wildlife species include boating, paddling, photography, birding, beach use, rock climbing, spelunking, ATV use, whale watching, and visiting natural attractions.
Recreational boating can impact species through direct collision with the hull or propeller, as well as disturbing submerged aquatic vegetation utilized by many species of freshwater and marine fish. Noise and turbulence caused by boating may also alter the natural behaviors of some species.
The legal and illegal use of off-road vehicles such as ATVs and UTVs can impact sensitive habitats and species that utilize shallow, ephemeral water sources through direct mortality, increasing dust on water surfaces and vegetation, and disturbance of natural behaviors from increased noise and vehicular passage. Trails created for and by off-road vehicles provide access for invasive species, nest parasites, predators and additional human use that would otherwise be less likely to occur in unfragmented habitats.
All species of cave bats listed as SGCN are threatened by disturbance of their hibernacula, either from recreational spelunking or from research activities. Human presence disturbs hibernating bats and may exacerbate the impacts of White Nose Syndrome. Human intrusion and disturbance of habitat also occurs during construction and repair activities, the most common of which are bridge projects and other in-stream work that impacts the freshwater bivalve species inhabiting those waters.
Military exercises were identified as a threat affecting 6 marine SGCN, blue, fin, humpback, sei, and sperm whales, and harbor porpoise. Noise pollution caused by such activities compromises the ability of cetaceans (whales and dolphins) to successfully communicate and locate food.

## Natural System Modifications

Impacts from the modification of natural systems were identified as a threat to HPSGCN 210 times and to SGCN 319 times. This category includes fire and fire suppression, dams and water management/use, other ecosystem modifications, and removing/reducing human maintenance at Level 2.
Threats in this category were identified to Level 3 a total of 374 times. The Level 3 threats cited most often were "water level management using dams" (127 species) and "vegetation succession" (56 species).
Several species are impacted by more than one modification of the natural ecosystem. Aquatic habitats are impacted by the construction of dams and bridges, hardening of shorelines, and stream channelization that create or maintain transportation infrastructure, drinking water supplies, flood control, and agricultural uses. These actions alter the flow of riparian habitats, may increase sedimentation, decrease conveyance, change water temperature, and inhibit individuals from moving upstream or downstream.
In terrestrial habitats, the primary natural system modifications impacting species in New York are succession and fire suppression. Natural succession of vegetative communities is a threat to species dependent on grassland, pine barren, and young forest habitats. Wildland fire, a natural process which is prevented through human suppression of these fires, causes a return of habitats to an earlier successional stage, replenishes soil nutrients, decreases the presence of invasive plant species, and allows native fire-dependent species to successfully complete their life cycle. Prescribed burning is increasingly being used as a habitat and species management tool in New York.

## Invasive and Other Problematic Species, Genes and Diseases

Impacts from invasive and other problematic species were cited as a threat to HPSGCN 287 times and to SGCN 309 times. This threat category includes invasive non-native plants and animals, problematic native plants and animals, introduced genetic material, pathogens, and intrinsic biological limitations at Level 2.
Threats in this category were identified to Level 3 a total of 406 times. The Level 3 threats cited most often were "aquatic animals" (73 species), "terrestrial animals" (68 species), "terrestrial plants" (57 species), and "aquatic plants" (35 species).
The proliferation of non-native plants and animals is changing the nature of upland and aquatic ecosystems throughout New York, often degrading the quality of specific habitats to the point where they are unsuitable for use by most native species. These harmful exotic plants and animals are called "invasive species." A species is considered invasive when it is not native to the ecosystem and its presence causes significant harm to the ecology of an area, the economy, or human health.
Invasive species are harmful to the economy when they cause damage to forestry, agriculture, and cultural infrastructure, or cause a loss of recreational opportunities and tourism. The ecological damage from invasive species is a product of direct competition with native species, altered food webs, a loss of biodiversity, and loss of ecosystem functions in degraded habitats.
In all habitat types, residential and commercial development increases the risk of new occurrences of invasive exotic as well as native problematic plants, animals and pathogens. Common vectors for the introduction and transport of invasive species include moving soil or other fill materials, unintended transport of seeds, spores, eggs, or larvae on equipment or clothing, and intentional introduction of invasive species through landscaping activities.
In wetland and aquatic habitats, plant species such as purple loosestrife, Eurasian water milfoil, water chestnut, Japanese knotweed, hydrilla, starry stonewort, and common reed displace native plant species, disrupt ecological processes, and have less value to wildlife than the native plant species they replace. Transport of these aquatic invasive species in New York was historically facilitated by the extensive canal system, and more recently by the movement of uncleaned recreational boats between waterbodies, contaminated construction equipment moving between sites, and extreme storm events that flood waterbodies and transport plant seeds, roots or stems to new locations.
Invasive aquatic animals degrade habitat quality and can fundamentally alter ecosystem processes. Zebra mussels are found in most major water bodies throughout New York, impacting water supply infrastructure, native mussel species, plankton, and other aquatic species. Zebra mussels, and their deep-water relative, the quagga mussel, are a key link in the cycle of Type E botulism in the Great Lakes, which has caused episodic large-scale die-offs of piscivorous (fish-eating) waterbirds. In the Hudson River, zebra mussels have caused a reduction in the biomass of other benthic (bottomdwelling) animals and depleted dissolved oxygen 
(Caraco 2000)
. This depletion of dissolved oxygen negatively impacts aquatic animals and exacerbates degradation of water quality.
There are 16 species of invasive fish in New York, and several introduced fish species have established populations within the state, some of which have impacted aquatic ecosystems. Native fish have also been distributed to additional watersheds, both through early fisheries management programs and through unintentional releases from bait buckets. Sea lamprey, a parasitic fish native to Lake Ontario and Lake Champlain that feeds on the body fluids of other fish, has had a significant negative effect on sport fish populations in those waters as well as other large lakes in New York. The intentional or unintentional introduction of non-native fishes has occurred in lakes and ponds statewide, causing drastic declines in native species such as brook trout. Other aquatic invasive species that impact natural resources in New York include the spiny water flea, fish hook water flea, rusty crayfish, common carp, and alewife.
In terrestrial upland habitats, invasive exotic plants and animals introduced through human activity reduce biodiversity. There are more than 50 identified invasive terrestrial plant species in New York, including garlic mustard, giant hogweed, Japanese barberry, and multiflora rose. There are also more than 10 vertebrate animal invasive species, including the Eurasian boar and mute swan, more than 10 invertebrate invasive animals, including spotted lanternfly, hemlock woolly adelgid, and Asian longhorned beetle, and four species of invasive fungi on the list of prohibited and regulated invasive species (
https://dec.ny.gov/nature/invasive-species/resources-regulations/regulations
).
In 2006 the invasive fungus Pseudogymnoascus destructans (Pd) was first documented in North America, near Albany, New York. Pd causes white-nose syndrome, named for the white fungus that grows on the muzzle, wings and other areas of exposed skin of bats. Infected bats wake from hibernation early and often, resulting in depleted fat reserves and death through starvation or exposure as they exit hibernacula in winter in an attempt to find food. While localized populations of cave bats such as Indiana bat, northern long-eared bat, and little brown bat were reduced by 90-100% in hibernacula across the northeast, a few sites are now showing signs of stabilization and slow recovery in numbers of bats counted during winter surveys.
Another invasive fungus, Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd), causes chytridiomycosis (chytrid), an infectious disease impacting amphibians, particularly frogs. Bd is waterborne, and enters through an amphibian's skin causing excessive shedding, abnormal feeding behavior, and an imbalance in osmotic regulation, leading to death by cardiac arrest. While harmless to humans, Bd is easily spread through contaminated footwear, clothing, wet gear, and by moving captive or wildlife animals from one location to another. Bd has caused steep declines and some extinctions of amphibian populations worldwide.
Ranavirus is a DNA virus that affects amphibians, reptiles and fish, resulting in a high rate of mortality. Outbreaks are often seasonal and can occur quickly, causing localized die-offs in just a few days.
Problematic native wildlife species can also present a threat to SGCN if they occur in sensitive locations or have overabundant populations. The threats from native species are a result of direct predation by subsidized predators, competition for nest sites, parasitism, and a reduction in habitat quality through alteration of vegetative composition and structure. Problematic native species identified as threats to SGCN include racoon, fox, beaver, deer, brown-headed cowbird, and gulls.

## Pollution

Pollution was the most frequently identified threat with impacts cited as a threat to HPSGCN 318 times and to SGCN 625 times. This threat category includes domestic and urban wastewater, industrial and military effluents, agricultural and forestry effluents, garbage and solid waste, air-borne pollutants, and excess energy.
Threats in this category were identified to Level 3 a total of 725 times. The Level 3 threats cited most often were "herbicides & pesticides" (211 species), "soil erosion, sedimentation" (101 species), and "nutrient loads" (96 species).
Often the harmful effects and sources of pollution are known, and the threat is currently being addressed through successful implementation of regulatory programs to achieve pollution control. Advances in analytical chemistry have refined our measurement of pollutant levels, and research continues to identify new environmental pollutants, such as pharmaceuticals, micro-plastics and perand polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS). Although our current environment is significantly cleaner than it was when the Clean Water Act, Clean Air Act, and the National Environmental Policy Act were first enacted in the 1970s, the cumulative effects of legacy pollutants and modern pollution will continue to be a threat to some SGCN for many years.
In the aquatic environment excessive nutrient loading resulting from both point and non-point source pollution impacts many SGCN. The primary nutrient of concern in freshwater systems is phosphorus; the primary nutrient of concern in estuarine and marine systems is nitrogen. Elevated nutrient levels in water bodies contribute to excessive algal and vegetative growth, exacerbating the spread of aquatic nuisance plants and diminishing the value of aquatic habitats for fish and wildlife.
Stormwater runoff from developed areas is a major source of water pollution in New York. Impervious surfaces such as roofs and pavement reduce infiltration of precipitation to the ground, resulting in increased surface runoff. This flow of water carries sediment, nutrients, chemicals, oils, and solid waste into waterways, causing multiple deleterious impacts to aquatic systems. In addition to the stormwater that directly enters waterbodies, another source of pollution is when stormwater is collected in sewerage systems that combine wastewater with stormwater. During intense storms, these combined sewer systems can be overwhelmed, resulting in a direct discharge of the pollution through a combined sewer overflow.
Runoff from road salt can cause a decline among populations of salt-sensitive species in the freshwater aquatic environment. Damage to salt-intolerant vegetation can have significant impact on wildlife habitat by destroying food resources, shelter and breeding and nesting sites, and by creating a favorable environment for non-native invasive species. Chloride in surface waters can be toxic to many forms of aquatic life. Sedimentation, pesticides, and herbicides were also identified as threats to many SGCN. Species impacted by these threats include freshwater fish, freshwater mussels, amphibians, and aquatic and terrestrial insects.
Discharge of metals and other toxic substances from discrete point sources to the aquatic environment is carefully regulated through the State Pollutant Discharge Elimination System Program. However, the cumulative effect of current discharges and legacy pollutants from historic discharges continues to have negative impacts, particularly in aquatic and marine environments.
Historic discharges of polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) have polluted major water bodies such as the Hudson River, Lake Champlain, the Great Lakes, and the St. Lawrence River, negatively impacting these aquatic ecosystems. PCBs entered these aquatic systems by direct discharge from industrial sites, where they have contaminated the sediment and entered the food web. PCBs are persistent in the environment, attach strongly to soils and river sediments, and readily accumulate in fish, wildlife, and humans. Remediation projects are complete or underway on all of these major contaminated sites, but work continues to determine the extent of natural resource damage and monitor the effectiveness of remediation efforts.
Lead, mercury, and other industrial discharges were cited as threats to many SGCN. The lead used in fishing tackle and shotgun ammunition has long been recognized as a pollutant that can be harmful to wildlife such as loons, herons, and other fish-eating birds and mammals. The sale of lead sinkers weighing one-half ounce or less is illegal in New York.
The lead in bullets is also recognized as a pollutant threat to wildlife, particularly bald eagles and other carrion-eating birds and mammals. Lead poisoning occurs when the birds consume the spent bullet fragments in carcasses or gut piles contaminated with lead shot. Bullets made from non-toxic material are now available, and in 2020 DEC and partners began a working group to incentivize the use of non-toxic bullets, and evaluate and address impacts of lead ammunition on non-target species.
In the environment, elemental mercury can be converted to methylmercury, which then enters the food web, leading to impairments to reproduction and health. The source of mercury emissions affecting New York comes primarily from the upper Midwest and is now under regulatory control.
While industrial pollutants are difficult to address on a small scale, one type of pollution that can be readily addressed at the individual and local level is light pollution. Many insect SGCN, as well as nocturnally migrating birds are impacted by artificial lights. Birds often collide with bright buildings at night or will circle a point light source until exhausted. Artificial light reduces the ability of fireflies to effectively communicate and find mates. State and national level "Lights Out" initiatives provide guidance on how families, businesses, and municipalities can reduce the amount of light pollution in their area.

## Geological Events

## Climate Change

Impacts in this category were cited as a threat to HPSGCN 290 times and to SGCN 371 times. This threat category includes habitat shifting and alteration, changes in geochemical regimes, changes in temperature regimes, changes in precipitation and hydrological regimes, and storms and severe weather.
Threats in this category were identified to Level 3 a total of 399 times. The Level 3 threats cited most often were "droughts" (158 species) and "gradual temperature change" (80 species).
Natural climatic changes can occur suddenly, such as when a meteor strikes the earth or major volcano erupts, but historically localized and widespread changes have been a slow process occurring over thousands or even millions of years, with gradual alterations to the earth's hydrologic and thermal regimes. While the slow pace of these shifts allowed species to adapt to new and emerging conditions, the current rate of change is occurring more rapidly than many species are able to respond and adapt to.
An increase or decrease in average air or water temperatures, changes to the timing and amount of precipitation events, increased salinity of coastal wetlands and tidal waters, increased unpredictability in the timing and severity of storms, and shifts in the composition of plant communities are some of the more frequently cited threats to SGCN in New York. The resulting droughts, floods, heat waves, altered habitat structure and other changes negatively impact many native species of wildlife and are happening now at a faster pace than ever previously documented.
These environmental changes are already affecting New York, and impacts are projected to grow. Primary impacts in New York include higher average temperatures and precipitation, more severe storms, more frequent and longer heat waves, decreased winter snowpack, and saltwater intrusion into freshwater habitats. Between 1895-2023, annual total precipitation (rain and snow) in New York has increased at a rate of about +0.46 inches per decade, with 17 of the last 20 years exceeding the 20 th century average 
(Karmalkar et al, 2024)
. Intense, damaging rainfall events have increased across the Northeast in recent decades, with an over 70% increase in heavy precipitation from 1958 to 2010, greater than any other region in the country (NYSDEC 2025). Relative to current precipitation amounts, New York is projected to experience up to 50% increase in precipitation, with more rain than snow. Over the next 80 years all areas of the state are likely to experience an increase in the number of days per year with extreme precipitation 
(Karmalkar et al, 2024)
.
Intense storm events are becoming more common, causing widespread flooding. Higher ocean levels will increase the frequency and severity of damaging storm surges and shoreline flooding, permanently inundate some low-lying coastal areas with saltwater, and dramatically accelerate coastal erosion, threatening the ecologically important salt marshes and estuaries of Long Island. These habitats serve as critical feeding grounds for migrating waterfowl and other species, breeding habitat for marsh birds and other coastal species, and nursery habitat for fish and other aquatic organisms. Water levels along New York's coast and in the Hudson River have risen more than a foot since 1900 (about 1.2 inches per decade) (NYSDEC 2025), and between 1850-2020, measurements at The Battery, New York have increased by about 0.95 feet, a rate of about +2.90 +/-0.08 mm/year 
(Karmalkar et al, 2024)

## Chapter 7. Monitoring SGCN, Habitats, and Project Effectiveness

## SGCN Monitoring

There is a strong body of science to inform the monitoring of fish and wildlife. Monitoring protocols and accepted methods for statistical analysis of monitoring data are well established for several taxa. For some species, there are monitoring records covering many decades, with robust statistical analysis of the data to elucidate the cause of detected changes. For other species, there are no established monitoring programs and little available information on abundance and distribution. Birds are relatively well monitored, and the information is widely shared through various reports, local birding groups, and eBird. Some mammals, such as cave bats, are systematically monitored, but other SGCN mammals, like the least weasel, are so rare that a structured monitoring program is not possible. Freshwater fish are regularly surveyed by DEC, so, in most cases, distribution and population trends can be determined with a high degree of certainty. Similarly, most marine fish are recorded in regular surveys, although some species are rarely encountered due to their scarcity or selection of less surveyed habitats. Populations of some amphibian and reptile species are regularly surveyed by DEC and conservation partners, with efforts often focused on a particular locality or species (largely those listed threatened or endangered) rather than being conducted through a systematic statewide program. While there has not been systematic statewide monitoring of freshwater mussel species, SWG funding has enabled DEC to monitor r mussel populations in watersheds in Western New York. The freshwater mussel program will be expanding in the coming years to cover surveys in other parts of the state. Several insect taxa have been surveyed for presence/absence, but only a few species with protected status have ongoing long-term monitoring to detect trends in distribution and abundance.
The USFWS monitors species and habitats in the National Wildlife Refuge (NWR) system. There are 13 National Wildlife Refuges located wholly or partly in New York. Iroquois National Wildlife Refuge is the largest, with about 10,800 acres, followed by the Montezuma NWR with about 10,000 acres. These two refuges are a matrix of wetland and upland habitats used by numerous SGCN and provide critical stopover sites for migrating birds. The 597-acre Shawangunk Grassland NWR provides important habitat for grassland SGCN, as does the Wallkill River NWR that is shared between New York and New Jersey. To be effective, monitoring programs must be designed to meet specific objectives, such as documentation of baseline conditions, compliance monitoring, and identifying trends over time. Monitoring of threats to species and habitats provides more information than population surveys alone and helps to identify local and landscape level conservation needs. The most valuable monitoring data are collected using standardized methodologies over a long time period to establish trends and link the causes and effects of observed changes.

## Monitoring the Effectiveness of Conservation Actions

Monitoring the performance of conservation actions has been a longstanding challenge for environmental resource managers. Traditional measures, such as number of acres of habitat restored, sometimes do not result in immediate improvement in the stability of a species' population. Due to the statistical margin of error, natural population variability, and lag time, it can be difficult to document population increases with precision. Additionally, even when population improvements are documented, a direct causal link between the measured improvement and a particular conservation action sometimes cannot be documented based on available data.
Efforts to facilitate monitoring of conservation actions at the northeast and national levels are ongoing. The Northeast states have developed a standardized terminology for conservation planning and a regional database to store and query data on Regional SGCN. These efforts facilitate rollup of state-level data for analysis at a regional level, providing a base of information for the assessment of landscapescale conservation projects. At the national level, the USFWS has developed Tracking and Reporting Actions for the Conservation of Species (TRACS), a tracking and reporting system for conservation actions funded by the Office of Conservation Investment (formerly known as the Wildlife and Sport Fish Restoration Program), including SWG. TRACS provides long-term tracking of conservation actions implemented for each project.

## UNDERSTANDING, PREPARING FOR AND RESPONDING TO WILDLIFE HEALTH ISSUES

There are many diseases, pathogens and toxins that negatively impact SGCN in New York. Some have been on the landscape for decades, while others are new or emerging threats. As of the end of 2024, some diseases and toxins of particular concern in New York include highly pathogenic avian influenza, lead, rodenticides, white nose syndrome, and Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans (Bsal). Two additional diseases of concern that would affect only one SGCN each in New York are rabbit hemorrhagic disease virus (RHDV2) and chronic wasting disease (CWD). While neither currently affect wildlife populations in the state, RHDV2 and CWD could impact New England cottontail and moose populations, respectively, should these diseases circulate in eastern cottontail and whitetailed deer populations.

## Chapter 9. Adaptively Managing SGCN and Habitats

The existing monitoring of species, habitats, and project effectiveness provides a strong foundation for adaptively managing SGCN and their habitats. There are opportunities to improve the efficiency of monitoring efforts, using concepts like indicator species to maintain a balance between monitoring costs and the expenses of management and restoration projects. Monitoring is a critical component of environmental and species management and is essential to managing adaptively. Monitoring must be well planned to ensure that the data will enable the appropriate statistical analyses to answer management questions. Quality assurance is an important consideration in a monitoring program, and it must be integrated into program design, data collection, and data analysis.
Occasionally issues arise which require immediate action, especially related to wildlife disease and invasive species. Managers must quickly assess the new information, determine the level of threat to natural resources, and sometimes take immediate action to control the threat. Examples of this emergency response type of natural resource management include the response to highly pathogenic avian influenza, chronic wasting disease, white nose syndrome, and the establishment of a northern snakehead population. Because of the obvious and immediate need for action, this type of crisis response necessitates managing adaptively, more so than issues that allow a more deliberative approach to resource management.

## YOUNG FOREST INITIATIVE

Effective wildlife management must integrate management, outreach, and monitoring efforts. In previous decades, populations of species dependent on young forest habitat have experienced declines across the state and throughout the Northeast due to natural forest succession. In 2015, the Young Forest Initiative was created within DEC's Division of Fish and Wildlife (DFW). The goal of this program is to address concerns about declines in young forest habitat, and the corresponding impacts to populations of associated wildlife species, by increasing the amount of young forest to 10% of total forested acreage on select State Wildlife Management Areas (WMAs).
This management approach has coupled habitat improvement efforts with targeted outreach to help build a public understanding of the importance of young forests to wildlife diversity. The program has increased habitat quantity and quality for many SGCN including American woodcock, eastern whip-poor-will, brown thrasher, and New England cottontail by integrating education, management, and monitoring. In addition to young forests, the DFW also manages mature and late-successional stands to improve habitat quality and provide a balanced approach to forest management for wildlife.

Monitoring efforts for a particular species or guild of species are not uniform across the state. For example, over the last 20 years DEC's work on freshwater mussels has primarily been focused in the western third of the state. As DEC's freshwater mussel program continues to grow, additional surveys in other portions of the state will improve understanding of mussel species' status and distribution and inform appropriate management and conservation measures. Wildlife management and monitoring programs are also critical to addressing the direct and indirect impacts of changing climatic conditions such as rapidly fluctuating temperature and precipitation regimes and more frequent or extreme storm events. As discussed earlier, these altered conditions introduce new impacts, such as saltwater intrusion into previously freshwater wetland systems and shifting vegetative communities, but they also have the potential to affect many other threats to SGCN as well as ecosystem responses to management actions. Even previously successful management interventions may no longer work in the future. Successful integration of these changes into natural resource management will require learning from fluctuating systems and species distributions and being able to adapt as needed.

## FIRE ON THE NEW YORK LANDSCAPE

Fire, in the form of lightning-caused wildfire or purposeful burning conducted by indigenous peoples, was historically an important disturbance regime in New York ecosystems. Across the state, fire frequency ranged from every few years to every few centuries depending on the landscape. Consequently, fire suppression over the last century has resulted in population declines of SGCN that rely on fire-adapted landscapes and open, early successional habitats such as pine barrens, grasslands, and oak woodlands. Prescribed fire, conducted by trained practitioners under strict timing and weather restrictions to mitigate risk, is an important habitat management tool when promoting firereliant species.
By matching prescribed fire to historic disturbance regimes, land managers can manage for habitat composition and structure that fire-reliant species have evolved to need. Additionally, prescribed fire is a tool to safely reduce flammable biomass that can result in dangerous wildfires if left to accumulate over time.
Over the last 10 years DEC has increased the number of Wildlife Management Areas where prescribed fire is utilized to enhance and restore habitat that benefits many SGCN. DEC will continue to build interagency capacity and work with New York State Parks, the Albany Pine Bush Commission, and other partners to develop long-term burn plans for additional properties to create a mosaic of fire-adapted habitats that support rare and declining species.
Effective conservation is a cycle of planning, implementing, and assessing, then revising plans based on implementation progress and current information. In updating the 2015 SWAP, the 2025 SWAP marks a full completion of this cycle. New York State and its conservation partners are committed to this cyclic model of conservation, and will continue implementation and assessment, with the next major revision of the SWAP scheduled for 2035.

## Threat Characteristics

Three threat characteristics can be used to help prioritize actions by identifying the most pervasive, severe and urgent threats to species, species groups, and/or habitats. Level 2 threats were identified 1,665 times for HPSGCN and 2,248 times for SGCN, though not all had threat characteristics quantified (see individual species status assessments). Identified as Spatial Extent (Scope), Severity, and Immediacy, these characteristics are further defined below.
Spatial Extent (Scope): Consider the impact of the threat within the next 10 years in New York.
• Small (low impact) = A small portion of the habitat or population is negatively impacted by the threat (<10%)
• Restricted (moderate impact) = (10-30%)
• Large (moderate impact) = (30-70%)
• Pervasive (high impact) = A large portion of the habitat or population is negatively impacted by the threat (>70%)
Severity: Impact of threat within the spatial extent within New York over the next 10 years.
• Slight (low impact) = degree of ecological change is minor (reduce population 1-10%)
• Moderate (moderate impact) = (reduce population by 11-30%)
• Serious (moderate impact) = (reduce population by 31-70%)
• Extreme (high impact) = Likely to destroy or eliminate occurrence or reduce population by 71-100%

## Immediacy:

The timescale over which impacts of the threat will be observable in New York.
• Long-term (low impact) = Effects of the threat are expected in 10-100 years given known ecosystem interactions or compounding threats
• Near-term (moderate impact) = Effects of the threat are expected within the next 1-10 years
