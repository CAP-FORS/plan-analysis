## Executive Summary

This document is the 10-year revision of Rhode Island's Wildlife Action Plan (RIWAP), an effective strategy for wildlife conservation enabled through the State Wildlife Grants program administered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. The goal of the RIWAP is to provide direction of wildlife conservation efforts in Rhode Island for the next decade. It represents a vision and a strategy that the Rhode Island Department of Environmental Management (RIDEM) shares with its partners for conservation of wildlife in the state.
Despite being the smallest and second-most densely populated state in the U.S., Rhode Island's wildlife is remarkably diverse, including thousands of resident and migratory species of mammals, birds, fish, reptiles and amphibians, beetles, butterflies and moths, and other insects, freshwater mollusks, annelids, crustaceans, and other marine invertebrates. From the western highlands to the coastlines and the adjoining ocean waters, the state supports a broad spectrum of biodiversity, ranging from the rarest and most endangered species to the most common and abundant.
In part, the 2025 RIWAP is a barometer of the State's ecological health. It offers a composite picture, based on up-to-date science and using the most advanced analytical tools available. This RIWAP outlines a solid and practical framework for wildlife management, for government and individual decision-making, and for protecting species that have been identified through a rigorous process as species of greatest conservation need. This effort was led by a Core Team who were responsible for the coordination of taxa teams, partner and public review, and preparation of the RIWAP itself, below. This RIWAP is presented in two complementary formats: first, this written plan; and second, the accompanying species and habitat database at 
https://dem.ri.gov/riwap-database
. These were assembled in partnership with RIDEM, The Nature Conservancy, the University of Rhode Island, and the RI Natural History Survey. Together these documents present the most current information available on Rhode Island's wildlife and on the habitats and natural systems that support them. It also includes detailed information on the threats to wildlife species in the state and outlines strategies for addressing those threats.

## REGIONAL CONTEXT

As few species are contained within a single state, regional coordination and knowledge sharing is critical. The Northeast Fish and Wildlife Diversity Technical Committee (NEFWDTC) of the Northeast Association of Fish and Wildlife Agencies (NEAFWA), has maintained a list of regional priority species since the 1990s, now called the Regional Species of Greatest Conservation Need (RSGCN) list.
This list is based on native species' conservation status and listing in State Wildlife Action Plans (SWAPs), as well as the percentage of the species' United States (U.S.) range that occurs in the Northeast. To keep the RSGCN list current and identify changing needs, taxa experts from across the region convene regularly to update species and threats of concern and priorities to address them. These regional teams include or consult the expertise of other, more taxa specific Northeast working groups and committees.
RSGCN updates are conducted every five years and staggered on either end of state 10-year updates. The RSGCN revision process specifically ensures timely and systematic review across taxa, including the development of "watchlist" species not previously listed. Collectively, this framework provides regional context to state experts to inform SWAP revisions.
As with Rhode Island's list, the regional invertebrate list remains incomplete and in progress. ## BIRDS

Approximately 454 species of birds have been reported in Rhode Island, including migratory, breeding, and residential populations. Rhode Island's bird species are incredibly diverse and utilize a variety of habitats. Narragansett Bay, Long Island Sound, and the Atlantic Ocean support pelagic and inshore birds, while coastal habitats support beach and mudflat and tidal species, including the piping plover and roseate tern, both federally endangered. The Bay Islands provide critical nesting grounds for populations of gulls, terns, and herons. Waterfowl and salt marsh birds are dependent on Rhode Island's freshwater and coastal wetlands, particularly salt marshes, which have declined due to development, contamination, and invasive species (see Chapter 2).
In the uplands, grassland birds, such as bobolink and grasshopper sparrow, have been on the decline due to shifting and decreasing habitat, including that of agricultural lands. Shrublands, young forests, and mature forests each support a wealth of bird diversity; the American woodcock, scarlet tanager, and broad-winged hawk all call Rhode Island's forests home.
Pelagic birds are not included in the RIWAP, in part because they do not nest in state waters and because local populations vary temporally and spatially. However, such species are still at risk from a variety of threats, including loss of habitat or mortality from offshore wind turbines and oil spills, and as bycatch in fishing gear. As part of the Rhode Island Ocean Special Area Management Plan, 
Paton et al. (2010)

The RI Department of Environmental Management (RIDEM) leads and collaborates on several bird monitoring initiatives (see Chapter 5). Most notably, The Second Atlas of Breeding Birds in Rhode Island, a rich account of the data captured in collaboration with the University of Rhode Island (URI) between 2015 and 2019, was published in 2024.

## FISH

More than 300 fish species have been observed in Rhode Island's freshwater and marine habitats (Table 
1-1
). Associated habitats for these fish species span the full range of northeastern aquatic environments, including freshwater, estuarine, and marine systems. Migratory (both anadromous and catadromous) species, as well as non-migratory species, are represented.
From a taxonomic perspective, the largest share of RSGCN fish in the Northeast are small-bodied freshwater species in the families Percidae (darters and perches) and Leuciscidae (chubs and minnows), a pattern which holds true across North America. These smaller fish are primarily threatened by habitat alteration, including sedimentation, construction of dams and other barriers, and other forms of aquatic habitat destruction and contamination.
Most of Rhode Island's fish diversity consists of saltwater species. This diversity attracts both commercial and recreational fishermen. RIDEM, NOAA, and the Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission manage fish species found in Rhode Island's marine and estuarine waters. Many of the saltwater and estuarine fishery resources found in the state's waters are exhibiting regional population declines, while others are highly migratory and population data are limited. The state of Rhode Island continually reviews its fisheries management programs, including permitted harvest numbers and dates, identifying potential conservation actions to improve protection of the state's fisheries.

## INVERTEBRATES

Almost 400 invertebrate species (terrestrial and fresh water) are presently tracked in the Rhode Island Natural Heritage Database, with 56 of those species listed by the state as endangered, threatened, or concern according to RIDEM's Division of Fish & Wildlife (DFW). ## MAMMALS

Rhode Island hosts approximately 84 different species of mammals, most of which are indigenous or native to the state. A few mammal species have recently established or reestablished breeding populations in the state (e.g., coyote, fisher, and beaver), while others have been introduced with the aid of humans (e.g., house mouse, eastern cottontail, and black and Norway rats).
Some of Rhode Island's mammals are generalists and can be found in a variety of habitats.
Others are specialists preferring a single habitat type and thus more susceptible to threats of development, deforestation, and other habitat conversions. Forest maturation has changed the compositional structure and age class of Rhode Island's forests from what it was 50+ years ago so that some small mammals, such as the New England cottontail, have declined as a result.

## SPECIES OF GREATEST CONSERVATION NEED

Historically, hunting and trapping caused the decline of certain mammal populations (e.g., beaver) but in recent decades most of these species have recovered.
RIDEM and its partners monitor the abundance and distribution of several mammal populations in the state. Species that are hunted and trapped, including coyote, beaver, fisher, and whitetailed deer, are monitored through RIDEM management programs. ## PLANTS

Plants comprise a significant proportion of any area's biodiversity, but this large taxon is not directly eligible for State Wildlife Grants (SWGs) and has therefore been less represented in SWAPs.
An assessment of plant populations is important information to consider when determining the condition of the habitats in which these plants are found. For example, brackish marshes are a rare community type along the coast of Rhode Island. They have been slowly degraded by a variety of intrusions, and according to many predictions are highly vulnerable to climate change related impacts, including stronger storms and rising sea level. Brackish marshes constitute the habitat for a well-defined flora that includes many plant species found in no other community.
Species have been added to the SGCN list based on their rarity in the wild, while others have been removed because they are now known to be more common than previously understood or taxonomic understanding of the species has changed so that the species is no longer considered rare in New England. Fungi are also included for the first time in recognition of their critical role in supporting both plants and animals.

The process for identification of Rhode Island's 2025 SGCN began with an updated evaluation of the 2015 SGCN list using the amended Northeast Lexicon criteria described above. Prior to expert review and in service to the NEFWDTC in support of SWAP revisions, Terwilliger Consulting, Inc. provided northeast states with materials to ensure consideration of newly emerging threats and native species that were not already evaluated in 2015 SWAPs. These materials included a list of all the potential species that may occur in the state, pulled from the RSGCN database and based off of NatureServe state occurrence records (with RSGCN species verified by regional experts). The materials also included state summary data from the RSGCN database describing the RSGCN and Watchlist species occurring in Rhode Island, broken down by taxonomic groups, habitat associations, and threats. State experts used these materials, as well as additional research and compilation of the best available quantitative and qualitative information on all species in the state to update the SGCN list. RIDEM information, along with data and assessments from a wide range of government agencies, academia, NGOs, and private individuals, were compiled and reviewed (i.e. IUCN, PIF, AFS, etc.). Data sources are detailed in the Rhode Island Wildlife Action Plan (RIWAP) database and Appendix V.
Over 50 internal and external experts most knowledgeable of the status of particular taxa in Rhode Island participated in the RIWAP Taxa Teams (Appendix I) (together referred to as the Technical Team). Collectively, the Technical Team assessed six major taxa groups: mammals, birds, herpetofauna, fish, invertebrates, and plants, as well as additional experts who addressed habitats directly. These teams developed recommendations for changes to the 2015 SGCN list utilizing the criteria listed as well as the best available scientific quantitative and qualitative information. The Marine Plant Team added several algae to the SGCN list, a first for the RIWAP. The Partner Review Period (described in Chapter 7) provided further input and recommendations for the SGCN list inclusion and supporting context that was incorporated into the RIWAP database. Most notably, it resulted in the inclusion of an additional taxa group, fungi, another first for the RIWAP.
The 2025 teams carried forward the 2015 decision to rank the actions that address threats to species and their habitats but not to rank the species themselves. This remains an inclusive approach that follows the intent of the SWG program to "keep common species common" and to direct limited resources toward the highest priority actions across taxa wherever possible. For taxa with an overwhelming number of native species (i.e., invertebrate groups and plants), teams selected subsets of "focal" or "indicator" species representative of certain larger groups of species with similar individual and habitat related needs. In this way, conservation actions developed for these focal species also provide for the diverse suite of additional species that utilize these habitats but were not chosen to be listed as SGCN. While the focal species approach is not new in 2025, it was further honed. One example is moths, where the conservation threats and actions remain largely unchanged, but the list of focal species to represent them was pared down significantly to remove the more secure species.
The 2025 RIWAP lists 625 species, with 542 SGCN and 83 SAPS. The SGCN include 248 vertebrates, 209 invertebrates, and 85 combined plants (77), marine algae (3), and fungi (5) (RIWAP database).
Most additions fall into a few taxa. The list of marine mammals and fish, particularly whales and seals in the former category and sharks and skates in the latter, have grown notably, largely due to rapid environmental changes in the Northwest Atlantic Ocean and so research can be conducted dynamically and adaptively prior to the next RIWAP revision in ten years. The list of invertebrates continues to grow, reflecting the greater level of inventory and assessment of species within these groups that has occurred during the past decade, spurred by recommendations in the 2015 plan (e.g., wild bees) and a call to continue this work in the coming decade (e.g., ants are newly added and all need more data). The plant list has grown as well, again largely due to increased attention and a dedicated taxa team to develop a more comprehensive plant list and, for the first time, identify threats and conservation priorities for the listed species. In general, the primary vertebrate groups (mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians) include a similar number of additions and deletions with resulting totals relatively comparable between the 2005, 2015, and 2025 lists. According to the logic provided by teams and documented in the RIWAP database, deletions resulted primarily from determinations that 2015 listed species' numbers have either improved or are not as rare or restricted as previously thought, that they are of limited concern elsewhere in the region (this mostly applied to highly mobile fish and birds), or that the species are no longer present in Rhode Island.

## GEOLOGY

The geologic history of Rhode Island, which is predominantly responsible for the state's present landscape and landforms, includes two periods of mountain-building followed by extensive periods of erosion that produced the state's current lowland and gently rolling topography 
(Gibbs et al. 1995;
Quinn 1997)
. The fault lines produced by Rhode Island's mountain-building phases are still present in the Narragansett Bay area and occasionally rock the state with minor earthquakes.
Most of the state is underlain by igneous and metamorphic rocks 136-570 million years old 
(Quinn 1997)
 where glaciers and waves have exposed the underlying rocks along the southern coast.
Otherwise, the state's landscape is covered with a mantle of sand and gravel that reflects the state's more recent glacial history. The bedrock is dominated by granites, with sedimentary rocks and coal beds found within and surrounding 
(Gibbs et al. 1995;
Quinn 1997)
. The only known occurrence in the world of the mineral cumberlandite is found in at Iron Mine Hill where it was mined during the 18th century. Fine-grained granite found in is also well-known and is one of the global standards for the granite rock type (Quinn 1997).
Rhode Island's more recent geologic history begins with the last period of glaciation roughly 14,000 years ago. The glaciers and the material they left behind (or took away) created Long Island, Long Island Sound, and Narragansett Bay. Conanicut and Aquidneck Islands are high points isolated from the mainland by rising post-glacial sea levels. The entire landform of Block Island is a pile of glacial till that is a remnant of the terminal moraine which once stretched continuously from Long Island (NY) to Cape Cod (MA).
The Charlestown Moraine, a ridge of sand and gravel, extends inland along the coast from Westerly to Narragansett and corresponds to similar landforms on Long Island 
(Gibbs et al. 1995;
Quinn 1997)
.

Worden Pond and Great Swamp in South Kingstown are remnants of a larger water body created behind the dam formed by the Charlestown Moraine. Other glacial features in the state include drumlins, which are streamlined hills oriented in the direction the glacier moved across the landscape, and eskers which are sinuous ridges of sand and gravel that formed underneath a melting glacier. Kettle holes are depressions created when chunks of glacial ice melted to form lakes, ponds and wetlands 
(Gibbs et al. 1995;
Quinn 1997)
.

## SOILS

Most of Rhode Island's soil types are derived from glacial till and glaciofluvial deposits. Sandy loam is the dominant soil type, covering more than half of the state on hills, drumlins, terraces, and outwash plains. These soils are moderately well-drained to well-drained and contain varying amounts of rock and stone that create an assortment of "very stony," "gravelly," or "extremely stony" characterizations. Silt loams are the second most abundant soil type and, along with the sandy loams, form the basis for many of the state's Prime or Important Farmlands. Mucks, which are very poorly drained soils associated with wetlands, cover about 4% of the state and are derived from organic material. Other soil types are less abundant and more localized in their distribution, including beach and dune soils, peats, and bedrock outcrops. Mucky sandy and/or silt loams are characteristic of Rhode Island's floodplain soils, poorly to very poorly drained, and derived from alluvium and sandy glaciofluvial materials 
(NRCS 1981)
.

## CLIMATE

Rhode Island's climate is largely governed by its proximity and exposure to the Atlantic Ocean, with coastal areas tending to have slightly moderated temperatures 
(Gibbs et al. 1995)
. The coastal lowlands receive an average of 45" to 50" of precipitation each year, while uplands record between 50" and 55" of precipitation annually. Providence County and western Kent County typically receive more snow (40-60") each year, than Washington County and areas adjacent to Narragansett Bay (20-40"). The mean annual temperature is 51.32 °F. January is the coldest month of the year (tenyear mean temperature of 30.8° F), and July is the warmest (10-year mean temperature of 73.8° F), with a +2.3° F/decade and +1.9° F/decade trend respectively between 2015 and 2025; NOAA 2025). This annual variation creates distinct seasons that affect or influence use of the state's land and waterscapes by a variety of migratory fish and wildlife. Precipitation tends to be more uniform than temperature throughout the four seasons. Overall, the state's weather is known for its frequent and dramatic changes, with temperatures that can shift up to 50 degrees in one week 
(Gibbs et al. 1995)
. Blizzards and hurricanes occasionally affect the state, as do tornadoes, ice storms, and flash floods.
Rhode Island has been growing warmer and wetter since 1895. Average annual temperature has increased by about 4° F between 1895 and 2023, faster than global mean temperature and that of the Northeast generally (+2.5° F). The ocean region next to RI is experiencing rapid warming as well; the Gulf of Maine is among the fastest warming ocean regions globally. This includes more frequent occurrences of warm sea surface temperature anomalies in recent years as well as increased frequency, duration, and intensity of marine heatwaves in the past decade. In addition, sea levels have risen by about 0.94 feet around Newport, RI over the last century. As Rhode Island has already begun to witness, seemingly small increases in sea level can have large impacts along the coast due to storm surges and exceptionally high tides. Rhode Island is also experiencing pronounced increases in precipitation; in 15 of the last 20 years, Rhode Island received more precipitation than the 20th century average. Changes in average climate conditions have been accompanied by upward trends in extremes of heat, cold, drought, and heavy precipitation events 
(Alexander et al. 2006)
.
Snow and ice coverage has decreased, and growing seasons have grown longer in many areas.
Studies began to detect changes in the phenology (flowering time) of plants more than two decades ago 
(Miller-Rushing and Primack, 2008;
Primack et al.,2004)
.
Incorporating climate change considerations into the RIWAP is vital for the development and implementation of effective conservation actions.

## ECOLOGICAL HABITAT AND VEGETATION SYSTEMS

A variety of classification systems have been developed to describe the natural communities of Rhode Island and the region based on vegetation, and these continue to evolve. This information enhances our understanding of the factors that govern the distribution of various species and how to conserve wildlife. For many wildlife species, especially invertebrates, the lack of distribution and abundance data means that associated vegetative communities are the best available sources of information for conservation planning and decision-making.
At the finer scale, the Natural Communities of Rhode Island (NCRI; Enser and Lundgren 2006) was a joint project of TNC and the RI Natural Heritage Program developed by conducting on-site ecological surveys to describe and catalog the natural communities that occur in Rhode Island based on the physical environment, climate, and natural disturbance regime. It remains an essential guide for conserving the full array of biodiversity in the state; however, the NCRI does not include anthropogenic communities (those created by and persisting under the influence of humans) and not all natural communities can be mapped with existing data.
The Rhode Island Ecological Communities Classification (RIECC; Enser et al. 2011) identifies recurring groups of biological communities found in similar physical environments and influenced by similar dynamic ecological processes, such as fire or flooding. It is an amalgamation of the NCRI and the regional NETHCS described above, the latter of which provides guidance in assigning anthropogenic (cultural) communities not included in the NCRI. The RIECC provides a classification framework that can be mapped, often from remote imagery, and is also readily identifiable by conservation and resource managers in the field. Ecological systems are vegetation-based and may be described as a collection of plant associations that occur together in some combination on the ground. Individually, these associations can be used as relatively fine-scale mapping units that can be useful in characterizing a specific area and driving local management decisions but may not be amenable to mapping at a regional scale. This classification was designed to support development of a detailed ecological communities map and database serving multiple conservation needs in Rhode Island, including the RIWAP.

## UPLANDS

This system consists of upland or terrestrial communities, forested and non-forested, which have welldrained soils and plants that persist without a lot of water, such as from a seasonally high-water table. Although there is more forest acreage in Rhode Island today than there was 100 years ago, few areas contain core habitats large enough to support the full complement of expected species and natural ecosystem processes. Key characteristics that determine a forest's value for breeding bird habitat, for example, are its size and shape, nearness to other forest tracts, and surrounding land use. In contrast, Rhode Island forest patches are becoming smaller and more isolated, primarily due to fragmentation caused by development. Forests in the Northeast region are fragmented by 732,000 miles of permanent roads. On average, 43% of forests occur in blocks less than 5,000 acres that are completely encircled by major roads, resulting in an almost 60% loss of local connectivity. Current patterns indicate that HABITATS securing land has been an effective strategy for preventing further fragmentation as there is a high proportion of conserved land within most of the remaining large contiguous forest blocks. There is strong evidence that the largest forest tracts, ranging up to thousands of hectares, support the highest diversity of forest species. Rhode Island's Conservation Opportunity Area mapping shows the distribution of forest blocks greater than 500 acres in size (RIDEM 2025) and illustrates the degree to which forests have been fragmented in Rhode Island. The continuing threat of fragmentation of Rhode Island's forests is addressed in Chapter 3 of this document.

## FORESTED UPLANDS

## Situated in the southern

In addition to spatial characteristics, the age and structure of forests influence the composition and diversity of their plant and animal communities. Historically, forests of interior New England have undergone frequent, small-scale natural disturbances in the form of tree wind-throws that removed individual trees or groups of trees, resulting in canopy breaks. Major catastrophic disturbances from hurricanes and wildfires occurred much less frequently. Natural disturbances create forests that are structurally diverse, with dense nesting cover at the shrub and ground levels that support a higher diversity of forest-nesting birds.
Following European colonization and the rise and fall of agriculture, an estimated 30% of lost woodlands were restored through natural succession of abandoned fields. As a result, almost all the state's current forests are second-growth and estimated to average only 60 years old with little age class or structural diversity; trees less than 5" in diameter comprise only 10% of Northern Hardwood and Central Oak-Pine forests, and no forest stands in the regional were dominated by old trees or trees more than 20" in diameter (USDA USFS -FIA program). Today's forests are concentrated in the western part of the state, where RIDEM has several large, preserved tracts and development pressures have been lower than in the highly urbanized coastal areas. Nevertheless, forestland is increasingly fragmented throughout the state as more of it is converted to residential use. Along with the general maturation of Rhode Island forests, there has been a reduction in understory vegetation critical for ground-nesting birds. This change is partially the result of a proliferating Whitetailed Deer population which contributes to the reduction of understory shrub and herbaceous plant cover through selective browsing. The creation of non-forested dispersal barriers has prevented some plant species from recolonizing, resulting in a further decline of understory and ground layer plant diversity. In such cases, even mature forests are still paying an "extinction debt" as small populations decline and disappear due to fragmentation and are not replaced 
(Vellend et al. 2007
).
Although forest ownership by public agencies and nonprofit conservation groups has increased in recent decades, private individuals continue to own more than half of Rhode Island's forest land, with the majority of owners holding less than 10 acres. This overall ownership pattern complicates the management of species that require relatively large tracts of habitat. Recognizing these issues, RIDEM has actively pursued the acquisition of forest land, focusing on tracts that increase the size of existing Wildlife Management Areas.
Statistics on forest cover in Rhode Island generally do not account for small woodlands that remain within the most heavily developed sections of the state, such as in municipal parks, cemeteries, hospital grounds, schools, and other marginally protected sites. These habitats are an example of ruderal forests, ruderal being a reference to plants that grow on ground damaged by human activity.
The species composition here is so heavily comprised of non-native plants that it is not recognizable as any native forest type. Despite these changes, ruderal forests support a surprisingly high number of species such as canopy and cavity-nesting birds, serve as valuable resting and feeding areas for migrating birds, and are often associated with small wetlands, especially Red Maple swamps.

## NON-FORESTED UPLANDS

Under natural circumstances, non-forested uplands are created by disturbances to the forest which can be due to regular daily conditions (e.g., coastal wind and salt spray), or occasional catastrophic events such as hurricanes, floods, and lighting strike wildfires. Before European settlement, indigenous peoples periodically burned areas to maintain their openness, but after settlement most of Rhode Island was cleared for agricultural and other purposes. During the past century the landscape has been continually altered by clearing, regrowth of forest, and subsequently by more clearing, which has resulted in a wide distribution of non-forested habitats in various stages of successional development throughout the state. Approximately 38,000 acres of the state are presently non-forested upland habitats.

## HABITATS EARLY SUCCESSIONAL HABITATS

Early successional habitats are defined as uplands where the potential natural vegetation is predominantly grasses, grass-like plants, forbs, or shrubs 
(Anderson et al. 1976
). Ancestrally, these habitats would have developed in openings created within the predominantly forested landscape by natural disturbances like severe storms and fires. Today, most old field habitat is created and maintained on state land, other conserved lands, and private properties under management agreements. Other places where early successional habitats are fostered include logging sites and utility rights-of-way (electric powerlines and gas pipelines) 
(Enser, pers. comm., March 5, 2014)
.
The condition of early successional habitats depends on the reference point used to make the assessment. During the pre-settlement period, when natural disturbances governed the amount and distribution of shrublands, this habitat comprised an estimated 3% of inland habitats. Near the coast, with regular maritime winds and a higher storm frequency, shrublands were more extensive, comprising more than 15% of coastal forests, and populations of shrubland animals probably reached their greatest densities in these habitats (Litvaitis 2003).
Shrubland wildlife significantly benefited from the increase in old field habitat that reached its peak circa 1870 when less than 30% of the state was forested. Since the mid to late 1800's, there has been a steady decline in shrubland habitat as old fields succeed to forest and old farms are converted to residential and commercial developments. According to Buffum (2011), 3.3% of the state is currently shrubland habitat, although coverage varies widely. Coastal communities, where natural shrublands reach their greatest extent, have the highest coverage, for example Block Island (31%) and Jamestown (15%). Inland areas average roughly 2% shrub cover, mostly anthropogenic early successional types. Approximately 82% of the powerlines in Rhode Island were classified as shrublands based on the descriptive methodology used (Buffum 2011).
Wildlife managers cannot rely on natural disturbances to maintain or create enough shrubland habitat to support certain SGCN that require it. 
Litviatis (2003)
 points out that human approaches to do so should not jeopardize species affiliated with other habitats, especially mature forests, and efforts to provide shrubland habitats in human-dominated landscapes should incorporate existing modified lands to avoid additional habitat fragmentation. Large, clustered patches of shrubland habitat may be more practical and beneficial in coastal areas where the creation and maintenance of ruderal shrublands can augment naturally maintained shrubland habitats.

## HABITATS AGRICULTURAL LANDS

The most valued agricultural lands for wildlife are the grassland habitats provided by hayfields and pastures that support a distinct assemblage of nesting birds and invertebrates. Although the origin and history of this fauna in southern New England is conjectural, the conversion of much of the Rhode Island landscape to agriculture by 1850 created significant acreages of grassland habitat and consequent increases in grassland species. However, unlike the naturally maintained grasslands of the Midwest, grassland habitats in Rhode Island are ephemeral and dependent on continuing agricultural practices, prescribed burns, or other management practices to maintain open, grassy conditions. In 1908, there were still more than 250,000 acres of farmland in Rhode Island (Griffiths 1965). Today, agricultural grasslands are widely scattered throughout the state, although most are concentrated near the coast, especially on Block Island; on Aquidneck and Conanicut Islands in Narragansett Bay; and in the seaside communities of Tiverton and Little Compton. State-listed grassland birds, including Barn Owl, Northern Harrier, and Grasshopper Sparrow, are most prevalent on Block Island where the largest patches of grassland habitat are found and where there is also a lack of mammalian predators.
Grassland species have declined because much of the original farmland was abandoned or more recently sold and converted to other uses. According to the USDA Census of Agriculture, there were 1,054 farms in Rhode Island in 2022, down from the 1,243 reported in 2012. Correspondingly, there was a 15% decrease in farmed acreage during the same period, from 69,589 to 59,076 acres (USDA 2022).
In addition, farmland is managed more intensively today to maximize production, with more frequent haying schedules often coinciding with the nesting periods of grassland birds. The amount of grassland in contiguous blocks is also critical to determining the value of a specific tract of land for these grassland species. For example, Upland Sandpiper and Grasshopper Sparrow generally do not inhabit grasslands less than 50 acres in size and, considering the average farm size is 56 acres and 43% of farms are less than 10 acres in size, distribution of these birds is mostly limited to larger non-agricultural grasslands found at airports and military reservations (USDA 2022). The predominant estuarine habitat in Rhode Island is salt marsh, which is a mosaic of communities dominated by plants adapted to varying salinity levels that are maintained by the degree of daily inundation of saltwater (NWI 2014). The largest salt marsh complexes develop within protected coves, bays, and salt ponds, but about 10% of these habitats are fringe marshes less than 5 yards wide and mostly found along the upper portions of Narragansett Bay and its tidal tributaries.

## WETLANDS

Fringe marsh habitat is vulnerable to coastal development and the use of "hard" materials like concrete, metal, and stone to protect eroding shorelines. Over 30% of Rhode Island's coastal shoreline has been displaced by hard materials (Freedman 2012).
Salt marshes are universally considered to be among the most important wildlife habitats in North America, and Rhode Island's contribution to the regional distribution and conservation of this habitat is significant. Partners in Flight (PIF) have identified maritime marshes as the habitat that harbors the largest number of high priority birds in the region. In 2010, the National Audubon Society Important Bird Areas (IBAs) program designated 16 IBAs in Rhode Island that are primarily salt marsh habitats (National Audubon Society 2014) selected because they support saltmarsh sparrow and piping plover, considered by PIF as the two birds of highest conservation priority in this region.
Brackish marshes occur in the upper reaches of tidal rivers and the upland edges of salt marshes where salinity levels are reduced. These marshes support a graminoid plant community similar to that found in salt marshes, but reduced salinity levels support a higher diversity of plants. Brackish marshes are primarily transitional habitats that occupy limited areas for a combined total of less than 300 acres statewide. This figure is much reduced from the pre-settlement period before dams were constructed at the mouths of most rivers in the state that restricted tidal flow. Old Mill Creek is the only remaining tidal waterway exhibiting the natural progression of tidal wetlands from saline to fresh marsh. Brackish marshes dominated by cattails are also present in several ponds along the south shore of Washington County and within the Narrow River system (Enser and Lundgren 2006).
Because of their rarity and limited extent, brackish marsh communities are extremely vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, especially rising sea level. Application of SLAMM (Sea Level Affecting Marshes Model) modeling at several federal wildlife refuges in the Northeast projected that the initial impact of sea level rise will be an increase in salt marsh (saline) habitats at the expense of brackish habitats (Grund and Walberg 2013). In 2015, SLAMM modeling was published for the entire Rhode Island coast with similar impacts indicated and the degree of brackish marsh loss dependent on the accessibility of adjacent upland and/or freshwater wetland sites for inland marsh migration.
More than 50% (approximately 4,000 acres) of the estuarine marshes present in Rhode Island at the time of European settlement have been lost 
(Cowardin et al. 1979)
, primarily by draining or filling to provide sites for coastal development. Moreover, most currently existing estuarine marshes have been ditched and sprayed for mosquito control. These impacts have been reduced or eliminated to varying degrees, but estuarine habitats face a newer threat; climate change vulnerability assessments consistently identify brackish marshes as the most vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, especially rising sea level. The major causes of impairment in coastal and estuarine waters of Rhode Island are low dissolved oxygen and pathogens, specifically bacteria. As a result, marine and estuarine waters are exhibiting an increased array of nutrient-associated symptoms, including fish kills, eelgrass loss, macroalgae blooms, and a gradual shift in the dominant fish communities from benthic (i.e., bottom-dwelling) to pelagic (i.e., water column-dwelling) species (RIDEM OWR 2024). The major sources of bacterial contamination are combined sewer overflows (CSOs) in certain locations, including the Upper Bay and Newport Harbor, and storm water discharges. Discharges from wastewater treatment facilities, CSOs, failing on-site wastewater systems, and urban runoff are the major sources of nutrient enrichment, which is integral to low dissolved oxygen problems in the estuarine portions of the Providence and Seekonk Rivers (RIDEM OWR 2024). While no waters were fully delisted for impairments during OWR's most recent reporting cycle, estuarine waterbody segments within Narragansett Bay in particularly have seen considerable improvement and may have the potential to be delisted in future assessment cycles, if trends in water quality continue (RIDEM OWR 2024).
RIDEM OWR evaluates the capability of estuarine habitats to support their designated human-uses, and the most recent evaluation of nearly 100% of these habitats (157 sq. miles) was conducted in 2024. This evaluation determined that approximately 36% (57 sq. miles) of the total estuarine habitats are impaired for one or more of their designated uses, similar to the 35% impaired in 2012.
RIDEM has also assessed the condition of approximately 79 miles of the marine shoreline, and none of these waters were found impaired for swimming or shellfishing (RIDEM OWR 2024). The relative condition of waters near Block Island is frequently monitored by URI. RIDEM monitors recreational and commercial fishing in the state's marine waters, collaborating with the National Marine Fisheries Service and other partners to manage fish stocks and habitats as needed.

## AQUATIC HABITAT SYSTEMS

Aquatic habitats are those of flowing or impounded fresh waters where emergent plants are sparse or lacking, but that may include areas with abundant submerged or floating-leaved vegetation. 

## HABITATS

The characteristic fish species of these streams is the eastern brook trout, along with fallfish, blacknose dace, and longnose dace. There is also a unique macroinvertebrate fauna of mayflies, stoneflies, caddisflies, midges, craneflies, blackflies, dragonflies and damselflies, crayfish, and mollusks.
Rhode Island's largest river, the Blackstone, is the only classified as medium-sized, with a warm water system and an average bank-full width of 115 feet. Portions of this river are higher gradient with colder temperatures, but lower sections show the more typical pattern of higher sinuosity, broader floodplain valleys, associated riparian wetlands, and lower width/depth ratios. Fish communities are dominated by warm-water species, especially those able to cope with the legacy of pollution and other degrading impacts the Blackstone River has sustained during its history as the first industrialized river in The Americas. The largest river system within the state is the Wood-Pawcatuck, which drains most of the southwestern portion of the state and portions of Connecticut.
There are 1,410 river miles in Rhode Island of which 1,112 miles (81%) were monitored in 2024 to assess their condition and ability to support aquatic life, fish consumption, water supply, and recreational uses. Overall, about half (44.2%) of the miles assessed were found to fully support aquatic life; however, approximately 35% are designated as impaired (RIDEM OWR 2024).
Pathogens are the major cause of impairment, originating from point and non-point sources such as CSOs, seepage from failing septic systems, runoff during storm events, and agriculture, as well as from natural sources (e.g., waterfowl). Other significant causes of impairment include metals and nuisance exotic species (RIDEM OWR 2024). As the RIDEM OWR Aquatic Invasive Species program has expanded monitoring efforts, invasive plants have been found to be a management issue in rivers and streams (see Chapter 3). Another noted cause of non-support is from low levels of metals that arrive in aquatic systems from a variety of sources including permitted industrial and municipal discharges, CSOs, and storm drains. Another potential source of low-level metals, one that is not routinely evaluated and characterized, is contaminated sediments. Non-point sources such as urban runoff and sources from outside the state's borders are also significant contributors of metals in Rhode Island's rivers (RIDEM OWR 2024).

## LAKES AND PONDS

The Pelagic habitat is the dominant type in Rhode Island coastal-marine waters. It is a dynamic environment with tidally and wind-driven circulation. A wide variety of plankton and nekton are found in open waters and provide food for a diverse assemblage of transient finfish, marine mammals, and sea turtles. The health of pelagic habitats is directly tied to seafloor habitats which are essential to fish and invertebrate spawning, foraging, resting and hiding from predators. Over 82% of Rhode Island's seafloor is composed of soft sediments which provide important nursery and spawning areas for commercially important fish such as winter flounder and American lobster.
Soft sediments support a diverse community of mollusks, crabs, and worms that live in and on the sediments. Tube-dwelling anemones, polychaetes, and amphipods can form dense mats in this habitat, modifying the seafloor structure and environment.
Rocky habitats, which include both natural and artificial rocky reefs, make up only a small percentage of marine habitats in Rhode Island waters. Typically, the hard or rocky substrate will be covered with sessile invertebrates, including sponges, bryozoans, corals, mollusks, and anemones, as well as mobile invertebrates such as polychaete worms, mollusks, and crustaceans. Sessile invertebrates provide food and shelter for many bottom-feeding fish. For numerous species, this area may also provide nursery or spawning habitat. Artificial reefs, piers, wrecks and other man-made structures are important additions to this community type, as they sometimes supplement natural reefs.
Various Rhode Island agencies have the authority to protect and restore marine areas.

While the habitats essential for wildlife haven't changed, the amount and conditions of some of these habitats, as well as some of the threats they face, have. This context was assessed and updated by RIDEM staff, then reviewed and improved by additional experts and collaborators during partner review.
Simultaneously, the Technical Team reviewed existing SGCN habitat associations and assigned all new SGCN to one or more habitats. The RIWAP describes habitats at two different levels; the more general, overarching habitats (e.g., Oak Forest) are referred to as Key Habitats, while the more precise communities they contain (e.g., Mixed Oak/Hickory Forest, White Oak/Mountain Laurel Forest) are referred to as Sub-habitats.
Except for freshwater rivers and streams and in the marine environment, these sub-habitats are very closely tied to the RIECC.
The accompanying database provides description and condition narratives and location information for each of the 37 key habitats. At the sub-habitat level, the database further includes current condition (excellent, good, fair, poor, unknown), biodiversity importance (high, medium, low), degree of threat (high, medium, low), and, for most, the specific threat from climate change (high, medium, low).
Habitat and sub-habitat associations for all SGCN are also presented. Since no habitat classification will perfectly capture the needs of such a broad swath of species (e.g., the current classification works well for Pine Barren specialists, while the needs of species like Chimney Swifts don't conform well to vegetative classes, as they inhabit cavities that have long been largely provided by artificial structures in the built landscape), habitat needs may also be presented by guild.
The above parameters of habitat health and condition were assessed using the best information available from RIDEM and its partners, along with assessments from other databases and the published literature. The RIWAP describes 37 key habitats from tidal salt marsh to oak forest, which are then further divided into sub-habitats with additional detail on characteristic species. Seven estuarine/marine habitats that overlap with other existing habitats have been removed from the RIWAP to provide clearer, mutually exclusive Sub-habitat classifications.
Maps were produced for key habitats and some sub-habitats according to the best geospatial data available. In most cases, this remains the RIECC mapping used for the 2015 RIWAP, and there is still insufficient information to accurately map many of the rare habitats because of their limited distribution, small size and the resolution and accuracy limits of available spatial data.

## Threats and Actions

## INTRODUCTION

This chapter discusses the most pressing threats to Rhode Island's SGCN and their key habitats and the priority conservation actions that best address those threats. The word "threat" is used here to describe all aspects of the processes by which human actions or natural events may jeopardize fish and wildlife species and their habitats. Rhode Island's species and habitats face numerous threats that may negatively impact them and compromise their ability to thrive in the state. Some threats are global or national in scale, while others may be regional, statewide, or local. Most of Rhode Island's wildlife populations are subject to multiple threats associated with human activities, and the SGCN list identifies the most vulnerable.
The stewardship of wildlife and natural areas is an important part of the overall mission of RIDEM, but implementation of all needed conservation actions will require the efforts of many conservation partners working together to incorporate the needs of SGCN and key habitats into their programs and plans throughout the next decade. Use and dissemination of this information will enable the important step of incorporating it into land use decisions and key conservation efforts across the state.

## IDENTIFYING SGCN AND KEY HABITAT THREATS AND ASSOCIATED CONSERVATION ACTIONS

Through a series of workshops, teams of experts reviewed and reevaluated the threats listed in the 2015 RIWAP and added new threats in accordance with protocols adapted from the updated NE Lexicon (Crisfield and NEFWDTC 2022). A similar process was conducted for updating and assigning actions to threats. The RIWAP employs a multi-tiered format to establish priorities for implementation, from overarching, statewide conservation needs (Tier 1), to Taxa-wide (Tier 2), those focused on a particular type of Habitat (Tier 3), or species (Tier 4). As such, coupled threats and actions are listed at four hierarchical levels, or Tiers (Table 
3-1)
.
Tier 1 threats are those that are prevalent across Rhode Island's species and habitats, and accompanying Tier 1 conservation actions are those that can be implemented throughout Rhode Island and have potential to affect all species and habitats (e.g., program coordination, land acquisition, education, pollution prevention and mitigation). Many actions that address regional needs, especially Regional Conservation Need (RCN) and other Northeast collective initiatives like coordinated regional data collection and management, are high priority and included in this tier.
Tier 2 includes threats and actions that have a high potential to affect groups of multiple speciesreferred to as taxa wide in the RIWAP. In many cases, data needs, research projects, and the creation of digital GIS coverage are appropriate for a suite of species. These threats and actions are considered more general and are largely independent of habitat boundaries, such as bird collisions with buildings. Many of the most important, highest priority threats and conservation actions are contained in the first two tiers because actions can extend across species and habitat boundaries. Northeast Regional Species of Greatest Conservation Need (RSGCN) and RCN priority species are included here.
Tiers 3 and 4 provide finer differentiation of actions as well as focal areas which help target and direct the statewide and taxa level (Tier 1 and 2) actions on the landscape. This SWAP revision relies heavily on key habitat identification as a form of "coarse filter" approach to address the needs of groups of SGCN.
Tier 3 provides additional differentiation. Where meaningful, species (especially invertebrates and birds) were grouped by habitats (e.g., pitch pine moths, shellfish reef invertebrates, or forest birds). This served to highlight interrelationships, which often include similar suites of threats and actions, and help further target conservation work. Some species groupings (e.g., moths and plants) also adopted a focal species approach to keep the SGCN list more manageable for taxa with far more species than resources to conserve them. Conservation actions developed for these focal species would then address the wider array of species in that same habitat or other functional grouping (RIWAP database).

## HABITAT LOSS, FRAGMENTATION, AND DEGRADATION OF KEY HABITAT

A primary threat to Rhode Island's fish, wildlife, and their habitats is loss of natural areas to human development (e.g., buildings, parking lots, roads, energy, and recreational uses Fragmentation subdivides large contiguous areas of natural land into smaller patches, resulting in each patch having more edge habitat and less interior. This can be a problem for many species, particularly species that rely on large patches of the same plant community or vegetative structure (e.g., interior forest for Marbled Salamander, Wood Thrush, and Scarlet Tanager). Thus, fragmentation can lead to an overall deterioration of ecological quality and integrity, and a shift in associated species from interior specialists to edge generalists.
Because the future of Rhode Island's wildlife will rely on the amount of habitat remaining, fee acquisition and easements that protect land will continue to be among the most important components of this SWAP. Acquisition, in conjunction with effective preserve design and focal area approaches (see below), has the greatest potential to protect the most species for the longest time.
No other suite of strategies can mitigate for a lack of habitat, so land protection strategies are key. To effectively conserve all SGCN, some wildlife preserves will need to be continually managed to reduce detrimental impacts associated with poor habitat quality, increased predation, nest parasitism by Brown-headed Cowbirds, contamination from outside sources, invasive species, human disturbance, and a changing climate.
Advanced spatial planning and prioritization is a critical first step.

## INVASIVE AND OTHER PROBLEMATIC SPECIES

The spread of invasive species poses a significant threat to SGCN throughout the Northeast. Invasive species go by many names, but all are introduced, non-indigenous organisms that can aggressively displace and overtake populations of native species and devastate natural habitats. These invasives are most often plants, and most SGCN are indirectly impacted by their spread. The changes most evident in an invaded community are declines in native plant diversity and a consequent restructuring of the community that very often negatively alters habitats of SGCN. Animals, such as non-native crabs in marsh systems, invasive invertebrates like Asian shore crabs and fouling tunicates like Didemnum in marine environments, outdoor cats that kill 1.3-4 billion birds and 6. The Rhode Island Invasive Species Council (RIISC) maintains a list of plants considered invasive, and both freshwater aquatic and marine invasives have been tracked for decades. Still, resources do not match the scale of the problem, and Rhode Island does not have comprehensive legislation to prevent even intentional introductions of invasive species not otherwise subject to prohibitions. Prevention is far more cost-effective than control once a species has taken hold in an area.
While invasives are, by definition, non-native species, "problematic" species can include opportunistic native species that can disrupt more sensitive native species when ecological systems get out of balance. Examples include Brown-headed Cowbird, a nest parasite; carnivores raiding bird and turtle nests; and deer over browsing native plant communities. Human disturbance is closely linked to this threat, whether it is from newly colonizing species or native species out of balance. Brown-headed Cowbirds are very successful in fragmented forests; carnivores like skunks and raccoons thrive alongside humans, where they are subsidized when we leave pet food outside or trash unsecured; and the elimination of larger predators and fragmentation of habitats by humans has contributed to the high density of deer in Southern New England.
Like the negative impacts of development, the best way to address the impacts of invasive and problematic species is to first plan to avoid as many as possible. For example, RIDEM has implemented protocols since the 2015 Plan to minimize the spread of problematic plants, animals, and pathogens during survey work that mandate cleaning and sterilization of gear between visits to different vernal pools. And Northeast states have increased their coordination and information sharing to maximize efficiency preventing and addressing disease outbreaks. The RIISC, which had not been active since 2005, reconvened and updated its list of invasive and weedy plants in Rhode Island in 2020, and several RIDEM, partners, and other volunteer programs and partnerships monitor terrestrial, aquatic, and marine invasive species in different habitats or throughout Rhode Island. Still, a terrestrial equivalent to the state's Aquatic Species Management Plan has yet to be completed, and full prevention would include an institutionalized framework and increased efforts to promote the appreciation, restoration, and stewardship of our native ecology.

## NATURAL SYSTEM MODIFICATIONS

Natural system modifications are defined as conversions or degradations of habitat in service of "managing" natural or seminatural systems, often to improve human welfare 
(Salafsky et al. 2008)
. The damming of rivers and streams is an example of a direct habitat modification, but some modifications involve the suppression of natural disturbances that, if allowed to occur under normal frequencies and intensities, could result in damage to property and/or loss of life, such as fire or flooding. This suppression or management of natural disturbance often results in the minimization or elimination of processes essential to the maintenance of natural communities.

## THREATS AND ACTIONS

While lightning-strike fires are relatively rare events in southern New England compared to much of the country, Rhode Island nevertheless has fire-dependent habitats, particularly pitch pine. Although there is little historical evidence that fire was purposely used to generate sizable areas of open habitat (Foster and Motzkin 2002), Indigenous peoples employed fire as a land management tool across the region, and it is apparent that such selective burning helped create and maintain firedependent habitats in southern New England, especially Pitch Pine barrens. Lack of fire in Pitch Pine barrens results in succession of these communities with an increase in hardwoods and reduction in Scrub Oak understories that support the unique assemblage of plants and animals characteristic of barrens habitats, many of which depend on fire to reduce competition from fire-intolerant species or to stimulate a physiological response, such as the high temperatures needed to release the seeds from Pitch Pine cones. Recognition of this consequence of fire suppression has spurred conservation organizations and agencies throughout the Northeast to conduct controlled burns to manage Pitch Pine barrens and other fire dependent habitats, and RIDEM continues to work to expand its capacity and outreach to conduct controlled burns as appropriate.
Another common threat to many aquatic species and wetland habitats is water management, including dam construction, surface water diversion, withdrawals from surface and groundwater sources, and other operations that alter water flow patterns from their natural range of variation either deliberately or as a result of other activities. Rhode Island has a long history of water management, particularly for mills and reservoirs. Of particular concern to fish and wildlife resources is the construction of dams on major rivers that has prevented the migration of anadromous fish to inland breeding locations and impeded the inland flow of tidal waters, resulting in the nearly total loss of Freshwater Tidal Marshes.

## POLLUTION

The Northeast consists of some of the highest population densities and oldest buildings and infrastructure. The combination of large metropolitan areas, thriving industries, often out-of-date septic and wastewater systems, and legacy contaminants generates significant amounts of waste in the form of household sewage, solid waste, industrial effluents, and leachate. Pollutants from these sources impair key riparian, aquatic, and terrestrial habitats throughout the region. Changes in water quality and quantity pose serious threats to all northeastern aquatic systems. As urbanization increases and natural vegetated buffers decline, natural filters for toxins are lost and they make their way directly into surface waters in runoff and sediments. The evidence for negative impacts from noise and light pollution are accumulating as well, and the use of outdoor lighting has grown exponentially 
(Kyba et al. 2017)
. As natural areas and canopy cover decline, the impacts of noise and light pollution also travel further across the landscape.
One recurrent pollution threat in the RIWAP is pesticides. By their nature, substances that are designed to kill pests can pose risks to humans and the environment. The organic farming community in the state remains a bright spot and factor in more recent curtailments of pesticides (including herbicides) and fertilizers, but improvements are still needed. Additionally, excessive use of fertilizers and pesticides by homeowners and other land managers is still a nearly ubiquitous threat across the state and actions to raise awareness, restrict by law or policy, and incentivize behavior change are found in the RIWAP database.
In the marine environment, drifting debris and microplastics also pose serious threats to wildlife.
Drifting marine debris can result in entanglements of marine mammals, fish, and sea turtles.
Microplastics have been found extensively in Narragansett Bay, outflowing rivers and marine sediments and have been shown to accumulate in the tissues of some species.

## HUMAN DISTURBANCE

This category includes threats from human activities that alter, destroy and disturb habitats and species associated with non-consumptive uses of biological resources 
(Salafsky et al. 2008)
. In a state with a high population density, the threat of human disturbance to many fish and wildlife species and their habitats can be considerable, and those species most sensitive to human presence are among the most vulnerable and most challenging to protect. In Rhode Island, most existing preserves are not large enough to support vulnerable species, due to pressures from public use and indirect influences from surrounding land uses. Therefore, a strategy of increasing the size of protected preserves is appropriate. RIDEM has typically prioritized new acquisition projects by their adjacency to existing open space, or that are inholdings within protected lands. Historically, this has been done largely to preserve or expand traditional recreational activities, but the spatial requirements of some of Rhode Island's SGCN has increased the urgency of preserving large parcels of contiguous habitat.
If acquisitions are prioritized with wildlife conservation as a primary goal, then issues such as habitat management, additive mortality of animals, and connectivity can be more strategically addressed.
Where public access is provided, it can be routed to skirt relatively remote habitat cores.
There are numerous examples of this threat that extend beyond insufficient preserve size. For example, maritime beaches and the unique suite of species they support are vulnerable because of the extreme use of these habitats for human recreation. Beach-nesting birds, including piping plover and least tern, are particularly vulnerable because human disturbance often curtails breeding success.
Foot and vehicular traffic may crush nests or young and excessive disturbance may cause the parents to desert the nest, exposing eggs or chicks to the summer sun and predators. Pets, especially dogs, may harass the birds, and developments near beaches provide food that attracts increased numbers of predators such as raccoons, skunks, and foxes. Additionally, compaction from driving on beaches was thought to be the primary cause for the loss of the northeastern beach tiger beetle from Rhode Island and that led to its listing as a federally threatened species. Interior sand flats and barrens also support rare tiger beetles, and even protected sites may be overrun and degraded by illegal off-road vehicle use. More aggressive protection of such habitat types is clearly indicated. The broader issue of human intrusions and disturbance across habitats will require education and outreach to increase awareness and creative solutions that balance minimal disturbance to critical habitats with thoughtful enjoyment of natural lands.

## NEW ENERGY DEVELOPMENTS

The Block Island Wind Farm, the nation's first offshore wind farm, was installed since the 2015 RIWAP was published. Terrestrial wind projects have slowed as municipalities have begun to enact guardrails on this type of development. The rapid expansion of industrial scale solar panel installations is a more recent concern, primarily because of the land they consume. Wind turbines can pose a threat to birds, although they appear to be a relatively minor source of concern compared to other sources of fatality at the current rate of development (e.g., collisions with buildings and communication towers and predation by cats). The degree of threat to bats is still not fully understood, but there have been developments in avoidance technology (curtailment), which are recommended to be included in permitting in the RIWAP as well as an in-process Guidelines for Wildlife Management and Renewable Energy in the Northeast commissioned as part of a larger RCN project (Canino and Grodsky, in progress). Offshore wind poses both threats and potential benefits to a variety of marine species. Construction presents with the possibility of pile-driving noise, habitat disturbance or destruction, and increased vessel activity in the area, while operation creates different anthropogenic changes such as operational noise, generation of electromagnetic fields, and added hardbottom habitat. This is an area of active research, as effects of offshore wind development on species in the Northwest Atlantic are not well understood at this time.

## LACK OF INFORMATION, PLANNING, AND CAPACITY

Although not direct threats, lack of information, planning, and capacity, including capacity for muchneeded outreach and coordination, are some of the most important stumbling blocks to conservation. Accurate information on the distribution, status, and ecology of SGCN and key habitats is critical to respond appropriately and meaningfully to identified threats. Bright spots include the publication of books on the reptiles, amphibians, Odonates, and birds of Rhode Island, the development of community science apps (e.g., Herp Observer, Bee Observer, Songbird Mortality Report), and participatory science with trained volunteers (e.g., RI Bird Atlas 2.0 and the Rhode Island Bumble Bee and Wild Bee Surveys), all of which have increased access to distribution data. However, primarily due to budgetary constraints, there has been little accomplished regarding the acquisition and management of data to complete conservation actions identified in the RIWAP. The same is true with the need for outreach and dissemination of information and capacity to carry out needed conservation and management actions. This problem is greatly exacerbated for the more than half of SGCN that are not traditionally hunted or fished, as the only consistent and meaningful funding for all but mammals, birds, and finfish across RIDEM is SWG. This funding has remained relatively static, while the species in need and cost of doing this work have increased substantially.
The number of priority needs and actions identified during this process will require significant effort within RIDEM. However, the extent to which it can be implemented is also dependent upon external factors outside of RIDEM's control. Specific actions that will better enable RIDEM to implement this RIWAP include increasing advocacy for conservation, increasing agency resources and capacity for conservation, and establishing dedicated state funding for work on SGCN and their key habitats.

## NEXT STEPS-FOCAL AREAS

The COA process should be considered preliminary and dynamic with additional work needed to improve and advance this mapping. A key action from 2015 that remains to be undertaken is to develop focal area and spatially specific focal species approaches in Rhode Island to inform a more data driven COA map.
The focal area approach is a form of biological triage. Focal areas should be large and shaped roughly like circles or squares (to maximize roadless core areas). This approach does not claim that small preserves near urban centers are without biological value -certainly some species will persist in those places. However, if the RIWAP is to protect viable populations of all species at the state scale, then the more difficult challenges will lie with species that require large tracts of contiguous habitat. Some species will not be preserved because we have already lost the opportunity to provide large enough areas. There are no clear answers to how much area and how many individuals are needed to ensure the long-term persistence of any given population and this varies dramatically by species.
Measuring population viability for a species requires the spatial requirements and population structure but population models are unavailable for most species and historical data, even to the presenceabsence level, can be unreliable. However, because vegetation communities and their spatial attributes can help predict areas that may contain viable fish and wildlife populations, those metrics can be used to identify focal areas.
First, focal areas must be large. When considering forest habitats for example, research has shown that many forest interior birds require several thousand acres of unfragmented habitat, and planning for focal areas of this size in Rhode Island is a difficult process. Second, the approach should be stratified according to the various land masses of Rhode Island. For example, all large islands should be treated independently -a focal approach on Block Island would occur at a different spatial scale than one for western Rhode Island. Lastly, focal areas should have roadless cores as large as possible and should ideally, but not necessarily, have a significant proportion of protected acreage. A model focal area will have a core of protected land surrounded by a buffer zone of varying extent where the goal will be to maximize the integrity of the overall preserve. The present COA map is a step in the right direction and contains many elements that would inform a more precise tailoring of Rhode Island's spatial priorities by marrying the landscape geometry and habitat information of the current version of the map with more detailed spatial data for focal species.

## INTRODUCTION

Monitoring Rhode Island's SGCN, their habitats, and conservation outcomes is necessary for RIDEM and its partners to determine the effectiveness of conservation actions to address threats facing the state's fish and wildlife. As conditions change (e.g., land use patterns, climate change, global or national population trends, new data), adaptive management and implementation of RIWAP conservation actions identified in the RIWAP database will enable RIDEM to respond appropriately and make efficient use of limited staffing and funds. Rhode Island's approach identifies monitoring efforts and tools currently used by RIDEM or its partners to assess SGCN, key habitats and related issues, as listed in the accompanying RIWAP database.
If monitoring is not identified for an SGCN or species group/taxa, the database describes monitoring actions for other species that occupy the same habitats and/or for their habitats; these recommendations are prioritized to benefit the overall habitat, community, or assemblage, including many other SGCN. In cases where not enough information exists to monitor a species or group, or for which monitoring protocols have not yet been developed, this need is documented and followed by a conservation action intended to address that information need. This is true for some taxa, including many invertebrate groups for which standardized protocols need to be developed and baseline data do not exist to form the basis of a monitoring protocol. As information gaps are filled, any relevant monitoring can be adapted to be more quantitative and specific.
This chapter describes how Rhode Island will use tools for information management and conservation planning to track the implementation and effectiveness of conservation actions through an adaptive management framework. These tools, which are inclusive of and build upon previous frameworks such as the Northeast Regional Monitoring and Performance Reporting Framework (NEAFWA 2008) and Measuring the Effectiveness of State Wildlife Grants (AFWA 2011), are the Northeast Lexicon Project (Crisfield and NEFWDTC 2022), and the USFWS's national Wildlife Tracking and Reporting Actions for the Conservation of Species (Wildlife TRACS). ## IMPORTANT DATA GAPS IN RHODE ISLAND

With limited resources available to support monitoring programs, it is simply not possible to monitor many aspects of the natural or human environment relevant to fish and wildlife conservation efforts.
Still, it is possible to identify high-priority target areas where additional data would be helpful for developing management prescriptions for fish and wildlife species and their habitats in Rhode Island.
The RIWAP addresses data gaps extensively through identified threats and actions, and some high priority data gaps identified by taxa experts, planners, and stakeholders through iterative RIWAP review, as well as progress toward filling them, are described below. RIDEM and partners continue to work together to develop monitoring programs to address these gaps including species, taxa, habitat and community-level monitoring. This will be an important step towards providing wildlife managers in Rhode Island with the information they need. As of April 2026, the atlas of Rhode Island's bees will be complete. Unfortunately, the RI Pollinator Atlas grant will also close and DFW will no longer have a Pollinator Entomologist position or expertise in invertebrates and insects. This, in addition to loss of staff at other partner organizations in Rhode Island, leaves a large gap in expertise and capacity for future work at the state level. Also, as noted in Chapter 1, data collection even for the state's rarest species has been under resourced for decades and much of the Natural Heritage data is out-of-date.
Broadly, RIDEM's Division of Marine Fisheries (DMF) hopes to utilize SWG funding to address research needs and to better reach its ecosystem-based management goals. The population status and dynamics of most marine species are not well understood, and additional resources are required to reach research and conservation objectives, particularly in the face of a changing climate. Some key priorities include expanding the acoustic telemetry monitoring program to track the movement and seasonality of SGCN species; conducting population assessments; developing habitat suitability models; restoring and enhancing shellfish beds, submerged aquatic vegetation, and other key marine habitats; and gaining a better understanding of the population levels of invasive species and their effects on native species.

## EFFECTIVENESS OF CONSERVATION ACTIONS

The purpose of tracking effectiveness measures is to obtain the information needed to adaptively manage fish and wildlife species and habitats in the state. The effectiveness of RIWAP conservation actions will be measured using a set of standardized effectiveness measures that have been developed by AFWA and described in their 2011 Measuring the Effectiveness of State Wildlife Grants Final Report (AFWA 2011). In accordance with current limited capacity, effectiveness measures will be tracked for particular classes of conservation actions and reported and tracked as part of RIDEM's regular reporting to the USFWS via the Wildlife TRACS database. Comparisons of the values of these tracked measures over time will begin to indicate the degree of effectiveness of individual projects as well as broader conservation programs.
Implementation performance indicators measure overall plan success based on guiding principles for conservation actions . Once an action is undertaken, information on all indicators may be collected initially; however, it is anticipated that, with experience, a subset will be selected that best measures the effectiveness of the RIWAP as a whole. The evaluation of conservation actions will continue annually through project reporting in TRACS and Federal Aid reports, and as new information becomes available. Annual federal assistance reports will provide new information from SWG and other Federal Aid projects that will be entered into TRACS, the RIWAP database, and GIS spatial coverages to capture successes, changes in SGCN and key habitat status, and the effectiveness of actions. RIDEM staff and technical committees will assess the status of SGCN and key habitats as new data are entered annually or at the end of these projects.

## INTRODUCTION

The RIWAP recognizes the changing state of knowledge and is intended as a dynamic document.
As data gaps are filled, including via recent and ongoing monitoring discussed in Chapter 4, new ones will be identified. The resulting increases in scientific knowledge on which this RIWAP is based will keep the strategy as up to date as possible. Just as these plans inform RIWAP review and revision, relevant plans are targeted for the inclusion of RIWAP information and conservation actions into their revisions.
The RIWAP is a broad and flexible plan intended to include all the conservation actions needed to secure Rhode Island's full complement of natural communities and the life they support. It also makes clear that far more resources are needed than are presently on hand to accomplish all this work. As such, the adaptive management and other adjustments to implementation described above may or may not require interim changes to the RIWAP itself prior to the 2035 10-year update.

## RIWAP REVIEW SCHEDULE

While the evaluation of conservation actions will continue annually through Annual Project Reporting in TRACS and Federal Aid reports and as new information becomes available, the RIWAP document will be amended as needed and revised every ten years. A five-year review and evaluation schedule is the most common timeframe for existing management plans in Rhode Island, and Federal Assistance (Application for Federal Assistance-AFA) reporting is also consistent with those plans. This 10-year revision will build upon improvements to the RIWAP to date and incorporate all relevant annual, biannual, and 5-year review and evaluation information and updates so the 10-year revision will not be a total rewriting, but instead an incremental and iterative review and revision process. Annual federal assistance reports will provide new information from SWG and other Federal Aid projects which will be entered into TRACS, the RIWAP database, and RI GIS/RIDEM GIS spatial data to capture and update the annual accomplishments, changes in SGCN and key habitat status, and the effectiveness of actions. RIDEM staff and technical committees will assess the status of SGCN and key habitats as new data are available. This 10-year revision schedule will be long enough to be practical in terms of administrative loads but short enough to be responsive to changing conditions, monitoring and research results, and adaptive management revisions.

Partners were included in many Taxa Teams (Appendix I), where their expertise, research, and perspectives enhanced the initial technical updates to the plan, and in each major phase of the RIWAP process thereafter. ## SURVEYS

RIDEM implemented a series of surveys to better understand Rhode Islander's awareness of the RIWAP, how they engaged with the 2025 revision, and what informational materials or tools they wanted from the RIWAP. The first survey measured the public's awareness, asking questions like, were you aware of the RIWAP before taking this survey, how important is conservation to you, and what are the largest threats facing RI wildlife. This survey received 860 responses and showed that, while most Rhode Islanders (75%) were unaware of the RIWAP, 96% of respondents feel it is very important to conserve Rhode Island's fish and wildlife. This prompted the outreach team to focus on broader public participation and education about the RIWAP, resulting in community workshops to educate the public about the RIWAP before soliciting their feedback on the content of the plan.
The second survey intended to collect public feedback on the over RIWAP outreach process to understand if it was effective, what didn't work, and lessons for the future. Unfortunately, this survey only received 24 responses and therefore RIDEM was unable to draw meaningful conclusions. However, the discrepancy between interest in survey 1 and 2 might suggest that most folks who engage with the RIWAP do so indirectly, through the information and messaging it contains delivered through RIDEM staff and partners throughout the year in restoration updates, newsletters, outreach efforts, website content, family programs, newsletters, and social media. A final survey will be solicited following the RIWAP submission to USFWS to better understand desired and needed RIWAPrelated outreach (educational materials, public guides, printed materials, etc.).

This step was taken to ensure that there would be maximum RIWAP coordination, implementation and buy-in by partners for the next decade.
Coordination with all federal, state, local, and tribal partners will be carried forward on a regular, multi-level basis throughout the RIWAP implementation and future revisions. RIDEM staff will inform and update partners and interested communities at partners' regularly scheduled meetings. SGCN and key habitat information will be presented, and these partners will be asked to incorporate them into their plans and programs as they are revised. In this manner, there will be an ongoing dialogue and information feedback loop in which partners' plans reflect the RIWAP targets and the RIWAP incorporates partners' revisions and updated plans and information. This provides an effective mechanism for partner and community input into RIWAP review and implementation as well as participation at the staff, program, and organization level.
